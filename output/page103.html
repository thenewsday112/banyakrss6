United Airlines CEO Says Critics Don’t Understand His American Airlines Merger Vision. I Think We Do.

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scott Kirby thinks critics misunderstand his vision for merging United Airlines and American Airlines. I understand why he wants to do it. What I reject is his premise that a better airline would make up for the higher fares passengers would face once a major competitor disappears.

A United-American Merger Makes Sense For Scott Kirby. That Does Not Make It Good For Passengers.

One Mile At A Time brought a revealing Dallas Morning News interview with Kirby to my attention. The United Airlines CEO remains convinced that his proposed combination with American could have been a tremendous success, including for customers.

Before getting into that, a word about why Kirby appears so frequently on Live And Let’s Fly. Unlike Ed Bastian and Robert Isom, he puts himself out there constantly. Every few days, it seems, there is another interview or podcast with something worth discussing.

I don’t know whether this is laying the groundwork for a presidential campaign after he retires from United, but I am not going to ignore his statements simply because his competitors maintain a lower profile.

Kirby Thinks Critics Are Using The Wrong Lens

In the interview, Kirby argues that skepticism about regulatory approval viewed his proposal through a “traditional commodity lens.” He maintains that only he knew exactly what he intended to build.

“We were disappointed that it leaked and then I saw all of the feedback of what people said about how possible it was. All of the criticism about this would not get through regulatory muscle was looking at it through a traditional commodity lens, and I am the one person who knows what we were planning to do for 100% certainty because I’m the one that was going to do it.”

He also describes receiving encouragement from people at his children’s soccer games in Dallas who wanted him to buy American. I can certainly believe there are frustrated American customers who would welcome United taking over.

But enthusiasm for better service does not establish that eliminating American as an independent competitor would benefit the traveling public.

In case you missed it, there is no announced merger agreement. American rejected the proposal earlier this year, and Kirby acknowledged in his April statement that a “willing partner” was essential.

> Read More: Scott Kirby Says United’s American Airlines Merger Dream Is Dead

I Understand Exactly Why Kirby Wants American

From Kirby’s perspective, the strategic appeal is obvious.

American would bring a vast domestic network and a huge customer base. Combining that with United’s international reach would create an extraordinarily powerful airline, with more opportunities to sell connecting itineraries and persuade travelers to concentrate their spending in one loyalty program.

Kirby could extend United’s product strategy across American’s operation. I can imagine many American passengers appreciating better technology and a more consistent premium experience. This fits what Kirby told me in Denver when I asked about his merger ambitions. He wants enough scale to compete with the best Asian and Middle Eastern airlines on product as well as network.

That is an understandable corporate ambition. A larger airline could have more resources to invest, and some customers could enjoy more convenient connections.

But greater pricing power strikes me as the primary driver for this merger: Kirby has repeatedly said that he believes fares are too low. The ability to sell a better product and the ability to charge more because a competitor has disappeared can exist at the same time…in Kirby’s mind, he can only invest more if his margin his higher and a merger with AA would enable that to happen.

More Dots On A Map Do Not Replace Competition

Consider Chicago. Today, each airline has a reason to respond when the other offers a better fare or schedule. A merged company would decide what both networks should charge and how much capacity to offer…and we’d quickly see higher fortress hub pricing that Delta is able to extract from Atlanta,Detroit, and Minneapolis (or American is able to extract from Charlotte or Dallas or United itself from Houston).

It goes beyond nonstop routes. A passenger traveling between smaller cities may compare an American connection with a United connection. Putting both itineraries under one carrier removes the rivalry between the companies selling them.

Kirby says his vision centers on growth. Even if the combined airline initially retained every flight, which would be stupid, the competitive incentive would still change. And as we saw when Spirit went bust, higher (or lower) fares are not necessarily confined to the merged carrier. Other airlines would have less reason to discount aggressively if a major independent rival disappeared.

I don’t think anyone can put a credible percentage on the fare increases without a specific proposal and market analysis. But I would expect substantial upward pressure on prices in affected markets. Passengers would get a better app and more destinations within one program while paying considerably more to travel, which is not a tradeoff I would make.

That’s the underlying concern. Most travelers are trying to get somewhere at a reasonable price. A more impressive airline is of limited comfort if it prices people out of traveling.

Regulators Would Need More Than A Promise Of Better Service

The central merger standard under Section 7 of the Clayton Act concerns whether a transaction may substantially lessen competition. A compelling product presentation does not satisfy that test.

The JetBlue-Spirit case is instructive: the argument for creating a stronger competitor to larger airlines did not overcome the competitive harm the court found and that was a merger I thought should have passed regulatory scrutiny despite my personal opposition to it (for the sake of JetBlue’s deteriorating financial health).

Kirby has acknowledged that divestitures would be necessary. But selling some gates would not automatically recreate an independent nationwide airline with American’s ability to compete. Any remedy would need to preserve effective competition and I don’t think that is possible right now.

Opposition is also broader than Democrats. Republican Senator Mike Lee joined Elizabeth Warren in an April letter questioning a United-American merger and warning about its consequences for consumers.

A Friendly Administration Would Not End The Risk

If Democrats take control of one or both houses of Congress, I would expect more scrutiny of any attempt to advance this combination. Hearings and investigations alone could not derail the merger, but might create a powerful disincentive. Enforcement agencies and courts would remain central. But assuming political support today guarantees success years later is a bad assumption.

Senator Cory Booker has already introduced the CLEAN Mergers Act, proposing retrospective review and potential unwinding of major Trump-era transactions. In announcing it, Senator Chris Murphy explicitly threatened future breakups of deals Democrats regard as corrupt monopolies. That is just proposed legislation that could not overcome Trump’s veto at this point, but a broader political warning that might be enacted down the road.

Indeed, a Democratic administration taking office in January 2029 could revisit an allegedly anticompetitive transaction. As the Justice Department explains, closing a merger does not immunize it from a subsequent antitrust challenge. Unwinding an integrated airline would be difficult and contested, but the possibility is real.

I Am No Longer Against Every Airline Merger

Had you asked me a few years ago, I would have been flatly against any merger…I miss US Airways, Continental, and Northwest and the competition they brought. But times have changed.

I wanted Spirit and Frontier to combine because I saw the potential for a stronger nationwide budget competitor. I would also welcome a serious look at whether a combination involving Frontier, Allegiant, and Sun Country could create a more effective low-fare alternative. I also woudl love to see a JetBlue-Alaska merger if JetBlue could overcome its debt issues.

Those deals would still deserve scrutiny. Smaller does not automatically mean harmless, and preserving low-fare competition would be essential. But combining smaller carriers to challenge the largest airlines presents a different competitive proposition from allowing two mammoth network carriers to eliminate their rivalry.

The question is whether consolidation strengthens competition or removes it. In a United-American combination, I find the latter risk overwhelming, not matter what Kirby says, because he has also often repeated that he wants fares to rise.

CONCLUSION

I understand Kirby’s vision. He wants a larger network and the resources to build an airline that competes with the best in the world. I can see why American would be an attractive acquisition. I think we all understand him…

But I think he underestimates both the regulatory obstacles and the cost to passengers, even if he insists we are all wrong. Better service would be welcome. Eliminating a major competitor would give the combined airline less reason to offer that service at a competitive price.

More dots and lines on one map would make United more powerful, but I remain convinced that consumers would ultimately lose.

Dr. Becky Kennedy on self-trust, boundaries and letting kids struggle

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Dr. Becky Kennedy’s daughter once wrote home from summer camp asking for more bikinis, including one from a pricey brand her friends had. By their phone call a few days later, she didn’t care about any of it. For Kennedy, a clinical psychologist and the founder of Good Inside, that exchange says a lot about raising confident kids at a time when parents can fix almost any discomfort with a tap. She defines confidence as self-trust, built in the hard moments we’re most tempted to smooth over. In a candid conversation on The Motherly Podcast, Kennedy opens up about the two jobs every parent has, why “I just want my kid to be happy” falls short and how to support a struggling kid with one foot in and one foot out.

Meet the expert: Dr. Becky Kennedy is a clinical psychologist, the founder and CEO of Good Inside and the author of three New York Times bestsellers. Known to millions of parents simply as Dr. Becky, she teaches an approach built on two core parenting jobs, holding boundaries and validating feelings, and on the idea that kids grow confident by getting through hard moments with support. With Carter’s, she recently launched the Childhood Confidence Project, a free program for parents. A Duke graduate, she lives in New York City with her husband and their three kids.

Liz Tenety: Today we’re talking about raising confident kids, which is also about being confident as a parent. What makes you feel more confident as a mom?

Dr. Becky Kennedy: I don’t ever assume I have the right answer. This might sound weird. I’m not that attached to the right answer. I’m very attached to having a way to think through problems, and it makes me think about the difference in the question “What do I do? What am I gonna say?” versus “What’s really going on here? What’s my job?” Those are very different questions. I think confidence as a parent for me comes from having a repeatable way to think through hard moments. I think the thing that takes away from our confidence as parents is in every hard moment with our kid, we feel like we’re starting over. And I think that’s the thing that gives me confidence, is not knowing I can handle any situation well, but feeling like I can think through it.

Liz Tenety: You mentioned knowing what your job is. What’s the job description for a parent?

Dr. Becky Kennedy: I think a parent has two jobs, almost always. One job is to set boundaries. It’s the piece that we’re missing in modern-day parenting. We’ve over-corrected. Boundaries are decisions I make with my kid that almost inherently they don’t like today but are actually good for the type of person they need to become tomorrow. And boundaries are things we tell our kid we will do, and they require our kid to do nothing. We often think we’re setting a boundary when we’re actually making a request. “Please stop jumping on the couch. Don’t hit your brother.” Those are requests. Those are not boundaries. A boundary is an assertion of my very appropriate authority. The other part of our job is staying connected to our kids and validating their feelings, which doesn’t mean agreeing, which doesn’t mean letting their feelings dictate our behavior. But just seeing their feelings as real. Those two jobs answer, I think, kids’ questions that they’re always unconsciously asking. Am I safe and am I real?

Liz Tenety: Do you see yourself as having been a confident kid?

Dr. Becky Kennedy: I don’t think confidence is feeling good about yourself all the time. I think confidence is self-trust. I always tell parents it’s actually not feeling like the best. It’s trusting it’s okay to be you when you’re not the best, ironically. So was I the kind of kid going through childhood who trusted myself, who felt like I was okay being me even when something was really hard? No. I was a really perfectionistic kid. I was a kid who probably growing up had enough external success, good grades, A team in soccer, all that, that I could use external outcomes as a way of feeling good. We know it works sometimes in childhood, and then it catches up to you and crashes big time. When we think about kids who are so just oriented towards success, in a way, we’re setting them up to have a shrinking life. I want my kids to have a wide range of situations. I want them to say, “Yeah, I’m trying out for that team. Sure, I might not make it, but let’s see.” That’s confidence.

Liz Tenety: What do you think is unique about this moment for parents?

Dr. Becky Kennedy: This might sound funny. It has never been easier as a parent to make our kids’ life easier. And I think that is the biggest problem we have with confidence and resilience. You see someone have something, it’s a natural human urge to be like, “I need that right away.” We are doing such an insane disservice to our kids by pairing what happens to be the peak urge with the ability to get the thing in the moment. I saw this with my own daughter. She was away for a little bit in the summer. She wrote me a handwritten letter. “I don’t have enough bikinis. Could you send me two of the ones I left at home?” And then she said, “And a lot of my friends have this brand. Maybe it’s too expensive, but I would love one of those.” I knew I was talking to her in a couple days. At the end of the call, I said, “Hey, by the way, you haven’t brought up bikinis.” This is what she says: “Oh, yeah, I don’t need those. It’s fine.” I can’t even tell you what this did for me in my parenting. Just having time allowed her urge and her panic to subside that she didn’t even care. And because it’s never been easier to remove those things, it has never been harder to raise an actually capable, confident, resilient kid.

Liz Tenety: Generations of parents have said, “I just want my kid to be happy.” What do you make of that?

Dr. Becky Kennedy: People will say this in passing. We’re talking about our kids at a dinner and someone’s like, “I just want my kid to be happy.” And I’m sure my husband always looks at me like, “Just don’t. Please, just let this pass.” And sometimes I do, but sometimes I’m like, “No, we don’t.” I don’t think our job is to make our kids happy. I actually would say that if you’re operating even unconsciously as if that is your job, you’re setting your kid up not for happiness, but, if I say it as directly as I think it, you’re setting your kid up for a ton of anxiety and fragility. Because so much of capability in adulthood comes from the range of situations we learned to handle in childhood. Three of my best friends were in the same class in second grade, and I wasn’t. Now, do I think when my kid is 40, they’re gonna be thinking about who’s in their class? 0%. But it’s not about the class, it’s about the circuit. And you know what’s gonna happen when I’m 40? I’m gonna see a group of moms at lunch without me. I’m gonna see a group of people at work get promoted before me. We have to be able to handle jealousy and sadness and being left out for there to be room for happy.

Liz Tenety: My middle schooler just found a zero in his grades, and my urge was to say, “Don’t worry, we’ll go through it together.” Letting him struggle felt really bad.

Dr. Becky Kennedy: I just wanna zone in on that moment, your kid gets a zero, because again, we tend to think in extremes. No part of me thinks you should be in that car ride being like, “Sucks. Yep, zero sucks. Yep, figure it out.” There’s just a lot between that. And it’s interesting, the phrase, “I don’t want you to worry about that.” I think what we’re trying to say is, “I don’t want you to take this moment and feel like that’s just defined who you are.” But I do want you to worry a little. When our kid is in a hard situation, you don’t wanna leave them alone, and you don’t wanna fully jump in. I think you want one foot in and one foot out. One foot in would be saying, “Ugh, you weren’t expecting that. You didn’t want that to happen. Well, that’s a bummer on this Thursday car ride.” Whatever’s just the truth. And the one foot out is sometimes words, and it’s usually after some time, which is, “All right. This is recoverable. One number doesn’t change anything about you. You’re the same smart, awesome, responsible kid before the zero as you were after the zero.”

Liz Tenety: What do those confidence-building moments look like with a baby?

Dr. Becky Kennedy: Think about tummy time. Tummy time is one of the first times your baby is telling you, “Me no likey.” My job isn’t to remove all moments of discomfort for my baby. Now again, we go to the other extreme. “Oh, so you’re saying you just leave a baby crying?” No, I would never do that. But we’ve started to confuse, at all ages, giving comfort with making kids comfortable. They’re completely different things. My job is to provide comfort. My job is not to always make my kid comfortable. Okay, I’m gonna do tummy time. My baby might whine a little bit, cry a little bit. I’m gonna get next to my baby. “Oh, you’re right. This is hard. Oh, you’d rather be on your back. Oh, you know what? We’re gonna do 10 more seconds together, and I am right here with you. I know you can do it. You are doing it. Oh, you did it. I’m giving you a big hug. You did something so tricky.”

Liz Tenety: You give us these dream scripts, and none of us nail them. Even Dr. Becky doesn’t nail Dr. Becky.

Dr. Becky Kennedy: The scripts are helpful to talk about because ideas that are theoretical aren’t that usable. But where scripts are limiting is, I’m not a script person. I really mean that. I saw a comment, she goes, “Me and my husband, in any hard situation, look at each other and we say WWDBD.” What would Dr. Becky do? Now, the reason I love that is completely different than why I think people would think I love it. This actually has nothing to do with me. If you’re in a hard situation and you say, “What would Dr. Becky do?” That’s not my voice. I’m literally not there with you. That is your voice. Having a method, having a way of thinking through something. And that, to me, more than a perfect script, that is the most confidence-building thing as a parent.

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity. Listen to the full conversation on the Motherly Podcast.

How To Use Sharks To Forecast Hurricanes (And Swells)

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Sardines, sharks, and just about all sea-adjacent critters often get a little shifty ahead of a big storm. But while oceanographic vessels and satellites have traditionally offered most of our data on developing depressions, sharks can provide critical subsurface metrics, swimming through depths that satellite surface scans and research vessels miss.

Of course, gathering so much sea-surface data could have great implications for surf forecasting, too: The data could inform prediction models’ estimated size, power, and delivery windows of groundswells.

“This is the part of the water column that really drives hurricane intensity,” University of Delaware Marine Scientist Aaron Carlisle told Reuters. “A warmer mixed layer equals [a] stronger hurricane, as it holds the heat that feeds hurricanes.”

Related: Woman Tries to Pet Great White Shark. It Doesn’t Go Well. (Video)

Carlisle and his team at the University of Delaware School of Marine Science and Policy had to be highly selective in choosing their species. Most electronic wildlife tags are built for air-breathing cetaceans, seals, and turtles that break the surface constantly. Sharks, however, don’t breach in order to breathe. To make this work, the UD team focuses on deploying satellite-linked CTD (Conductivity, Temperature, and Depth) tags on pelagic sharks—smooth hammerheads, juvenile great whites, shortfin makos, and blue sharks—along the East Coast of the United States.

Having roving thermometers along the Eastern Seaboard makes for a much larger data set than any conceivable number of buoys or research vessels. The data allows scientists to track the Mid-Atlantic’s seasonal “cold pool,” a massive layer of bottom water that acts as a natural brake or accelerator for storm intensity. The tags also offer marine scientists a rare window into how apex predators behave when intense storms pass overhead—a data set of which the scientific community has a considerable dearth.

A better offshore understanding is vital for coastal mitigation. The tracking is especially crucial now that NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) retired its active updates for the Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters database due to budget constraints. While NCEI still hosts authoritative historical data spanning 1980 to 2024, new open-source initiatives are stepping up to fill the gaps.

So, better hurricane forecasting—and by extension, drastically more accurate groundswell and surf forecasting—courtesy of our own proverbial taxman? We’ll gladly take all they’re willing to share.

Phantom Blade Zero

Wesley LeBlanc

Phantom Blade Zero is one of the best action games I’ve ever played, and it’s certainly one of the most impressive to watch. Beijing-based developer S-Game uses China’s Wuxia brand of action (which was actually banned for some time during the 20th Century by the country’s government) to create a stylish, fluid, and hyper-engaging combat system. It will constantly leave you in awe that action combat can look this good. But looks aren’t everything. Fortunately, Phantom Blade Zero delivers on all fronts, with weapon mechanics so detailed that picking up a new blade can feel like unlocking a new character in a fighting game. The story won’t blow your socks off, but it’s certainly engaging and enticing enough to bring you through Phantom Blade Zero’s 40 or so hours of content. If you’re not interested in the “perfect ending,” which is the only satisfactory one in my opinion, you can complete the game in less than 30 hours. S-Game mixes high-octane combat with sprawling and rewarding exploration to create a must-play during this already busy season of games. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Phantom Blade Zero review, and if you are hopping in this weekend, be sure to check out my list of Phantom Blade Zero tips and tricks I wish I knew before starting it.

Halloween: The Game

Wesley LeBlanc

If you have any fond memories of IllFonic’s ill-fated Friday the 13th: The Game as I do, you simply must play the studio’s latest, Halloween: The Game. It is essentially Friday the 13th with Michael Myers instead of Jason, but with a decade of lessons learned from the team’s teeth carving in other horror properties and games. You’re either Michael or a citizen; the former wants to kill you and all the NPCs in various suburban maps, while the latter must rescue as many NPCs as possible while staying alive themselves. It’s that classic cat-and-mouse formula IllFonic is the master of, but with all the style, substance, and synths of John Carpenter’s Halloween masterpiece. Plus, Halloween: The Game features IllFonic’s first crack at a single-player campaign, and while it’s largely a tutorial for playing as Michael, if you’re a Halloween mega-fan like me, it’s worth checking out if only to take part in some of the film’s most iconic kills. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Halloween: The Game review here, and if you’re hopping in this weekend, there’s a new map, additional Halloween-themed cosmetics to unlock, and more waiting for you.

Overwatch Halloween Event

Kyle Hilliard

Overwatch is in the midst of its Halloween event, which have always historically been pretty interesting. This year it's Mystery Madness: Graveyard Games. In it, you begin by picking one of three characters in a random role. And then, as the game progresses, you choose perks that upgrade, alter, or completely change what your character can do. It can get really chaotic where by round three characters like Orisa might be three times her normal size or Torbjorn might have placed a giant turret on Mercy's making her a flying death machine. There are also Halloween skins, as well.

Tom The Postgirl

Charles Harte

Tom the Postgirl is an odd little adventure that I'd recommend to fans of Untitled Goose Game. You play as Tom, a creepy girl who's tasked with delivering packages to the people of her wacky town, which is depicted with storybook-style illustrations and a color palette limited to black, white, and red. On the surface, you're delivering packages, but the game's real story is based on Tom's tendency to peep where she shouldn't, peering into townsfolks' windows to watch their odd lives. On house is full of kids who live like dogs; another has a couple with a bizarre obsession with tickling. You never know what each window might reveal, and I quickly sympathized with (and enabled) Tom's compulsion. You also have the capacity to cause quite a bit of trouble by stomping on flowers, haphazardly snipping strings and cords, or even stealing packages and using them for your own mischievous purposes. It's a pretty short experience, completable in just a few hours, making it a nice palate cleanser between the massive releases at the end of the year.

Virtue And A Sledgehammer

Marcus Stewart

This Devolver Digital-published game has a simple but head-turning premise. You’re a profoundly angry woman who returns to her small hometown armed with nothing but a sledgehammer, which she uses to smash everything in sight. The town is also inhabited by robots possessing human consciousnesses. Swinging the hammer can either punch holes through walls or, with enough force, reduce entire buildings to rubble. But why is she doing this? Why is she so mad? A big part of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s allure is its fascinating and well-written tale about the protagonist’s strained relationships with her sister and mother. Learning more about their lives requires you to keep smashing, but the more you learn, the more you begin to question whether smashing everything is actually a good idea.

Titanium Court On Switch 2

Marcus Stewart

This bizarre indie blends fun match-3 puzzle-solving with tower defense gameplay wrapped around a funny but surreal story. The match-3 gameplay involves sliding tiles to match geographic elements like castles, trees, and rivers to collect resources that feed into the tower defense portion. Depending on how the board looks when the puzzle portion ends, you send troops (sustained by the resources you collected) to attack/defend against rival castles and gather more items; board positioning determines how well this can go. The two mechanics feed into each other to create a fun loop that grows in complexity as new mechanics are introduced. Did I mention the game is also very weird? The silly tale centers on a medieval court designating you the chosen savior to rescue their lost queen (and many other tasks), and the dumb tone adds personality to a sometimes dry genre. Titanium Court charmed and impressed us when it launched on PC earlier this year, and now console and mobile players get their chance to hold court.

Sword Of The Sea On Switch 2

Kyle Hilliard

Sword of the Sea is a beautiful, 2025 game about surfing through the sand atop a floating sword. It recalls Journey, the emotional 2012 PlayStation game, which makes sense as it comes from the artists and developers that worked on that game, developer Giant Squid. Giant Squid also made 2016's Abzû and 2020's The Pathless. It's a talented team and Sword of the Sea maintains that reputation. And now you can play it on Switch 2. You can read my full review of the game (though this review pertains to the PlayStation 5 version) right here.

Cities: Skylines On Switch 2

Brian Shea

Cities: Skylines arrived on PC more than a decade ago, but is still revered as one of the greatest modern city-builders. Minecraft On Switch 2

Charles Harte

Minecraft has held its dominant spot in pop culture for so long that it's easy to forget a simple fact about it: the game is awesome. This week, it's coming to Switch 2, giving potential miners another platform to explore procedurally-generated caves in. You've certainly heard of it, and you've probably played it, but I still feel it often doesn't get the flowers it deserves in high-level critical discussions about games, despite its regular cadence of major updates. The opening gameplay loop is so satisfying that for many of my peers, whether they regularly play video games or not, it's only a matter of time before they boot up a new world again. While you can never play the game for the first time again, if you haven't checked it out in a couple years, you might be surprised by the amount of new blocks, materials, and mobs they've added in recent updates. And if you've never played before, the Switch 2 launch is as good a time as any to see what all the fuss is about.

Tabletop Pick – Arkham Horror: The Card Game

Matt Miller

The card game incarnation of Arkham Horror remains one of the best narrative-driven and horror-themed tabletop games around, and it's an easy recommendation if you're looking for something engaging to dive into on Halloween night. Rooted in the monsters and mysteries of the Cthulhu Mythos, this living card game is built on major core sets and expansions. But unlike a TCG, each new installment has the same cards included, fueling the cooperative adventures you'll undertake together with your fellow players. Last year Arkham Horror: The Card Game did something of a soft relaunch for a chapter 2 of its narrative, and that new core set is an ideal place to jump on  play. Players each control an individual investigator as they explore arcane and weird mysteries, confront monsters, and unfold plots of cultists, ancient entities, and forbidden lore. For fans of cosmic horror storytelling, it's an easy pick.
