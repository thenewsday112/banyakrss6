Court to consider federal inmate’s ability to use habeas to accelerate transfer to a halfway house or home confinement

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

In Maxwell v. Dinis, to be argued on Nov. 2, the Supreme Court will address a federal inmate’s ability to use a habeas statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2241, to contest the Federal Bureau of Prisons’ refusal to accelerate his transfer to a halfway house or home confinement.

By way of background, when the BOP designates a federal inmate spend time in a halfway house or in home confinement, that portion of the sentence is still considered part of the inmate’s term of imprisonment under federal law and, as such, is overseen by the BOP. That said, such a portion of a sentence is formally distinct from, for example, a term of federal supervised release, which begins after the term of imprisonment is completed and is overseen by a probation officer and a federal district court.

The special character of how a federal inmate serves a sentence in a halfway house or home confinement gives rise to the procedural question that the court is considering in Maxwell.

What claims Maxwell is trying to pursue

In 2007, William Maxwell conspired with members of the mob-related organized crime group, La Costa Nostra, to take over a Texas-based mortgage-loan company, FirstPlus Financial Group, Inc., and drain it of its assets. Specifically, Maxwell and others threatened the CEO of FirstPlus by saying that they would accuse him of bribery, money laundering, and other offenses, and took more than $14 million from the company, leaving it with less than $2,000 in its accounts. Convicted at trial in 2015 of numerous offenses, Maxwell was sentenced to 20 years in prison.

Maxwell did not have infractions while in prison, was moved to a low-security facility, and sought to accelerate his transfer to a halfway house or home confinement by participating in programs established by the First Step Act of 2018 under 18 U.S.C. § 3624(g)(1) and (2). Maxwell argued that the BOP failed to count some of these activities and that he was entitled to between 42 and 48 months of time credit that he did not receive. (Even under the BOP’s stricter calculations, according to the government Maxwell is eligible to be transferred to a halfway house on Jan. 22, 2027.)

The district court rejected Maxwell’s habeas petition, reasoning that he had failed to exhaust his administrative remedies. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit affirmed on the alternative ground that the circuit had adopted a bright-line rule that habeas relief under Section 2241 is unavailable unless the inmate’s claim would automatically entitle the inmate to accelerated release. According to the circuit court, since neither Maxwell’s requested relief of being transferred to a halfway house or home confinement “would entitle him to accelerated release,” he could not seek habeas relief.

Some legal background

What makes Maxwell’s situation tricky is that he is not attacking the validity of his conviction or sentence, which is what is contemplated by the federal habeas statute itself. And under the court’s precedent, it is at best unclear that a federal inmate can challenge conditions of his confinement, such as an inmate’s placement in solitary confinement or in a maximum-security facility. When state inmates have brought such claims about the conditions of confinement, the court has said that they must bring those claims in federal court under a civil rights statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1983, not via habeas.

But at least two initial points seem to weigh in Maxwell’s favor. First, in the 2023 case of Jones v. Hendrix, the court said that habeas remains available as a procedural mechanism for a federal prisoner to “challenge ‘the legality of his detention’ without attacking the validity of his sentence.” What Maxwell is trying to do (contest the BOP’s refusal to accelerate his transfer to a halfway house or home confinement) can plausibly be described as challenging the legality of his detention without challenging the lawfulness of his sentence. Second, and perhaps more importantly, in 1973, the court held in Preiser v. Rodriguez that a state prisoner must use a habeas petition – and not Section 1983 – to challenge the New York Department of Correctional Services’ denial of good-conduct time credits.

The arguments

Maxwell begins his argument for why Section 2241 is available to him by relying on language tracing back to a concurring opinion by Justice Antonin Scalia. Specifically, in Skinner v. Switzer, the court stated that habeas relief is available for claims seeking to “reduce the prisoner’s level of custody” (quoting Scalia’s concurrence in Wilkinson v. Dotson). Maxwell also draws upon the language in Jones quoted above and the case of Preiser to argue that the situation in Preiser is a close analog to his circumstances and the court’s language in Jones leaves further room for challenges to the legality of an inmate’s detention.

Maxwell also points out that if habeas relief is unavailable, he has no other alternatives. The court has severely limited plaintiffs’ abilities to bring a civil-rights suit under Bivens v. Six Unknown Federal Narcotics Agents, for example, and a Section 1983 claim is inapplicable to suits against federal officials. The Administrative Procedures Act is also likely inapplicable because of a statute that limits review of the bureau of prisons’ decisions, as is injunctive relief through a suit in equity.

Notably, the government agrees with Maxwell that alternatives to habeas are unavailable. The government also declines to defend the lower court’s rule that habeas relief is limited to claims that automatically entitle the inmate to accelerated release. But the government endorses a narrower approach to when habeas relief is available than Maxwell. According to it, an inmate may seek habeas relief when arguing that his imprisonment has been unlawfully prolonged, a standard that does not require the inmate to show an entitlement to a sentence reduction. At the same time, the government’s standard would not extend to just any challenge to the “level of custody,” such as allowing an inmate’s classification in a maximum-security prison to be cognizable in habeas.

As for Maxwell, the government contends that his claim should be rejected for failure to exhaust all of his administrative remedies, the ground on which the district court dismissed the claim. On the “merits,” although the government says that Maxwell’s claim is of a type that may be brought in habeas, the government argues that, by statute, Congress has barred courts from reviewing the bureau of prisons’ classification of where an inmate “is placed during his term of imprisonment.” In the government’s view, this statute is fatal to Maxwell’s position.

Because the government declined to defend the court of appeals’ rule, the Supreme Court appointed a “friend of the court,” or amicus. The amicus contends that Maxwell’s and the government’s positions both create line-drawing problems. As to Maxwell’s position, the amicus argues that, given the wide continuum of conditions of incarceration that inmates face within the BOP’s system, determining whether a particular change in custody is meaningfully less restrictive would be difficult as a practical matter and require a fact-intensive, prisoner- and facility specific inquiry. As to the government’s position, the amicus argues that it has not offered any recognizable standard for deciding when an inmate’s imprisonment has been unlawfully prolonged.

In an effort to avoid those line-drawing problems, the amicus argues that habeas relief should instead be available in three relevant circumstances: (1) if the inmate’s claim implies that his physical detention must be shortened or terminated; (2) he is entitled to earlier release on bail or parole; or (3) the sentencing court ordered him to serve his sentence in a place it lacked jurisdiction to send him.

It is unclear whether the justices will find the amicus’ proposed circumstances appealing. But the amicus’s sustained attacks on the difficulties in applying Maxwell’s and the government’s position may well attract their interest.

Even for Desperate Restaurants, Fake Reviews Just Aren&#8217;t Worth It

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Yelp was first launched in 2004, it flopped. The company’s initial model of an email-based referral service failed to take off until its creators realized users were taking the platform into their own hands; in lieu of sending one another recommendations via email chain, they began using a “hidden” link at the bottom of the startup’s website to write unsolicited reviews of everything from doctors to restaurants. Yelp 2.0 launched in 2005, and gave those reviews a more visible place to live online — one that emboldened users to feel more authentic stewardship, by way of direct authorship, over hyping up the businesses they enjoyed (or didn’t). The startup bought a whopping 20-million-business database, and amplified a world of reviews outside of the restaurant- critic echo chamber. It was a fresh democratization of information, which was itself one of the more optimistic legacies of the Y2K internet. 

It’s been over 20 years since Yelp was founded. At its best, the platform still feels like a place where the voices of hungry, curious diners can share their personal experiences and offer recommendations and tips; at its worst, it feels like a bloated repository for reviews filled with misdirected anger, unreasonable expectations, or else completely fake customer reviews. Initially, unsubstantiated and unreliable reviews were more often written by a person — either someone from the business itself, or paid by the business; now, a fake review can be penned by a paid influencer, a detailed generative AI prompt, or purchased from third-party agencies that will produce fake bot reviews in bulk.

Fake reviews were perhaps inevitable. Bot-driven comments became ubiquitous during an overripe moment in the untamed internet’s lifespan. But how does a restaurant shamed for gamifying its reviews — even if it was, presumably, out of desperation in a tough economic landscape — regain customers’ broken trust? 

… The offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp.

In 2026’s AI-addled digital ecosystem, researchers are starting to understand even more about how paid-for, planted reviews impact the restaurants behind them, and whether trying to gamify platforms to achieve high ratings is worth it at all for businesses. A recently published working paper on the consequences of restaurants flagged by Yelp for fake (planted, incentivized) reviews found that since 2023, more than 4,900 businesses have been flagged with Yelp’s “Consumer Alert” banner, which exists on the penalized business’ page for a 90-day period and lets Yelp users know that the business has been caught peddling fake reviews. In 2020, the fast-casual chain Burgerim was found guilty of planting fake reviews, and received over 200 digital warning banners across its various locations’ Yelp pages.

The impacts of that digital pillory extend far beyond its 90-day duration. Yi Cao is an assistant professor of accounting at George Mason University, and the co-author of the paper; almost immediately, Cao noticed the offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp. Cao’s team cross-analyzed the business’ performance on Yelp with real-life foot traffic reports (from global location data firm SafeGraph) and credit card transaction data (courtesy of Consumer Edge), and discovered that most of the penalized restaurants struggled to recover to pre-alert traffic even after a year and a half. The stink of chicanery lingered. 

While Cao says his team doesn’t know every signal that Yelp uses to identify fake reviews, they do know that it relies “substantially on reports from consumers and business owners,” and that “they also have an algorithm running in the background to screen reviews for AI-generated language — which is a sign of possible review inflation.” At this point, the cost of planting a good review is only getting steeper as the technology capable of detecting it improves. “This is a classical theory of a trade-off between the benefits of manipulating reputation and the risks of getting caught,” Cao explains, “When the risk is relatively low, the business owners decide the incremental benefits are worth the risk.”

Yelp’s real restaurant reviews don’t necessarily need to be etched in stone, but as AI-penned reviews become less detectable and more sophisticated, what diners want now, more than ever, is a human touch and a sense of authenticity.

Blocking Data Centers at Home Could Put Americans’ Data at Risk Abroad

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If artificial intelligence (AI) companies are boxed out of every jurisdiction in the United States, they may need to locate their infrastructure offshore instead. That may resolve not-in-my-backyard concerns and prevent utility rates from increasing—at least from data centers—but it presents other disadvantages that AI’s opponents are likely overlooking.

The most obvious is the economic loss caused by pushing out a booming industry. Another is losing autonomy over Americans’ data.

Why Offshore Data Centers Put Americans’ Data at Risk

Consider what happens to U.S. data that relies on infrastructure in countries that do not share our value of freedom of speech. Our data could be subject to the same censorship laws of our host nation.

Governments in countries in the Middle East control internet infrastructure and restrict connectivity by blocking encrypted services like WhatsApp, FaceTime, and Facebook Messenger. These governments also censor content that is anti-Islamic, critical of those governments, obscene, and offensive.

The region also faces political instability that could affect data center performance and reliability. Amazon Web Services recently announced that customer data stored in data centers in Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates was unrecoverable after drone attacks by Iran in March and April.

In another scenario, a host nation may place tariffs on data transmitted back to the United States or use our data as a bargaining chip. In March, the World Trade Organization’s multilateral moratorium on customs duties for electronic transmissions lapsed. The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement also prohibits tariffs on data transfers, but the future of this agreement is uncertain due to its ongoing review process and the recently renewed trade war between the United States and Canada.

Or say AI developers go to any of the European, South American, or East Asian countries that favor carbon emissions taxes or high fuel-use taxes. Like tariffs, taxes would be costs passed down to U.S. consumers.

These are some of the risks the United States faces if enough jurisdictions do not allow data center development domestically. Outside its boundaries, the United States loses control.

How AI Already Shapes Americans’ Daily Lives

Yet disillusionment with AI chatbots and disdain toward AI-generated content are growing. These objections are reasonable. People value human connection, critical thinking, and human multimedia rather than life-like imitations.

But rejection of AI is not as simple as abstaining from consumer-facing, general-purpose AI chatbots or objecting to AI-generated media. AI is embedded across systems in society, and regular people encounter uses of AI more than they realize.

For example, facial recognition technology or Face ID that allows users to unlock their phones, sign into password-protected accounts, and verify their age for age-restricted platforms with a quick scan of their face is enabled by AI software.

Prospective homeowners engage with home lending advisors who use AI-powered software to verify income and financial records and extract data from applications and other documents. AI can improve objectivity and efficiency of application reviews. Banks and credit-card companies use AI to analyze patterns and deviations in transactions for improved fraud detection.

Many physicians use AI to record and summarize clinical encounters. Modern medicine involves administrative tasks to maintain a medical history for patients, which helps inform patients and their providers. But these tasks are time-consuming and take away time providers can spend with patients. AI frees up some of providers’ time spent on administration.

Additionally, more physicians use the AI-powered medical search engine OpenEvidence to assist with complex diagnoses and research the latest treatment options. Modern medicine is advancing constantly; it is challenging even for specialists to remain informed of every new clinical trial, technique, or other development in their field. Unlike general-purpose AI tools, the platform complies with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and is trained not on the open internet but on peer-reviewed medical literature, which helps ensure its output is accurate and relevant. The tool is only made available to health care professionals with verified credentials.

Drivers relying on GPS navigation obtain real-time traffic information, alerts, and faster routes due to AI. AI analyzes historical and live traffic conditions to estimate arrival times, predict slowdowns, and determine routes.

So whether or not you embrace AI, you likely unknowingly use it or you are on the receiving end of its use. And each of these examples involves processing and collecting sensitive data that should be safeguarded.

Why the United States Needs Data Centers at Home

U.S. AI data sovereignty means Americans’ data captured by AI systems is processed and stored in the United States. It also means it is governed and protected by U.S. laws and regulations and not subject to the whims of foreign governments. Maintaining data sovereignty requires sufficient data infrastructure.

>>> What’s Really Behind the Call to Regulate AI

This is why it matters that we build AI data centers in the United States. Wishing for AI’s extinction will not yield the desired results, and neither will widespread moratoriums on data center developments.

But in this current environment where data centers are politically unpopular, the future of data sovereignty is at stake.

Ratepayers are right to be concerned with how potential data center developments may affect their utility bills and access to necessary resources. Homeowners and buyers are right to be concerned with how it impacts homeownership and quality of life.

AI companies and data center developers must be honest, transparent, and fair—generous, even—to escape their villain trajectory. State and local leaders have an opportunity to respond to their constituents’ concerns and make favorable deals—ones that raise revenue for their jurisdictions and ensure that ratepayers are not suffering. Moratoriums on development and excessive demands stall, if not kill, negotiations.

If data center developers have nowhere to go domestically, they will have to go abroad. It could have similar consequences on the U.S. economy as offshoring in other industries, and it would be a shame after all the efforts to reshore manufacturing and other supply chains. Most certainly, it would relinquish data sovereignty.

Two trends are converging at an opportune time for new-build homeowners: the revival of front porches in home design and the hottest Halloween decorating trends that are centered, quite literally, on the front porch.

In new-home design, front porches are making a comeback alongside the growth of walkable neighborhoods and homeowners’ desire for community connection. Also, homes and yards are getting smaller, so homeowners are relying more heavily on outdoor spaces, like the front porch, to extend living space without increasing square footage.

According to the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB), porches rank as the most popular outdoor feature on new homes.

Meanwhile, Halloween is big business, and homeowners are getting serious about decorating. Over a third of consumers say Halloween is their favorite holiday, according to the National Retail Federation’s annual Halloween consumer shopping survey. They report that consumer spending on Halloween is expected to reach $13.5 billion in 2026.

Halloween decor used to be somewhat limited to front door wreaths, a jack-o’-lantern or two, or for more ambitious homeowners who had the space, lawn decorations and even some special effects. But the availability, square footage, and design of today’s front porch are offering new opportunities for homeowners who are Halloween fans too.

Sheltered from the elements, the front porch has enough space for decorative vignettes, wide front steps that encourage traffic flow, room to gather with neighbors and trick-or-treaters, and electrical outlets that make lighting and special effects easy.

Want to style your porch, but don’t have time to scour your local pumpkin patch, or are unsure of how to curate the bespoke pumpkin-inspired designs that are everywhere on social media?

Style support is available, very possibly in your neighborhood. The growing popularity of front porch decor has given rise to a niche industry: pumpkin concierges, a reportedly in-demand service in several regions of the country.

For a fee, pumpkin concierges work with local farmers to source pumpkins, then arrange elaborate, coordinated displays with seasonal greenery and other decorative accents, and some companies even return to remove everything at the end of the season.

The growing popularity of these services underscores how much attention homeowners are paying to their front porches, particularly during the holidays, and how intricate some of the seasonal designs are becoming, not only for the pros, but for DIY homeowners too.

Want to conjure Halloween magic on your front porch this holiday? Here are the spooky and stylish trends to watch.

​Layered, Luxe Pumpkins

Today’s front porches are intended to be additional living space, so homeowners are embracing many of the same design elements outside that they are inside, including layering materials and colors cohesively.

Rather than relying on a traditional orange-and-black color scheme, homeowners are using harvest-inspired curated palettes with sage, marigolds, and creams, or plums, deeper greens, and muted oranges for Halloween style.

Instead of one-off pieces of Halloween decor, Halloween front porches are being layered with textiles and accents that complement the existing porch furniture, like themed doormats, patterned pillows and throws, and seasonal candles.

Witchcore

Although Witchcore, a micro-aesthetic inspired by witchcraft, folklore, mysticism, and nature, isn’t specific to Halloween, several elements of this look are perfect for the holiday and for your front porch.

Assemble decor using twisted branches, wildflowers, and leaves, along with weathered and slightly spooky accents, like vintage books, lanterns, and apothecary bottles, that may or may not contain magic potions. Broomsticks, black cats, ravens, and cauldrons are nice Witchcore-inspired touches too.

Haunted Gardens

Moody, atmospheric haunted gardens are taking over front porches this Halloween, drawing on Gothic and Victorian influences for a hauntingly romantic look.

Think oversized planters with dark, spiky foliage, weathered urns, brass, dried flowers, and twisty, “creeping” vines. Cluster pumpkins of various sizes, and strategically place cobwebs for a hauntingly romantic look. Consider uplighting to create shadows, or weave twinkle lights through the porch, for an ethereal feel.

Old-School Halloween Theater

Design in general is leaning more heavily into the experiential, and Halloween, for its spine-tingling fun and jump scares, aligns perfectly with decor curated to assemble an emotional response, fun or fright, depending on your spooky goals.

​Consider your front porch a mini-stage for a theatrical, immersive trick-or-treat experience, with a defined, enclosed space for a backdrop, as well as easy access to electrical outlets for special effects.

Popular are fog machines, flickering lights, motion-activated skeletons, creepy soundtracks, and animatronic figures that spring to life as trick-or-treaters approach, if they dare. Make use of architectural elements, like porch columns, to enhance the decor, with wrapped cobwebs or tattered materials.

The latest move in the bond market may look like a story for Wall Street, but its consequences extend directly to the housing industry. Long-term Treasury yields have risen sharply as investors demand greater compensation to hold government debt, reinforcing the possibility that elevated borrowing costs could remain part of the economic landscape.

Several forces are pushing yields higher. Inflation remains persistent while the economy continues to show resilience, raising questions about how quickly price pressures will normalize. At the same time, investors are paying closer attention to federal deficits and the growing supply of Treasury debt.

Artificial intelligence is adding another dimension. Massive investment in data centers and related infrastructure could support economic growth while also keeping inflationary pressures firmer than they otherwise would be. That expectation can feed into higher long-term bond yields.

For housing, the most immediate concern is mortgage rates.

Higher Treasury yields generally put upward pressure on mortgage rates, creating another obstacle for prospective buyers already struggling with affordability. There is some relief from the mortgage spread, or the difference between the 10-year Treasury yield and the 30-year fixed mortgage rate. That spread, which historically averages about 1.7 percentage points, widened beyond 3 percentage points during the pandemic but has since narrowed to roughly 2.3 points. If the spread were still above 3 percentage points, mortgage rates would be closer to 8.3% rather than 7.6%.

For businesses, elevated rates translate into higher borrowing costs and interest expenses at a time when operating costs are already challenging. Consumer confidence could become even more sensitive as households react to negative economic headlines.
