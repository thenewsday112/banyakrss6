VoiceStudio คืออะไร

โปรแกรมเดียวที่รวมงานเสียงไว้ในเครื่อง

debpalash/VoiceStudio เป็นสตูดิโอเสียงในเครื่องที่รวมโคลนเสียง พากย์วิดีโอ ออกแบบเสียง ถอดความ และทำหนังสือเสียงไว้ในโปรแกรมเดียว [2] [3] ค่าเริ่มต้นคือประมวลผลในเครื่อง ไม่ต้องส่งไฟล์เสียงขึ้นคลาวด์

แต่ต้องเข้าใจให้ตรง เอกสารแบรนด์ของรีโปเตือนนักพัฒนาเองว่า ห้ามอ้างเรื่องความเป็นส่วนตัวแบบเบ็ดเสร็จ เพราะฟีเจอร์ที่ต่อเครือข่ายมีจริง ทั้งการแปลซับด้วย LLM ผ่านคลาวด์ การเก็บสถิติแบบขอความยินยอม และการดาวน์โหลดโมเดล ทั้งหมดเป็นแบบเลือกเปิดเอง [9]

คำว่า "สองคน" จริงแค่ครึ่ง

แต่รายชื่อ contributor บน GitHub มีเกินร้อยคน โดยสองคนแรกมี commit 4,594 และ 912 ครั้ง ส่วนที่เหลือกระจายกันไป [6] แปลว่าแกนหลักคือสองคน แต่ผลงานที่เห็นไม่ใช่ฝีมือสองคนล้วน

ห้าข้อที่ทวีตบอก และผมตรวจแล้ว

หนึ่ง โคลนเสียงจากคลิปเดียว README เขียนว่าใช้ไฟล์เสียงอ้างอิงที่สะอาดแปลงเป็นคำพูดด้วยเสียงคุ้นหู [3]

มีรายละเอียดที่ทวีตไม่บอก คือต้องมีข้อความถอดเสียงของคลิปนั้นด้วย ถ้าไม่มี โปรแกรมจะให้ Whisper ถอดให้ ซึ่งกินเวลาเพิ่ม และในรุ่นก่อน v0.3.15 ยังถอดซ้ำทุกครั้งที่สร้างเสียง [4]

สอง พากย์วิดีโอ 646 ภาษา ตัวเลขนี้มาจากหน้า README ที่เขียนว่าโคลน ออกแบบเสียง พากย์ ถอดความ และทำหนังสือเสียงได้ใน 646 ภาษา [2] [3]

ฐานเบื้องหลังคือโมเดล OmniVoice ของ k2-fsa ซึ่งหน้าโมเดลระบุ 646 ภาษา ตรงกับตัวเลขใน README [7] ตารางภาษาในรีโปก็มี 646 แถว รวมข้อมูลฝึกราว 581,000 ชั่วโมง [10]

สาม ออกแบบเสียงด้วยคำอธิบาย มีจริงตาม README หัวข้อ voice design คือพิมพ์คำบรรยายเสียงแล้วให้ระบบสร้าง [3]

สี่ ถอดความและทำหนังสือเสียง มีจริงในหัวข้อ dictate, transcribe และ narrate [3]

ห้า ใช้ได้บน Mac, Windows, Linux, Android, iPhone จริง แต่ต้องอ่านสถานะให้ดี [3]

macOS (Apple Silicon) เสถียร Windows 10/11 x64 เสถียร Linux x64 เสถียร Docker เสถียร Android 10+ Preview iPhone / iPad iOS 17+ Preview (ติดตั้งแบบ sideload) Windows on ARM ทดลอง Intel Mac จำกัด (UI อย่างเดียว ต่อ backend)

คำว่า Preview สำคัญ เพราะแปลว่ายังไม่ผ่านการทดสอบเท่ารุ่นเดสก์ท็อป และ iOS ต้องติดตั้งผ่านไฟล์ ipa ไม่ใช่โหลดจาก App Store [3]

เรื่อง "ไม่ต้องใช้ GPU" ที่ต้องเข้าใจให้ถูก

ทวีตเขียนว่าไม่ต้องใช้การ์ดจอแรง ซึ่งเอกสารยืนยันว่าใช้ซีพียูได้จริง

แต่ประโยคที่ตามมาคือ ช้ากว่าหลายเท่า [4] ไม่ใช่ช้าเล็กน้อย

เอกสารยังเตือนอีกสองเรื่องที่คนเพิ่งเริ่มมักตกใจ

  • การสร้างเสียงครั้งแรกหลังเปิดโปรแกรมจะช้าที่สุด เพราะต้องโหลดโมเดลเข้า RAM ราว 8 วินาที ต้องวัดความเร็วตั้งแต่ครั้งที่สองเป็นต้นไป [4]
  • ถ้าเครื่อง RAM 16 GB แล้วเปิดเบราว์เซอร์หลายสิบแท็บพร้อมกัน ระบบอาจสลับหน่วยความจำจนโมเดลหลุด ต้องปิดแท็บหรือสั่ง unload โมเดล [4]

กับดักที่ทวีตไม่พูดถึง

สี่ข้อที่ต้องรู้ก่อนโหลด

หนึ่ง สัญญาอนุญาตของตัวโปรแกรมกับของโมเดลคนละชั้น ตัวโปรแกรมเป็น AGPL-3.0 ซึ่งใช้เชิงพาณิชย์ได้ตามเงื่อนไข แต่โมเดลที่ใช้สร้างเสียงมีลิขสิทธิ์ของตัวเอง [5]

เอกสารลิขสิทธิ์ของรีโปเขียนตรง ๆ ว่ามีอย่างน้อยสี่รายการที่เป็นเงื่อนไขแบบไม่ใช่เชิงพาณิชย์ ได้แก่ OmniVoice, OmniVoice-GGUF, NLLB และ Llama-OuteTTS และหลายรายการยังไม่ผ่านการตรวจสอบ [5]

แปลว่าถ้าคุณจะเอาไปใช้ในงานที่ได้เงิน ต้องเปิดเอกสารนี้ตรวจทีละโมเดลก่อน ไม่ใช่เชื่อคำว่า "ฟรี" ในทวีต

สอง AGPL มีผลกับคนที่ให้บริการผ่านเครือข่าย ถ้าคุณเอาโปรแกรมนี้ไปทำเป็นบริการให้คนอื่นใช้ผ่านเว็บ คุณมีหน้าที่ต้องเปิดโค้ดที่แก้ให้ผู้ใช้ด้วย ไม่ใช่แค่ใช้ฟรีเงียบ ๆ [2]

สาม คุณภาพเทียบ ElevenLabs ยังไม่มีงานวิจัยอิสระ ทวีตเขียนว่า ElevenLabs "จบแล้ว" แต่ผมค้นไม่พบการทดสอบเปรียบเทียบที่เป็นกลางว่า VoiceStudio ให้คุณภาพใกล้เคียงหรือดีกว่าในทุกภาษา

ที่พบคือรีวิวจากผู้ใช้และวิดีโอสาธิต ซึ่งบอกได้แค่ว่าใช้ได้ ไม่ได้บอกว่าแทนกันได้ทุกกรณี

สี่ การโคลนเสียงมีข้อกฎหมาย README เองเตือนให้โคลนเฉพาะเสียงที่ได้รับอนุญาตจากเจ้าของ [3] เรื่องนี้สำคัญกว่าประเด็นราคามาก เพราะเสียงเป็นข้อมูลส่วนบุคคล

ใครควรลอง ใครยังไม่ต้อง

ตัดสินจากงานที่คุณทำ

ควรลองถ้า คุณทำงานเสียงบ่อย ต้องการเก็บไฟล์ไว้ในเครื่องเพราะข้อมูลอ่อนไหว หรืออยากทดลองทำหนังสือเสียงและพากย์วิดีโอโดยไม่ผูกค่าสมัครรายเดือน

ยังไม่ต้องถ้า คุณต้องการคุณภาพระดับโฆษณาที่ผ่านการรับรอง หรือต้องใช้เชิงพาณิชย์เต็มรูปแบบ เพราะยังต้องไล่ตรวจลิขสิทธิ์โมเดลทีละตัว

และถ้าเครื่องคุณเป็น Intel Mac ตัวโปรแกรมทำได้แค่หน้าจอ ต้องต่อกับเครื่องอื่นที่รันโมเดล [3]

เริ่มยังไง

ทางที่ตรงที่สุดคือโหลดตัวติดตั้งจากหน้า release ของรีโป มีทั้ง dmg สำหรับ mac, exe สำหรับ Windows, AppImage กับ deb สำหรับ Linux [8]

ถ้าอยากรันบนเซิร์ฟเวอร์ในบ้าน ใช้ Docker จากอิมเมจ GHCR ได้ มีอิมเมจสำหรับ CPU ด้วย ทำงานได้แต่ช้ากว่า ถ้าต้องการความเร็วให้ใช้ NVIDIA CUDA หรือ AMD ROCm [3]

สิ่งที่ผมยังไม่ได้ทำ

ผมยังไม่ได้ติดตั้ง VoiceStudio แล้วสร้างเสียงจริงในรอบนี้ สิ่งที่ผมตรวจคือข้อมูลรีโป เอกสารประสิทธิภาพ เอกสารลิขสิทธิ์ และหน้าโมเดล

ดังนั้นคำถามว่า "เสียงออกมาดีแค่ไหน" ผมตอบด้วยประสบการณ์ตรงไม่ได้ และไม่ควรให้ใครตอบแทนผมด้วยตัวเลขที่ไม่มีที่มา

ถ้าคุณลองแล้ว อยากรู้เหมือนกันว่าเสียงภาษาไทยเป็นอย่างไร เพราะเอกสารระบุจำนวนภาษาเยอะ แต่คุณภาพแต่ละภาษาขึ้นกับข้อมูลฝึกของโมเดล ซึ่งรีโปไม่ได้แยกตัวเลขไว้

SRE vs DevOps: Key Differences and Which Your Startup Needs

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This guide was written by Michael Zion, CEO at MeteorOps, and first published on the MeteorOps blog.

Bottom line

SRE vs DevOps in one paragraph: DevOps is a culture and set of practices for shipping software faster by removing the wall between development and operations. Site reliability engineering (SRE) is a specific, opinionated way to run production that came out of Google: it sets reliability targets (SLOs), spends an error budget against them, and treats operations as a software problem. Google sums it up as "class SRE implements interface DevOps". DevOps optimizes the path to production. SRE optimizes what happens once you are there.

Below is how the two differ in goals, daily work, metrics, skills and pay, how they fit together, and which one a startup actually needs at each stage.

What DevOps actually means

DevOps started as a reaction to a familiar split: developers were measured on shipping features, operations on keeping things stable, and every release became a fight. DevOps says one team should own the whole path from commit to production, with automation doing the repetitive work.

A common way to describe it is CALMS: Culture, Automation, Lean, Measurement and Sharing. In practice that means CI/CD pipelines, infrastructure as code, small and frequent changes, environments anyone can recreate, and developers who can see and act on how their code behaves in production.

"DevOps engineer" is the job title that grew out of this. In most companies a DevOps engineer builds and runs the delivery platform: cloud infrastructure, CI/CD, containers and Kubernetes, secrets, and the tooling that lets developers ship on their own.

What SRE actually means

SRE is what you get when you ask software engineers to run operations. Its central idea is that 100% reliability is the wrong target. Users cannot tell 99.99% from 100%, and chasing the last fraction slows every release. So SRE makes reliability an explicit, measured number:

  • SLI (service level indicator): what you measure, such as the share of requests that succeed in under 300 ms.

  • SLO (service level objective): the target for that indicator over a window, such as 99.9% over 30 days.

  • Error budget: the failure you are allowed. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days allows about 43 minutes of full downtime, or the equivalent in failed requests.

While budget remains, teams ship freely. When it runs out, the priority shifts to reliability work until the service is healthy again. That one rule turns the old dev vs ops argument into a shared, data-driven decision.


The error budget turns a reliability target into a clear rule for when to ship and when to fix.

SRE also sets hard limits on operational load. Google's SRE teams aim to keep toil, the manual, repetitive, automatable work that "scales linearly as a service grows", below 50% of each engineer's time. Of the remaining operational time, no more than 25% should be on-call.

  • Reduce organizational silos: SREs share ownership of production with developers and use the same tools.

How DevOps and SRE work together

In a healthy setup, the platform or DevOps team builds the paved road (pipelines, environments, infrastructure modules), and SRE makes sure what runs on that road meets its reliability targets. Concretely:

  1. Product teams own their services and their SLOs.

  2. The platform team makes the right thing the easy thing: standard deploys, built-in metrics, safe rollbacks.

  3. SREs set the SLO framework, review production readiness, lead major incidents and push systemic fixes back into the platform.

  4. Error budget policy decides, in advance, what happens when a service runs out of budget.

A practical first step for any team is cleaning up alerting so that pages map to user impact. Our article on handling alert fatigue shows how, and the observability stack guide covers what to run at each team size.

Common mistakes

  • Renaming the ops team "SRE". Without SLOs, error budgets and a toil cap, it is the same ops team with a new title.

  • Hiring a "DevOps engineer" to be the only person who can deploy. That rebuilds the silo DevOps was meant to remove.

  • Setting SLOs at 99.99% by default. Each extra nine costs a lot. Start from what users notice and what your dependencies can deliver.

  • Measuring only uptime. Latency and correctness matter to users as much as availability.

  • Treating it as either/or. Most teams need DevOps practices first and SRE practices as they grow, not one instead of the other.

FAQ

Is SRE better than DevOps?

Neither is better; they solve different problems. DevOps improves how you build and ship software. SRE improves how reliably it runs. SRE is often described as one specific implementation of DevOps principles, so a mature organization usually uses both.

Is DevOps a part of SRE, or SRE a part of DevOps?

SRE is generally seen as an implementation of DevOps. Google's phrase is "class SRE implements interface DevOps": DevOps defines the principles, and SRE is a concrete, prescriptive way to apply them to running production services.

Who earns more, SRE or DevOps?

SREs usually earn slightly more. In the 2024 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, the US median was $166,500 for SREs and $145,000 for DevOps specialists. Seniority, location and employer matter more than the title.

Can a DevOps engineer become an SRE?

Yes, it is one of the most common paths. Add stronger programming skills, learn SLO and error budget design, get comfortable leading incidents and postmortems, and go deeper on distributed systems failure modes and capacity planning.

Will AI replace SRE jobs?

AI tools are taking over parts of the work, such as summarizing incidents, correlating alerts and drafting runbooks and postmortems. The judgment parts remain: choosing reliability targets, deciding trade-offs during incidents and designing systems that fail safely. The role is shifting toward that work, not disappearing.

What is the difference between SRE and platform engineering?

Platform engineering builds the internal products developers use to ship: templates, pipelines, environments, an internal developer platform. SRE focuses on the reliability of the services running in production. Many startups combine them in one team until they grow.

Does a startup need an SRE?

Usually not as a first infrastructure hire. Most startups need DevOps capability first, with simple SLOs on critical user journeys. A dedicated SRE starts to pay off when incidents, on-call load or customer uptime commitments take a meaningful share of engineering time.

The short answer

DevOps gets your changes to production quickly and safely. SRE keeps production as reliable as your users need, and no more. Start with DevOps practices, add SLOs early and cheaply, and bring in dedicated SRE when reliability becomes a job of its own.

§ 01 · Definitions

What Is AI Slop?

LinkedIn now has a feedback option called “Seems like AI slop.” Open the menu on a post, choose it, and LinkedIn treats that as a signal that the post did not feel valuable. It does not remove the post or make a policy finding about its author.1

The feature is useful because it names a feeling that has become familiar. A person opens a post promising an insight about leadership, AI agents, or the future of work. The prose is smooth. The paragraphs are clean. There is a confident opening, a tidy list, and a final lesson. Then the reader reaches the end and realizes that no one has actually said anything.

But the label also creates a confusion. People hear “AI slop” and infer “text that an AI wrote.” That is too broad to be useful. An AI can help a person research, outline, translate, edit, test an argument, or make a difficult idea clearer. None of those acts makes the result slop.

The real question is harder: did someone exercise enough judgment to make this worth another person’s attention?

The distinction LinkedIn gets right

LinkedIn’s own definition is better than the name of its button. It describes AI slop as low-effort, likely AI-generated content that sounds polished on the surface but lacks a point of view, unique perspective, or substance. It says the distinction is not how content was created, but whether it adds value and reflects a person’s perspective, experience, or expertise.2

That is the right center of gravity. The generated sentence is not the offense. The unexamined sentence is.

Imagine three people publishing an article about AI agents:

  • Generate → publish: What happened: A model produces 1,500 words and the author pastes them into a post.; Slop?: Usually.
  • Human idea → AI draft → human edit: What happened: The author supplies a thesis, examples, corrections, and a real rewrite.; Slop?: Usually not.
  • Human research → AI synthesis → human judgment: What happened: The author decides which sources matter, checks the claims, and stands behind the conclusion.; Slop?: No.

The table is intentionally about process rather than style. The visible artifact is a weak signal. A heavily directed and carefully edited piece can retain the rhythm of a model. A person can write generic, derivative, attention-seeking prose without touching an AI. Manual typing has never been a reliable measure of thought.

Two articles can therefore look similar while being fundamentally different. One may be prompt, generate, publish. The other may contain an idea, reporting, context construction, criticism, correction, and verification before the final generation. A reader cannot reliably see that history from punctuation, em dashes, headings, or an AI-detection score.

Calling both pieces slop because both contain generated tokens collapses authorship into keystrokes. Authorship is better understood as the work of choosing: choosing the question, the evidence, the examples, the omissions, and the words one is prepared to defend.

A family resemblance, not a test

There is no settled technical definition of AI slop. A recent scholarly paper argues that it has resisted formal definition and proposes three recurring properties instead: superficial competence, asymmetric effort, and mass producibility.3

Those properties explain why the phrase feels precise even when its boundary is fuzzy.

Superficial competence is the polished shell. The grammar works. The image looks plausible. The memo has the expected headings. Underneath, the claims are unsupported, the summary never gets specific, or the apparent conclusion is just the premise repeated in better phrasing.

Asymmetric effort is the production problem. It takes seconds to make a convincing-looking artifact and much longer for another person to decide whether it is accurate, useful, or original. The producer gets the convenience; the reader, reviewer, customer, or colleague inherits the verification cost.

Mass producibility is the ecosystem problem. One mediocre image or awkward paragraph is not necessarily what people mean by slop. The term becomes more apt when a system can make thousands of near-substitutes for search rankings, ad impressions, engagement, or the appearance of output. Reporting on the rise of the term has repeatedly connected it to low-quality text, images, videos, and entire sites produced cheaply for search traffic.4

None of these is a pass-fail test. A personalized surreal image can be silly and still mean something to the person who made it. A concise generated answer to a narrow question can be exactly what someone needs. The scholarly account makes this point too: so-called slop can meet demand for niche or personalized content and can carry aesthetic or social value.5

That is why “I dislike it” and “it is AI slop” should not be interchangeable. The term is most useful when it names a failure that affects someone beyond the author: an output that asks for attention, trust, or review without having been given enough thought to earn any of them.

The missing word is judgment

The usual list of tells is familiar: generic language, repeated ideas, unnecessary length, fake profundity, too many headings, confident claims with no evidence. Those are symptoms. They matter because they suggest an absence of judgment, but none proves it.

A person can use every fashionable AI writing habit and still make an original, careful argument. Another can avoid every tell and publish a confident falsehood they wrote alone. The important difference is whether someone took responsibility for the content: checked what could be checked, removed what did not hold up, supplied the context a model could not know, and decided that the piece had a reason to exist.

This makes a better working definition:

AI slop is generated output whose cost of production has fallen much faster than the effort spent deciding whether it deserves to exist.

Or, in the shortest form: AI slop is a failure of judgment, not a method of generation.

The definition does not let every AI-assisted author off the hook. It raises the bar in the place that matters. If a model makes drafting cheap, then choosing, checking, and editing become more valuable, not less. The person who publishes the result still owns the claim.

The software version of the same problem

This is not only a problem for feeds. Generative AI can produce code, documentation, tests, issue comments, design notes, and pull requests much faster than a team can understand them. The same asymmetry appears: generating an artifact is cheap; reviewing it, maintaining it, and recovering its rationale are expensive.

The danger is not that AI wrote a function. A well-directed model can remove drudgery and leave more time for work that needs judgment. The danger arrives when a team measures generated volume as if it were progress, accepts code that nobody can explain, or creates documentation that no one has verified against the system it describes.

That is engineering slop: artifacts that create the appearance of movement while increasing uncertainty about why they exist, whether they are correct, and who understands them. The technical debt is not simply AI-generated code. It is the gap between what the repository contains and what its humans can account for.

The practical response is ordinary, even if the tools are new. Ask before publishing or merging: What is the point? What is new here? What did we verify? Who will own this when it breaks? If those questions do not have good answers, the artifact is not finished merely because it looks finished.

LinkedIn’s button cannot reconstruct the invisible process behind a post. No detector can do that reliably either. But the phrase can still be useful if it reminds us what readers are reacting to: not the presence of a machine, but the absence of a mind that cared enough to decide.

§ 02 · The Debate

The Argument About the Word

“Slop” is a hostile word. That is part of its usefulness and part of its risk. It carries the picture of something poured into a trough: plentiful, uniform, barely worth choosing. It is not a neutral description of a production method.

That matters because the word is now used for at least four different complaints. Sometimes it means false information. Sometimes it means ugly or derivative culture. Sometimes it means a volume problem: too much content competing for the same attention. And sometimes it means a moral complaint about a creator who outsourced too much of the work.

Those are related, but they are not the same.

A fabricated local-news story is dangerous because it can be false. A hundred perfectly grammatical product descriptions can be useless because they add no information. A strange AI video made for one family may be neither useful nor dangerous; it may simply be a new form of play. If every one of these is called slop, the word stops helping us decide what to do next.

The academic account of the term is unusually careful here. It does not offer a test that separates slop from non-slop. It offers a family resemblance: superficial competence, asymmetric effort, and mass producibility.6 That is a better starting point than an AI detector because it points at the social conditions around the artifact.

The boundary will remain fuzzy because value is contextual. A generated bedtime story made for one child may be cheap to create and deeply valuable to that child. A heavily researched report may use model-written sentences and still earn attention because the author made the decisive choices. The object does not announce its process.

The question is therefore not “Was AI used?” It is: what burden did this artifact place on other people, and what care did its maker take before placing it there?

In practice

  • Name the failure precisely. Say false, repetitive, derivative, or unreviewed when that is what you mean.
  • Do not make style a verdict. Smooth prose is neither evidence of care nor evidence of its absence.
  • Reserve “slop” for the larger failure. It is most useful when cheap output has shifted verification, attention, or cleanup onto somebody else.

§ 03 · The Mechanism

When Output Became Cheap

For most of the internet’s life, publishing imposed a small amount of friction. Someone had to write the article, make the image, record the voiceover, or assemble the page. The cost was never high enough to guarantee quality. It was high enough to force a choice.

Generative systems changed the shape of that choice. They did not make judgment cheap. They made the artifacts that normally signal judgment cheap: paragraphs, illustrations, outlines, summaries, scripts, reviews, and code.

That creates a predictable temptation. When the cost of production falls, the producer can run more experiments. Some experiments are good: a teacher can make an explanation for a student who needs it; a small business can translate a manual; an engineer can draft a test case. Others are attempts to capture attention at a price close to zero.

Content farms have found the second use especially attractive. NewsGuard’s tracker describes thousands of unreliable AI-generated news sites operating with little or no human oversight. It reports that such sites often publish dozens of articles a day, and that programmatic advertising can reward them without regard to the quality of the site carrying the ad.7

The important fact is not that the articles are machine-written. The business model does not need a reader to learn anything. It needs a page to be indexed, clicked, and shown an advertisement.

This is why scale changes the moral texture of mediocre content. A weak essay asks for five minutes. Ten thousand weak essays change the cost of finding anything strong. They turn discernment into unpaid labor for everyone downstream: the reader searching, the editor reviewing, the platform ranking, the model training on the resulting web.

Cheap supply does not create demand for meaning. It can, however, create a great deal of material that resembles meaning at first glance.

In practice

  • Measure a publishing system by reader benefit, not artifacts shipped.
  • Ask what each item is for. “To rank” and “to help a reader decide” are not interchangeable purposes.
  • Put human review where distribution is automated. Scale without review is the condition in which a small quality problem becomes a public one.

§ 04 · The Limit

The Artifact Cannot Tell You

A reader sees the final text. They do not see the notebook beside it, the rejected drafts, the source tabs, the fact check, or the moment someone removed a sentence because it could not be defended.

This is the central difficulty in calling something AI slop. We can observe the artifact and infer from it. We cannot observe the process that produced it.

The familiar tells are weak evidence. Certain transitions, an abundance of headings, em dashes, a polished but generic cadence: these may suggest a model, but they do not tell us whether an author supplied a real claim, constructed the context, or checked the result. A detector faces the same limit. Even if it identified machine-generated prose perfectly, it would identify generation, not judgment.

The opposite error is equally common. A human can type every word of a vague, derivative post. The labor is real; the contribution may still be zero. We should not confuse authorship with keystrokes.

Authorship is the chain of decisions that makes an artifact answerable: why this question, why these sources, why this example, why this conclusion, why now. AI can participate in that chain. It cannot remove the need for someone to own it.

This is why disclosure alone is not a quality test. “Written with AI” can be honest and still leave a reader with no reason to trust the work. “Written by a human” can conceal carelessness. The more useful convention is responsibility: the named author should be able to explain and defend the work.

In practice

  • Keep an evidence trail for consequential work. A short list of sources and decisions is more meaningful than a purity claim about tools.
  • Use disclosure to clarify process, not to borrow credibility.
  • Ask the author question. Can the person whose name is on this explain why the claim is here and what would change their mind?

§ 05 · The Cost

The Price Paid by Readers

The complaint about AI slop is often described as aesthetic: too many strange images, too much bland prose, another LinkedIn post that sounds like every other LinkedIn post. That is real, but it is not the deepest cost.

The deeper cost is attention. Every plausible-looking low-value artifact asks a reader to make a small investigation. Is this true? Is it copied? Is there a real person behind it? Is the advice specific enough to use? Each investigation is cheap in isolation. At scale, it makes the information environment tiring.

That fatigue changes behavior. Readers skim more aggressively. They trust less. They favor familiar sources because checking new ones is expensive. Small publishers and careful novices then pay a tax created by people who can flood the same channels with cheaper imitations.

Researchers discussing AI slop describe the asymmetry directly: output can take far less effort to generate than a comparable human-made artifact, while its surface competence can disguise a lack of substance.8 The reader must do the expensive part after the producer has skipped it.

The problem resembles spam, but only up to a point. Spam filters work because many messages share patterns and because the cost of a false negative is often low. Slop may be persuasive, topical, and nearly correct. The most expensive cases are not absurd. They are the calm, credible paragraph that leaves out the one condition that matters.

This is also why a blanket hostility to synthetic media is a poor answer. The goal is not a return to a world where only people with spare time can publish. The goal is a world where the cost of publishing does not become an invoice sent to everyone who reads.

In practice

  • Respect attention as a finite resource. Do not publish a summary when a link and one sentence would do.
  • Make claims checkable. Link the source, name the uncertainty, and remove decorative certainty.
  • Reward useful specificity. It is harder to mass-produce and easier for a reader to test.

§ 06 · The Workplace

When Slop Enters the Repository

Software teams are beginning to meet the same problem in a form they recognize immediately. An AI assistant can generate a feature branch, a test suite, a migration, a design memo, and a pull-request description before the reviewer has finished reading the ticket.

The code may run. That is not the end of the question.

Code has a longer afterlife than a post. It will be reviewed, deployed, debugged, changed by someone new, and used as evidence of how the system works. A patch that its author cannot explain transfers all of that future reasoning to other people.

A recent paper frames this as a software commons problem: individual gains from AI-generated output can externalize costs onto reviewer capacity, codebase integrity, shared knowledge, and collaborative trust.9 Another qualitative study of developer discussion identified review friction and quality degradation as central themes in complaints about AI-assisted software development.10 These are early accounts, not settled measurements. They are still useful because they name where the cost appears.

The engineering version of slop is therefore not “code made with AI.” It is code that creates the appearance of progress while increasing uncertainty about behavior, rationale, and ownership.

The crucial question is not whether the assistant wrote the first draft. It is whether a human can trace the change from requirement to design to tests and can carry the responsibility when it fails. When that chain exists, AI can reduce drudgery. When it does not, generated volume becomes technical debt at production speed.

In practice

  • Review for explanation, not only execution. A passing test is necessary; an understood change is durable.
  • Require a rationale for non-obvious code. Generated comments are not a rationale unless someone verified them.
  • Keep changes small enough to own. A reviewer should be able to say what changed, why it is safe, and who will maintain it.

§ 07 · The Test

The Responsibility Test

There is no reliable detector for care. That does not leave us helpless. It means the standard should be behavioral rather than forensic.

A useful responsibility test has five questions.

What is the point? A piece should be able to name the question it answers for a particular reader. “Content about AI” is a category, not a point.

What is new here? The answer may be a firsthand observation, a synthesis, a worked example, a useful framing, or a decision. If nothing is new, a link is usually better than a post.

What was checked? This does not demand impossible certainty. It demands that a publisher distinguish verified facts, interpretation, and speculation.

What was removed? Editing is evidence of judgment. The sentences that did not survive are often more important than the sentences that did.

Who owns the result? The person who publishes or merges should be able to answer questions about it without blaming the model.

This test is deliberately indifferent to how many tokens a machine produced. It makes room for a writer who uses AI extensively and for one who uses none. It also makes the actual obligation visible: publishing is a promise to bear some of the reader’s verification burden yourself.

In organizations, this should be a process rather than a cultural wish. People need enough time to read outputs, enough permission to say “this does not add up,” and incentives that do not reward artifact count over outcomes.

The strongest defense against slop is not better taste. It is a system in which care is cheaper to exercise than carelessness is to pass downstream.

In practice

  • Add the five questions to publishing and PR templates.
  • Treat uncertainty as useful information. “We do not know” is often the most valuable sentence in a report.
  • Make ownership explicit. A named owner is not decoration; it tells others where understanding is supposed to live.

§ 08 · The Practice

What to Do on Monday

The useful response to AI slop is not to ban tools or to become a detective of punctuation. Both answers attack the visible surface and miss the real failure.

Start with the work that leaves your hands.

Before publishing, ask whether the piece contains a claim, a reader, and a reason. Remove the paragraph that only restates the title. Link the source that carries the fact. Replace a generic example with the actual case that changed your mind. If the answer is not ready, keep it as a note.

Before merging, ask whether the author can narrate the change. Ask what was tested, what assumptions remain, and what would make the design wrong. Keep the change small enough for a reviewer to understand. These are not anti-AI rules. They are the old rules of responsible work, made urgent by a new rate of output.

At the platform level, reward useful material rather than the frequency of posting. LinkedIn’s own guidance makes the same broad distinction: AI-assisted content is welcome when it reflects a real person’s perspective, experience, or expertise; generic and repetitive material is the problem.11 That is a reasonable direction, even if no feedback button can see the full process behind a post.

The word “slop” will remain imprecise. It may even become too broad to survive. The underlying problem will not disappear with the vocabulary: when production gets easier, judgment becomes the scarce resource.

That is the thing worth defending. Not human typing for its own sake. Not a nostalgia for artificial scarcity. The expectation that when a person asks for our attention, someone has done the work of deciding why we should give it.

In practice

  • Use AI to increase the quality of decisions, not only the quantity of artifacts.
  • Publish less when less is all you have. Silence is cheaper than asking strangers to sort through a draft.
  • Stand behind the work. That is what authorship has always meant.

Retry Timing Is a Hypothesis. Test It Like One.

Ask five billing operators when a failed payment should be retried and you will get five confident answers, each certain, each different, and almost none of them tested. Retry timing is usually inherited — from a platform default, a predecessor's spreadsheet, or a blog post (possibly like this one) — and then defended as if it were a finding. It is not a finding. It is a hypothesis about your subscribers' lives, and it deserves the same treatment as any other hypothesis: a test with a result you are willing to be wrong about.

The hypothesis has more dimensions than "which day." There is the interval before the first retry, which trades urgency against the issuer's memory of the refusal. There is spacing between attempts, which for insufficient-funds declines is really a bet on pay cycles — biweekly Fridays, monthly firsts, the weekly rhythm of gig income. There is time of day, which interacts with bank posting order in ways that matter for accounts running close to zero. And there is the total window: how long recovery should keep trying before the honest conclusion is that this subscriber needs a conversation, not another attempt.

Testing any of this is unglamorous and entirely doable. Hold the message copy constant and vary one timing dimension for one decline family at a time — soft declines only, say, so hard declines do not pollute the read. Insufficient funds is the natural first subject because its mechanism is explicitly temporal: if your retries are landing before payday, moving them should move the recovery rate in a way no copy change ever could. Give the test enough failures to mean something; on a small base, a fortnight of declines is anecdote, not evidence, and the discipline is mostly in not peeking early and declaring victory.

Two traps wait for the impatient. The first is testing everything at once — new spacing, new emails, new sender name — and learning nothing except that something changed. The second is reading recovery rate alone. A timing change that recovers more payments while doubling attempts per recovery may be worse, not better: attempts cost fees and issuer goodwill. Record attempts per recovery alongside the rate, and unsubscribes or cancels that arrive inside the dunning window, because timing that bullies people into paying once and leaving is not recovery — it is churn with extra steps.

What you are really building, test by test, is a map of your own subscribers rather than the average subscriber in a benchmark post. Gym members paid on shift cycles, newsletter readers on salaries, course buyers on neither — the right retry calendar is a fact about a specific business, which is why copying a famous schedule so rarely works. A grounded reference on when to retry a failed payment can frame the options; only your own decline file can choose among them.

Start with the payday question. It is the cheapest test in billing, the answer is usually visible within one cycle, and it teaches the habit every other timing decision needs: the schedule works for the subscribers, or it does not work at all.

Crediting a booking to the AI agent that sent it, without cookies

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When an AI agent recommends a food tour and the person books it, the booking platform pays a commission to whoever owns the link. We wanted that money to reach the agent, or the person or company behind it, so we opened a partner program that pays out half of what we earn. The hard part was not the payout. It was making sure each booking, reported weeks later by a partner we do not control, can be traced to the agent that sent it.

This post covers how that works at TableJourney, a food travel guide with a public MCP server. None of it depends on a cookie in the traveller's browser.

The only thing that survives: the partner's own sub-id

Every booking partner reports commissions per booking, and every one of them echoes back one free-text field you put on the link:

Partner Field GetYourGuide cmp Viator campaign Musement cid Awin (hotels, car hire) clickref CJ (hotels) sid

That field is the whole attribution system. Anything we know at click time has to be written into it, because it is the only thing that comes back in the commission report or the network's transaction API. So each member gets a short code, and the value we write is tjr-<code>-<placement>, for example tjr-k7m2p9xq-city-hub-module. The prefix lets the ledger pick member lines out of everything else with one LIKE 'tjr-%', and the placement tells us which part of the page converted.

Agents: the link is born attributed

An agent never sees a raw partner URL. When it calls a tool such as experiences or stays with its API key, every booking link in the answer is a short signed redirect on our domain:

https://tablejourney.com/go/<payload>.<hmac>

The payload names the key, the kind of link and the destination. The redirect checks the signature, looks up the key's campaign, writes it into that partner's sub-id field and sends a 302. The agent passes the link on unchanged and the attribution is already inside it. Nothing needs to persist on the traveller's device, which matters because the traveller may open the link in a different browser, a chat app's web view, or days later.

Getting a key is two MCP calls: partner_join with an email address, which sends a six-digit code, and partner_confirm with that code, which returns the key and the referral code. For clients that prefer OAuth there is the usual discovery (RFC 8414 metadata, RFC 7591 dynamic registration, PKCE).

People: hold the code, stamp every link

A person shares any page with ?ref=<code> on the end. On arrival, a first-party cookie set by our server (not by script, so browsers that cap script-written storage at seven days leave it alone) holds the code for 30 days, and the page rewrites every partner link's href on load.

Rewriting on load, rather than on click, is deliberate. We tried click-time tagging first and lost attribution on more than half of the links: middle-click, "open in new tab" from the context menu, long-press on a phone and "copy link address" never fire a click event. So the stamp goes into the href itself, and the click handler only re-checks it.

The links that appear later

Our first audit of this found a gap. Two kinds of partner links were not in the page when the load-time pass ran:

  • hotel links whose dates are filled in by script with the coming weekend, which replaced the stamped href with a fresh, unstamped one;
  • cards swapped in after a location lookup ("near you") and the booking bar on phones.

Clicks still carried the code, but a long-press on iOS opened the unstamped address. The fix is a MutationObserver that stamps any partner link added to the page or whose href changes:

new MutationObserver(function (ms) { ms.forEach(function (m) { var nodes = m.type === 'attributes' ? [m.target] : m.addedNodes; Array.prototype.forEach.call(nodes, function (n) { if (n.nodeType !== 1) return; if (n.matches('a[rel~="sponsored"]')) stamp(n); if (m.type === 'childList') n.querySelectorAll('a[rel~="sponsored"]').forEach(stamp); }); }); }).observe(document.documentElement, { subtree: true, childList: true, attributes: true, attributeFilter: ['href', 'rel'] });

stamp leaves a link alone when its sub-id already starts with the member's mark, so its own write does not loop. A check across 15 live pages afterwards found the code on all 699 partner links.

Iframes on other people's sites

Our food tours widget is an iframe other sites embed. It runs no script, by design, so the page inside the frame cannot stamp anything. A member who embeds it adds their code to the frame's address (/embed/tours/italy/rome/?ref=<code>), and each card links to a redirect on our domain instead of straight to the partner:

/go/embed?u=<partner link>

The click is a same-origin request from the frame's document, so under the default strict-origin-when-cross-origin referrer policy the browser sends the frame's full address, query string included, as the Referer. The redirect reads the code from it, checks it belongs to an active member, writes it into the sub-id and logs the click. It only accepts the two partners that allow our links on third-party sites, so it is not an open redirect.

Reading it back

Once a day the network APIs (Awin, CJ) return transactions with the sub-id, and the partners without one are imported from their own reports, broken down by the same field. A line with a tjr- sub-id becomes a member earning at the member's share; it becomes payable only after the partner has paid us and a holding period has passed, and every payout batch is approved by a person.

Etsy Title Templates: Six Structures That Fit Inside 140 Characters

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Etsy gives you 140 characters for a listing title. The median seller uses about 70 of them on words that describe nothing, then wonders why the listing is hard to find.

This is not a writing problem. It is an allocation problem. You have 140 slots, you need to fill them with searchable intent, and the order you put them in determines how much of the tail survives.

Here are six title structures you can paste straight into a listing, with the character counts worked out, plus the reasoning behind each one.

The rule that makes the rest work

Etsy reads titles left to right. Buyers scan them left to right. The earliest words carry the most weight inside the title field.

So the 140 characters are not a budget to fill. They are a budget to front-load.

If your primary keyword sits at character 118, you have written a title that works for exactly one search and buries your best term behind eleven words of preamble.

One practical target before the templates: aim for 80-130 characters of readable title. Long enough to carry a product noun, a qualifier, an occasion and a synonym tail. Short enough that a phone screen shows the whole thing without cutting off the payoff phrase.

The four-part structure every template is built from

Read any good Etsy title as four parts in order:

  1. What it is - the exact product noun ("initial name necklace")
  2. The defining detail - the feature that makes yours different ("personalized birthstone pendant")
  3. Who or what it is for - recipient or occasion ("custom gift for her")
  4. The searchable tail - synonyms and materials buyers also type ("sterling silver necklace birthday present")

What it is, the detail, the occasion, the tail. Every template below is a different way of filling those four slots for a different product type.

A before and after, with the numbers

Before:

Handmade Personalized Custom Initial Name Necklace for Women Gift, Birthstone Birth Month Gift for Her, Sterling Silver Pendant Necklace, Birthday Present for Girlfriend Wife

That is 192 characters. At 140, everything after the comma following "Gift" is discarded. No error, no warning, the listing saves fine.

The seller believes they covered "birthstone", "birth month", "sterling silver", "birthday present" and "girlfriend". They covered none of them.

What actually survives in the index:

Handmade Personalized Custom Initial Name Necklace for Women Gift, Birthstone Birth Month Gift for Her, Sterling Silver Pendant

Look at the front: "Handmade Personalized Custom Initial" is four slots spent saying almost nothing. The word "necklace" - the actual product noun, the thing buyers type - does not appear until character 51.

After:

Personalized Birthstone Pendant for Women, Custom Gift for Her, Sterling Silver Necklace Birthday Present

That is 124 characters. Nothing is truncated.

Measure Before After Characters 1-25 Handmade Personalized Custom Initial Name Necklace Product noun appears at character 51 character 13 Distinct intents covered in 140 chars 3 6 Wasted leading words 4 0

The rewritten title covers more search intents in fewer characters. That is the whole point.

Truncation is not really the enemy - padding is. A padded title loses characters to filler, and then loses the tail to the limit.

Template 1: the universal four-part

[Product noun], [defining detail], [occasion or recipient], [synonym or material tail]

Fill-in example for a ceramic mug:

Custom Name Mug, Handmade Stoneware Coffee Cup, Birthday Gift for Her, Personalized Ceramic Latte Cup

Character count: 99.

Use this when you sell a physical product with a clear noun and one differentiating feature. It is the safest default and it is the structure most sellers should start with.

Template 2: the gift-buyer template

Gift searches dominate Etsy. Phrases like "gift for her", "birthday gift for her" and "gift for mom" show up across our measured buyer-phrase set with demand scores of 92, 91 and 93 respectively, and the listings carrying them run into the thousands - 2,890 listings for "gift for her", 2,030 for "birthday gift for her".

[Occasion] [Product noun] for [Recipient], [Defining detail], [Material or synonym]

Fill-in example:

Birthday Gift for Mom, Personalized Name Necklace, Handmade Clay Bead, Sterling Silver Birthstone Pendant

Character count: 100.

Put the occasion first here on purpose. When the buyer is shopping a gift, they are searching the occasion, not your product type. The product noun still lands inside the first 40 characters, so you do not lose the category signal.

Template 3: the material-led template

For jewellery, ceramics, leather and metal work, material is a strong filter and buyers type it explicitly.

[Material] [Product noun] [Defining detail], [Occasion or recipient], [Synonym]

Fill-in example:

Sterling Silver Name Necklace, Personalized Birthstone Pendant, Gift for Her, Custom Initial Chain

Character count: 99.

Note the order: material first, noun second. Buyers filtering by "sterling silver" want to see it immediately, and the noun stays within the first 20 characters so the category signal survives.

Template 4: the digital-download template

Digital products are searched differently. The buyer is searching for the format and the problem, not the object.

[Format] [Topic] [Who it's for], [What they get], [File type or style]

Fill-in example:

Printable Wedding Invitation Template, Rustic Minimalist, Editable Canva Template, 5x7 A4 Digital Download

Character count: 101.

Etsy digital listings live or die on the format phrase. "Printable", "template", "SVG", "PDF", "editable" are all high-intent modifiers that buyers type into the search box.

Template 5: the ultra-short template

Some products do not need 140 characters. A simple, well-understood item can outperform a stuffed title.

[Product noun], [one qualifier], [one occasion]

Fill-in example:

Gold Initial Necklace, Dainty Layered Chain, Birthday Gift for Her

Character count: 63.

Use this only when the product noun is unambiguous. If a buyer could call it three different things, every word you drop is a search you lose.

Template 6: the attribute-first template

If your product has a strong functional attribute - waterproof, adjustable, dishwasher safe, reusable - put it up front. Attribute filters convert browsing into intent.

[Attribute] [Product noun], [Defining detail], [Occasion], [Synonym]

Fill-in example:

Adjustable Gold Chain Bracelet, Dainty Charm, Gift for Her, Everyday Wear Layered Bracelet

Character count: 94.

A length cheat sheet

Title length What to do Under 50 characters Too short. You are leaving search intents unclaimed. Add a qualifier and an occasion. 50-80 characters Reasonable for a very simple product. Often fine as-is. 80-130 characters The sweet spot. Primary term, detail, occasion and tail all fit. 130-140 characters Tight but valid. Make sure the primary term is in the first 40 characters. Over 140 characters Truncated or rejected. Whatever is past the limit is not indexed.

Nine mistakes that quietly cost you clicks

  1. Keyword stuffing. "Necklace Necklace Silver Necklace Gift" - Etsy already de-duplicates, and you just look desperate.
  2. Leading brand names. Unless you are the brand, a famous brand name in your title costs you relevance for your own product.
  3. Price and shipping words. "Free shipping cheap discount sale" - Etsy filters on price already. This is dead weight in a 140-character budget.
  4. Front-loading the filler. "Handmade Personalized Custom Beautiful" - four words that describe nothing.
  5. Writing one long sentence. Commas separate search-relevant segments. Etsy reads each segment; a title without commas reads as one long phrase.
  6. Burying the noun. If "necklace" is not in the first 40 characters, the title is working against itself.
  7. Ignoring the tail. The last 20 characters are a free slot for synonyms - use them.
  8. Punctuation and hashtags. Etsy strips them. Zero return, wasted characters.
  9. Leaving the title stale. Renaming a listing every couple of weeks is one of the cheapest refresh signals you have.

A 10-minute title checklist

  • Does the product noun appear in the first 40 characters?
  • Is the total length between 80 and 130?
  • Does the title contain commas separating distinct keyword phrases?
  • Is every one of the 140 characters doing work?
  • Have you removed price, shipping and "best/cheap" words?
  • Do you have a synonym for the tail that buyers actually type?
  • Does it read as a phrase a human would say?

Where tags come in, and why the title carries the long phrases

A title is only half your search surface. Tags are the other half, and they behave differently: tags are how Etsy finds listings that a buyer's query did not literally match.

The catch is that each tag is capped at 20 characters, and anything longer is discarded silently - no error, no partial credit. A tag like "handmade custom personalized jewelry gift" does not become a partial tag. It does not exist.

That constraint has an interesting implication for titles. Since long phrases get clipped in tags, the title is where your longer multi-word phrases must live. If "initial name necklace" is worth saying, it belongs in the title, not squeezed into a 20-character tag slot.

The circularity trap in most tag tools

Most tag generators read your own title and hand the words back to you. That is circular. If you never knew buyers search "birthstone pendant", re-reading your own listing will never surface it, because the phrase was never in there to begin with.

The fix is to source tags from a dictionary of phrases real buyers actually used, rather than from your own copy. When we built ours, we harvested 1,128 live listings across 23 keyword seeds (23/23 successful runs), which yielded 504 distinct buyer search phrases, of which 457 survived a filter that strips specification strings and duplicates. Every suggestion is drawn from those 457 phrases.

The bottom line

The 140-character limit is real, but treating it as a target is the mistake. Treat it as a hard stop and front-load.

Every character you spend on filler is a character stolen from a keyword, and the tail you think is still there is not.

Pick one of the six templates above, drop it into your ten slowest listings, and count the characters before you publish. Scientists thought the Bangka slow loris was extinct for a century until one got stuck in a garden fence

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A Bangka slow loris was caught in a garden fence in Tua Tunu, in the city of Pangkal Pinang, on July 7, 2025. Staff at the Alobi Wildlife Rescue Center, on the Indonesian island of Bangka, entered it in their records as 5/2025.

By then scientists had not recorded the species for nearly a century. The IUCN Red List had it down as Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct).

That loris is one of more than 90 that Alobi rescued between 2015 and August 2025, all counted in a paper in the October 2026 issue of the journal Spixiana. No verified count of the wild population exists yet.

Glaw meets Menner in Java

Timon Glaw, from the Haus des Meeres Aqua Terra Zoo in Vienna, was at the Taman Safari Prigen Conservation Breeding Ark in East Java. There Jochen Menner, a conservationist, mentioned that the loris was listed as possibly extinct.

It also sat on the most wanted lost primates list kept by the group Re:wild. Menner gave Glaw a contact at Alobi, north of Pangkal Pinang, and the rescue center turned out to have documented encounters with the species.

Three Alobi staff members, Langka Sani, Endi Riadi and Andre Septian, are co-authors with Menner and Glaw. Their records were in Indonesian.

The team put them through Google Translate and DeepL, and each rescue spot was placed in Google Earth and then checked with Alobi.

According to Re:wild, residents and local authorities have been reporting slow lorises to Alobi for more than a decade. Re:wild’s exploration program manager, Benji Barca, said Alobi’s “careful records are what have finally now brought this primate back into scientific view.”

Re:wild describes the animal as small and reddish-backed with a pale facial mask. Slow lorises are the only primates known to be venomous.

They lick a gland on the inside of the upper arm and mix its secretion with saliva, so their bite can be painful and cause swelling.

Sody’s three males from 1937

M. W. Lyon named the species in 1906 from Klabat Bay, in the north of Bangka. Later authors put it under the Sunda slow loris, Nycticebus coucang, and then the Philippine slow loris, N. menagensis. Munds and colleagues gave it species rank in 2013.

The IUCN puts the last sighting in 1937 but does not say where that comes from. Most likely, the authors write, it is H. J. V. Sody’s 1937 paper on the mammals of Bangka. Sody looked at three adult males and gave no collection dates or precise localities.

A 2025 review of the Naturalis museum collection adds that forestry officers collected some of Sody’s Bangka mammals between November 1930 and September 1932. Whether his three lorises were among them is unknown.

Farel Situmorang wrote a 2021 bachelor’s thesis at Sriwijaya University on two lorises kept at Alobi, and iNaturalist users posted likely sightings on July 9, 2023, November 3, 2025, and in April 2026.

Houses, gardens and roads

Calls came by phone, WhatsApp or social media, from locals or from the authorities. From 2021, Alobi kept case notes on 43 rescued animals, 39 of them with a known location.

One was collected at a school in Kimak on November 17, 2021. Two more, found in a house in Air Mesu, had been kept there for two months before they were reported to Alobi on January 14, 2022.

Firefighters found one in a house on November 13, 2023. In Kacang Pedang on August 4, 2024, an injured loris turned up on a pickup back from banana plantations. On October 26 that year, one was eating guavas in a garden in Namang.

Sixteen of the 43 had got into houses or gardens, and six were picked up off roads. Seven had burns that fit an electric shock, most of them apparently from power lines. Five came from the wildlife trade or from people who kept them.

Plantations accounted for three, and one had been attacked by a dog. At least three died.

Seven release forests

Of all the lorises, 86 went back into the forest at seven sites. Bukit Mangkol Forest Park in Central Bangka took the most, 37. The Permisan nature park at Bukit Nenek in South Bangka got 22, and Gunung Menumbing Forest Park in West Bangka 15.

Jering Menduyung got six and the Bangka Island Outdoor forest three. Gunung Maras National Park took two, and Payak Riang near the village of Zed one.

Staff at the Alobi Foundation chose forests that still have some legal or practical protection.

No one has followed the released animals to see how they fare. For other slow loris species, the authors note, survival after release has been relatively low. A separate study of rescued primates found that a gradual return to the wild worked well.

Tin pits and oil palms

Bangka covers about 4,600 square miles (12,000 square kilometers). More than 1 million people live there, and tin has been mined on the island for over 300 years.

Between 2000 and 2012, a 2017 study by Supriatna and colleagues found, the species lost 62 percent of its forest habitat.

The team went to Bukit Mangkol on July 31, 2023, with Lintas Ardati, who supported the rescue work in the field. Two unknown people had lit a fire there on purpose, and locals were working small tin mines.

A possible Bangka slow loris habitat at Bukit Mangkol, also used by Alobi to release rescued animals. Smoke and flames from a small forest fire are visible on the slope. Credit: Sani et al., Spixiana (2026). Click image to enlarge.

For a few hours, including after dusk, they looked for lorises and saw none. The authors say this was not a systematic survey.

“We are glad to contribute to the scientific record to show that the Bangka slow loris is not lost, but the species is still very close to extinction,” said Sani, who directs Alobi.

In Sani’s words, its habitat “has shrunk significantly because of illegal tin mining and massive land clearing for oil palm plantations.”

Slow lorises are sold as pets in Indonesia, and viral videos have pushed up that demand. Alobi staff told the authors the trade on Bangka seems smaller. Still, a loris from Pagarawan reached Alobi on February 9, 2022, after about a year as a pet.

No census or DNA yet

The species is identified by fur, face mask and teeth, not by DNA. A 2023 genetic study by Blair and colleagues included no animal from Bangka.

Maria Nara and Joko Trianto photographed a skeleton and a skull kept at Alobi for the paper. The upper jaw has two incisors, where lorises from Sumatra and Java have four. Two matches the slow lorises of Borneo, so the authors doubt the Bangka animals came from Sumatra or Java.

“Bringing the Bangka slow loris back onto the scientific radar is only the first step,” Glaw said. “We now know the species has survived in several parts of Bangka, and we urgently need a long-term conservation strategy built on population surveys, genetic studies and, above all, protection of its remaining habitat.”

The paper calls for surveys on Bangka, Belitung and Borneo and for an IUCN review of the Possibly Extinct tag.

In 2023 researchers in Indonesia found Attenborough’s long-beaked echidna in the Cyclops Mountains, another animal lost to science. Case 5/2025, the loris from the fence in Tua Tunu, is the last entry in the paper’s appendix.

The full study was published in the journal Spixiana.
