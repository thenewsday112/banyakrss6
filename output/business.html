Retry Timing Is a Hypothesis. Test It Like One.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ask five billing operators when a failed payment should be retried and you will get five confident answers, each certain, each different, and almost none of them tested. Retry timing is usually inherited — from a platform default, a predecessor's spreadsheet, or a blog post (possibly like this one) — and then defended as if it were a finding. It is not a finding. It is a hypothesis about your subscribers' lives, and it deserves the same treatment as any other hypothesis: a test with a result you are willing to be wrong about.

The hypothesis has more dimensions than "which day." There is the interval before the first retry, which trades urgency against the issuer's memory of the refusal. There is spacing between attempts, which for insufficient-funds declines is really a bet on pay cycles — biweekly Fridays, monthly firsts, the weekly rhythm of gig income. There is time of day, which interacts with bank posting order in ways that matter for accounts running close to zero. And there is the total window: how long recovery should keep trying before the honest conclusion is that this subscriber needs a conversation, not another attempt.

Testing any of this is unglamorous and entirely doable. Hold the message copy constant and vary one timing dimension for one decline family at a time — soft declines only, say, so hard declines do not pollute the read. Insufficient funds is the natural first subject because its mechanism is explicitly temporal: if your retries are landing before payday, moving them should move the recovery rate in a way no copy change ever could. Give the test enough failures to mean something; on a small base, a fortnight of declines is anecdote, not evidence, and the discipline is mostly in not peeking early and declaring victory.

Two traps wait for the impatient. The first is testing everything at once — new spacing, new emails, new sender name — and learning nothing except that something changed. The second is reading recovery rate alone. A timing change that recovers more payments while doubling attempts per recovery may be worse, not better: attempts cost fees and issuer goodwill. Record attempts per recovery alongside the rate, and unsubscribes or cancels that arrive inside the dunning window, because timing that bullies people into paying once and leaving is not recovery — it is churn with extra steps.

What you are really building, test by test, is a map of your own subscribers rather than the average subscriber in a benchmark post. Gym members paid on shift cycles, newsletter readers on salaries, course buyers on neither — the right retry calendar is a fact about a specific business, which is why copying a famous schedule so rarely works. A grounded reference on when to retry a failed payment can frame the options; only your own decline file can choose among them.

Start with the payday question. It is the cheapest test in billing, the answer is usually visible within one cycle, and it teaches the habit every other timing decision needs: the schedule works for the subscribers, or it does not work at all.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

TL;DR: Ondo launched seven investment-portfolio tokens on Solana for eligible non-US investors. Its reported $45 million in earlier portfolio TVL refers to Ethereum and BNB Chain, not Solana alone.

Ondo Finance has expanded its Intelligent Portfolios to Solana, bringing seven tokenized basket products to eligible investors outside the United States. The initiative places managed exposure to equities and ETFs in the same onchain setting that already hosts liquid trading and lending applications.

The seven-token rollout

Ondo Finance announced on October 29 that seven of its Intelligent Portfolios were available on Solana for eligible investors outside the United States. Each product combines exposures to a defined basket of tokenized equities or exchange-traded funds in one token, so a holder need not purchase and rebalance every underlying position individually.

The lineup covers income, growth and thematic strategies, including a Magnificent Seven and crypto-related portfolio and an artificial-intelligence leaders portfolio. Ondo said three of the strategies were developed by BlackRock for use by Ondo. The company also reported that the portfolio products previously launched on Ethereum and BNB Chain exceeded $45 million of total value locked within their first weeks; that figure is for those earlier deployments, not a Solana-only balance.

How portfolio exposure is packaged

The move extends tokenization beyond single instruments into packaged investment strategies. It may simplify how a customer obtains a diversified exposure and could give other financial applications a composable token rather than a basket of separate contracts. But the value of that packaging depends on portfolio construction, fees, tracking accuracy and market liquidity rather than just the convenience of one token.

Solana gains a new type of tokenized investment product, but the distinction between access and adoption remains important. Seven portfolio tickers can exist without seven deep secondary markets, and early total-value-locked figures from other networks should not be presented as Solana inflows. Users will also need to understand the portfolio’s exact composition and rules. A token that represents an investment strategy is not itself a claim that the strategy will outperform a conventional fund.

Who can use it and the risks

A portfolio strategy defines target exposures while smart contracts implement allocation and scheduled rebalancing rules. Users hold a single transferable onchain token that provides economic exposure to its basket of Ondo Stocks. The underlying tokens represent tokenized securities exposure and connect to traditional market liquidity, so blockchain transferability does not remove the legal, pricing and redemption mechanics of the represented assets.

Availability remains restricted to eligible users in permitted jurisdictions, specifically excluding the United States in the announcement. Tokens trading around the clock are not the same as underlying stock markets operating around the clock. Holdings can fluctuate, trading may be thin and a token’s price may diverge from its economic value under stressed conditions.

The next demand signals

Useful evidence will include Solana-specific assets under management, turnover, tracking performance, liquidity depth and integration with independent DeFi venues. Investors should read the precise product conditions and avoid interpreting references to BlackRock-developed strategies as a guarantee of performance or liquidity.

A pilot, framework or technical partnership should not be conflated with a live secondary market. Investors need to know whether assets have actually been issued, whether redemption is possible and where responsibility sits if records disagree.

For investors, the distinction matters because product news and actual changes to liquidity or demand are not interchangeable.

Why the development matters

The question for market participants is what Aave’s tokenized-equity lending on Base establishes today and what remains uncertain. An immediate reaction is not the same as evidence of a durable economic outcome.

The application demonstrates how programmable securities could gain uses beyond simple transfers. Combining equity exposure with on-chain lending, however, also connects traditional market schedules and corporate events to DeFi risk engines.

DeFi applications make financial rules executable through code, but code still depends on market inputs, transaction ordering and the availability of liquidity. A smart contract can follow its programmed conditions precisely even when those conditions produce an outcome a user did not expect.

From a market perspective, the first task is to separate the scale of the announcement from the scale of economic activity that has actually occurred. A company may report a large addressable market, an institutional name or a future capability, but those are not equivalent to settled transactions or recurring fee income. This is why the primary disclosure is a better starting point than a trading narrative attached to it.

Risks the headline does not resolve

None of this removes the need for independent assessment. The published evidence identifies a development, while its risk and long-term economics are still open to examination.

Borrowing against a tokenized stock lets an investor obtain liquidity while retaining market exposure to the share. That introduces leverage: if the value of collateral falls relative to the debt, a protocol may require repayment or liquidate some of the collateral.

Geographic eligibility, securities rights, market liquidity and oracle design matter. A continuously open loan market does not mean the underlying stock’s cash market is open or that token redemptions are instantaneous under every condition.

For developers, execution quality involves more than a quoted price or an advertised feature. Gas costs, settlement success, oracle quality, permissions and incident handling all shape whether an integration is safe and convenient in real markets.

The milestones to watch

The immediate checklist is relatively straightforward: look for verifiable implementation details and avoid interpreting a stated target as an outcome that has already occurred.

Watch whether additional equity collateral is approved and how the lending market handles holidays, sudden gaps in stock prices and unexpected liquidations. A useful product must keep those risks understandable to borrowers.

Investors also need to separate changes in infrastructure from changes in token demand. Revenue may accrue to a company, a protocol treasury or a service provider without necessarily creating a one-for-one benefit for holders of a related token. Market impact depends on adoption, competition and the design of whatever fees or incentives are involved.

A durable signal would be consistent follow-through: actual usage, transparent reporting and a credible explanation of risk. Short-term token-price moves can be driven by macroeconomic conditions or leverage and may say little about whether the product or initiative succeeds over months and years.

A yuan network that’s international enough, even without being dominant, could bypass any dollar-based sanctions the U.S. might impose in the future.

“Placing a respectable second, or even a consequential third, would suffice to break the dollar’s effective monopoly and constrain the United States’ ability to impose financial sanctions unilaterally, while saving China the effort and cost of sustaining currency supremacy,” she said.

“From Beijing’s perspective, the mission is to establish the renminbi as the Pepsi to the dollar’s Coca-Cola: never the market leader, but serviceable in a pinch and more than capable of keeping the dominant incumbent honest.”

(For what it’s worth, Pepsi has actually fallen further behind Coke in the cola wars and was dealt a major symbolic defeat recently when KFC said it would replace PepsiCo soft drinks with Coca-Cola’s products at its 3,700 U.S. restaurants.)

So far, China facilitates renminbi use among firms in more than a hundred countries and across a growing array of commodity markets. As of June, its Cross-Border Interbank Payment System had 1,829 participants who could send payments to more than 5,200 banks in 191 countries.

Global currency requirements

But that’s just a small fraction of what the U.S.-dominated system processes, Liu said, adding that a global currency also entails pricing, financing, risk transfer, and reinvestment.

Chinese exporters still invoice in dollars and convert them to yuan just before brining them into China. But Beijing is promoting “China prices,” or yuan benchmarks on domestic commodity exchanges and has succeeded with lithium, she noted.

The financing stage is gaining momentum too as Chinese banks have more than doubled their yuan lending to overseas borrowers between 2023 and mid-2026. The People’s Bank of China has also reached currency swap agreements with 32 foreign central banks and monetary authorities.

More modest steps have increased the yuan’s presence in risk transfer markets as state firms have begun settling cargo insurance in yuan. But reinvestment is perhaps the weakest link in the chain. A strong yuan ecosystem requires recipients to hold and reinvest their balances rather than convert them, Liu argued.

China has expanded overseas access to domestic stocks and bonds, but Beijing’s unpredictable capital controls remain a worry. In March, foreign investors held just 1.6% of the Chinese bond market versus 27% in the U.S. bond market.

“Without a far larger pool of renminbi assets that foreigners can freely buy and sell, the renminbi cannot come close to replacing the dollar,” she said.

But similar to how the “petrodollar” anchors the greenback’s role in global trade, the yuan can dominate trade in an essential good that China controls.

That could take the form of critical minerals, clean energy equipment, and electrical infrastructure used in green energy technology, Liu posited.

China has already established supremacy across much of the green supply chain and is promoting standards built around technologies Chinese-owned firms.

“If China consolidates its dominance over that industrial ecosystem, using its scale and cost advantages to prevent competitors from catching up, it could gain enough leverage to price those essential, hard-to-substitute exports in renminbi, creating the foundation for a ‘green yuan’ that might eventually replace the petrodollar,” she predicted.

Petroyuan

Meanwhile, the Iran war has raised doubts about the petrodollar, which goes back to a deal struck in 1974 when Saudi Arabia agreed to price its oil in dollars and invest surpluses in U.S. assets.

In exchange for Saudi Arabia recycling its dollars back into the U.S., Washington guaranteed the kingdom’s security, which also involved stationing troops in the region, providing advanced weapons, and ensuring free navigation in the Strait of Hormuz.

But America’s role in the Mideast looks vastly different today. Despite 40 days of punishing bombardment of Iran and round-the-clock protection of commercial ships, the U.S. still hasn’t fully restored traffic in the strait.

At the same time, Iran’s swarms of missiles and drones have inflicted significant damage on U.S. aircraft, radars and bases, while American air-defense systems have failed to completely protect Gulf allies’ critical energy infrastructure.

“Damage to Gulf economies could encourage an unwind in their foreign asset savings,” Deutsche Bank warned in March. “In this context, reports that the passage for ships through the Strait of Hormuz may be granted in exchange for oil payments in yuan should be closely followed. The conflict could be remembered as a key catalyst for erosion in petrodollar dominance, and the beginnings of the petroyuan.”

The question for market participants is what Hanwha Investment & Securities’ Chainlink partnership establishes today and what remains uncertain. An immediate reaction is not the same as evidence of a durable economic outcome.

Representing an asset on a blockchain is the relatively straightforward part of tokenization. Making that representation useful requires legal rights, reliable valuation, restrictions on eligible holders, custody and a clear process for settlement and corporate actions.

A strategic partnership does not establish that the platform is fully operational, that collateral has already changed hands or that tokenized instruments have universal legal recognition. Actual utility depends on market participants agreeing the same operational standards.

Institutions may use public, private or hybrid networks, and each choice creates different expectations about access, settlement finality and oversight. Interoperability attempts to connect those environments without pretending they have identical rules.

New Florida ‘Agrihood’ Development Bets on Organic Farming to Lure Homebuyers

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new masterplanned community in Northeast Florida will offer multiple organic farms to attract buyers who are more interested in fresh vegetables than the state's famed beaches.

Saratoga Springs, a new development from Freehold Communities, will eventually have about 4,500 homes within multiple communities on 2,400 acres of land.

Located in Green Cove Springs, outside of Jacksonville, the "agrihood" will contain numerous working farms, bringing sustainable agriculture, farm-to-table dining, farmers' markets, and all things farm-related to the community.

Agrihoods, which mix subdivision developments with agriculture, most often appear in communities with large, undeveloped tracts of land or existing agricultural zoning.

This combination of agricultural land and neighborhood must then be developed to include housing while still incorporating an agricultural component of the community, according to Freehold Communities.

Saratoga Springs in Clay County, FL is one of a growing number of "agrihood" masterplanned communities that centers on working farms. (Freehold Communities)

The first phase of the Saratoga Springs agrihood—a 55-plus community called Sagebrooke—began sales in August 2026.

The gated senior community is built by Ryan Homes, with eight single-family home designs ranging from 1,488 to 2,729 square feet.

The active adult community will contain the usual amenities—such as a resort-style pool and pickleball, tennis, bocce courts and a putting green—but also boasts a private working farm and vineyard, a farm share from harvested crops, and a farm store.

Here, the signature crop will be a muscadine vineyard featuring Florida’s native grape, which thrives in the heat and humidity.

Home prices in the development range from $349,990 to $556,990. A spokesperson for Freehold wouldn't reveal the number of sales thus far but says that "it's exciting to see the level of demand."

Meet the farm director

Carmen Eldridge, 40, cofounder with her husband, Tripp, of Convivial Foodscapes, is heading up the farm aspect of Saratoga Springs. The couple led farm planning on two other Freehold agrihoods—Arden, in Palm Beach County; and Carnes Crossroads in Goose Creek, SC.

The pair navigate the process for creating functional agrihoods—everything from vetting land purchases, to business planning and budgets, to irrigation needs, to farm design and infrastructure construction, as well as distribution models for getting the produce into the wider community.

"It's a movement that is catching on," she says of agrihoods.

According to an Urban Land Institute study, agrihoods are a growing trend, available in at least 27 states. Key agrihood features include conservation space, protected farmland, farm stands and markets, and edible landscaping.

Carmen and Tripp Eldridge break ground at agrihood Saratoga Springs in Clay County, FL in Jan. 2025. (Freehold Communities) Saratoga Springs will incorporate multiple working organic farms into its housing development. (Freehold Communities)

"People are drawn to the idea of living closer to where their food comes from," says Eldridge. "They like the wide open green spaces, and are into healthy eating and longevity."

"It's more appealing to live near an organic farm than a golf course," she adds, noting that a lot of parents want their children to know that "a carrot comes out of the ground, not from a bag in the grocery store."

The Saratoga Springs farms will be cultivated by professional farmers—not the residents. But residents will be able to pick or bring home farm produce round the clock, a perk included in the HOA fees. (They will not have private plots or gardens.)

Eldridge tells Realtor.com that her agrihood consultancy has been in brisk demand as sustainable farms become the latest trend in the masterplanned community world. But she asserts that farms go far beyond typical amenities such a pool, fitness center, or pickleball courts.

"A farm is social infrastructure, it's not just an amenity," she says. "It creates repeated, meaningful touch points between neighbors. A farm actively builds a community identity in a way that other amenities can’t replicate. That’s why it’s catching more attention now."

She counts Freehold as among the leaders in the agrihood trend.

"Some developers want to put in a community garden to check the box, like, got the dog park, got the pool, got the community garden, check," she says. "But they go all-in and do it really well."

Residents will have plenty of access to fresh grown produce, which is included in HOA dues. (Freehold Communities)

Becoming an agrihood consultant

Eldridge says farming is in her blood, as her maternal immigrant grandparents had been farmers in Spain.

"At the time, everyone was a farmer," she says. "There were no grocery stores. You farmed or you died."

She grew up with plenty of fresh produce from her family's backyard gardens.

"I didn't think much of it at the time," she says. "I was just told to go out back and pick some green beans for dinner."

When she informed her grandparents that she was going to study organic crop production in college, they weren't too pleased.

"They said, 'We moved here so you didn't have to be a farmer!'"

After graduating from the University of Florida with a degree in Political Science, focusing on Agriculture Policy and Organic and Sustainable Crop Production, Eldridge served as a Sustainable Agriculture Peace Corps volunteer in Panama, then founded a teaching farm at the University of North Florida.

Eventually, she and husband, Tripp, worked to set up the farms at Freehold's Arden agrihood, where they served as farm directors for five years; then they designed and built the farm at Carnes Crossroads.

Saratoga Springs will have an active senior community dubbed Sagebrooke that will boast the usual amenities such as this resort-style pool. (Freehold Communities)

The pair, who jokingly refer to themselves as "landless farmers," formed Convivial Foodscapes in 2020, and soon afterward came aboard the Saratoga Springs project.

Agrihoods, and the employment of local farmers to work the land, have been somewhat of an unexpected and steady income source for those in the notoriously difficult farming industry.

Farmers from the vicinity are also invited into the private community to sell produce and goods, offering a larger customer base for those who work the land on tight margins.

"Our farmer population is aging," she says, noting that agrihoods offer "a job for farmers with a living wage and health benefits."

Eldridge, who uses only organic farming techniques, takes great joy in people's reactions to fresh produce.

"I love when people's faces light up when they learn what a real tomato actually tastes like," she says. "I live for those moments."

Saratoga Springs will have social gardens where residents can bond over picking produce, included in HOA fees. (Freehold Communities)

But what most excites Eldrige about the concept of agrihoods and their growing popularity is the social aspects.

Farms offer diverse ways for communities to connect through food—picking produce, volunteering, helping to farm, attending workshops, hosting farm-to-table meals, or mingling at special events tied to fresh food or signature crops.

Eldridge, who often attends model home showings at Saratoga Springs, says that the buyer pool is very broad—everyone from young families to downsizing retirees—but they often have one thing in common: they are foodies at heart.

"There is something universally appealing about food," she says. "We all like to eat and we all have a food story."

"It really does bring people together."

Why United left JFK, and how the airline plans to return

United obviously has a huge hub at Newark Liberty Airport (EWR), and different airline executives have different takes as to the overlap in demand between Newark and Kennedy.

Through late 2015, United had a relatively small presence at JFK, with a majority of the service consisting of premium transcontinental flights to Los Angeles (LAX) and San Francisco (SFO), with a product that was branded as p.s. The airline operated these flights with specially configured, three cabin Boeing 757-200s.

However, in late 2015, the airline made the decision to end service at JFK, and instead consolidate flights at EWR. The airline was reportedly losing money on these flights, and just couldn’t compete with American and Delta, which both had major hubs there.

Shortly after that decision was made, Kirby joined United, before eventually being appointed CEO. For as long as he has been at United, Kirby has felt that leaving JFK was one of the carrier’s biggest strategic blunders. In 2021, United briefly returned to JFK, operating transcon routes with Boeing 767-300ERs. United returned to JFK in 2021, but it didn’t last long

Scott Kirby says Boeing and Airbus have quietly agreed not to develop new aircraft because neither faces enough competition to force its hand. I understand his frustration with the slow pace of aircraft development, but that is a substantial allegation, and the United Airlines CEO’s argument overlooks both an airplane his own airline ordered and the competition concerns he dismisses when discussing airline mergers.

Scott Kirby Says Boeing And Airbus Are Holding Back New Aircraft Development

I have already discussed Scott Kirby’s appearance on the Cheeky Pint podcast with John Collison and Ben Thompson. The full conversation is worth a listen, even if I disagree with some of his conclusions. As always, Kirby offers a remarkably candid look at how he thinks about the airline business.

One exchange that deserves a closer look concerns Boeing and Airbus, the lack of an all-new Boeing 757 replacement, and whether the aircraft manufacturers have become too comfortable with their position in the market.

Kirby Accuses Boeing And Airbus Of Quietly Agreeing Not To Build New Airframes

In the interview, Kirby argues that the Boeing–Airbus duopoly has weakened the incentive to invest in new aircraft. He says:

“I think the fundamental problem is Boeing and Airbus have a global duopoly, and they have quietly, not officially agreed, but they have quietly agreed: ‘Neither one of us will invest in new airframes.’”

He attributes that reluctance partly to investors and says he has spent more than two decades asking for a 757 replacement. His proposed remedy is another aircraft manufacturer to challenge the incumbents.

First, that is quite a serious accusation. Kirby does not present evidence of an agreement during this exchange. Two companies independently deciding that an expensive development program does not offer an attractive return is different from agreeing to avoid competing. I can accept that limited competition may reduce the urgency to invest. But that does not establish collusion.

What About The Airbus A321XLR?

There is also a rather large omission from this argument: Airbus has developed a functional replacement for the Boeing 757.

The A321neo family fills much of that commercial role, with the A321LR and especially the A321XLR extending it to longer routes. These are not identical aircraft with interchangeable capabilities in every operating condition and I concede that an A321XLR does not solve every requirement an airline might have for replacing both 757s and larger 767s.

But it is difficult to argue that Airbus simply refused to answer the 757 replacement question.

In fact, United announced its order for 50 A321XLRs in December 2019 specifically to begin replacing its Boeing 757-200 fleet. Its own announcement described the aircraft as an “ideal one-for-one replacement” for older aircraft on certain intercontinental routes.

Airbus advertises a range of up to 4,700 nautical miles for the XLR, opening longer routes with single-aisle economics (though it seems the actual range is much shorter…)

An aircraft does not have to be designed from scratch to represent innovation. Improving an existing platform’s efficiency and extending the routes it can economically operate can achieve the same goal. Kirby may want something substantially better–he mentioned the superior efficiency of delta wing design (a triangular wing shape) like the SR-71–I would welcome that too. But wanting a more ambitious airplane does not mean show collusion or that Airbus has not already offered a 757 replacement.

This debate also brought me back to two stories I wrote seven years ago.

In July 2019, I covered United’s push for clarity on Boeing’s proposed new midmarket airplane, commonly called the 797. I wrote:

“Boeing is still studying the business case for the NMA, but has shifted the bulk of its attention to the troubled 737 MAX program.”

That same story identified the A321XLR as Airbus’ answer to United’s replacement needs.

Two months later, I covered Delta’s interest in the proposed 797, including its substantial need to replace aging 757s and 767s. Boeing’s MAX crisis was already consuming attention and engineering resources. Meanwhile, Airbus was securing XLR orders.

As I put it then:

“But Boeing’s pain has been Airbus’ gain.”

That sounds like competition to me, even if Boeing failed to offer the product its airline customers wanted.

There is ample room to criticize Boeing’s priorities. But a company struggling with its existing aircraft programs may lack the resources or appetite to launch another enormously expensive one. It may also conclude that the potential market does not justify the investment.

Neither explanation requires a quiet agreement with Airbus.

United Airlines CEO Says Boeing And Airbus Need More Competition. What About Airlines?

Finally, one more thought on competition.

Kirby recognizes that when United has fewer suppliers to choose from, those suppliers may feel less pressure to deliver the products United wants. Another competitor could force them to work harder for his business.

I agree with that principle.

But when I apply similar reasoning to his proposed merger with American Airlines, Kirby wants us to focus on the benefits of a larger network and a better airline.

Aircraft manufacturing and airline operations have different economics. Adding a manufacturer is an immense undertaking, and combining airlines can create useful connections. I do not suggest that the two markets are identical.

Nevertheless, the underlying concern is common: customers lose bargaining power when credible alternatives disappear.

If United benefits from having another manufacturer compete for its aircraft orders, passengers can benefit from having American compete with United for their tickets. A larger route map does not eliminate that competitive pressure’s value.

Kirby is persuasive when explaining why he wants more choices as a customer. He is much less persuasive when explaining why we should accept fewer!

That said, I’d love to see Lockheed get back into the commercial airline business…coming soon, an L1011-800!

CONCLUSION

I would welcome a compelling new aircraft that advances what airlines can offer passengers. Kirby is right to push manufacturers to do better. But the A321XLR demonstrates that development has not stopped, and Boeing’s failure to launch the 797 does not establish an agreement to suppress innovation.

More fundamentally, Kirby understands why a customer should worry about too few competitors. I wish he would give that concern equal weight when the customer is buying a United Airlines ticket!

Inexpensive things to do in Alaska on a cruise

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you're used to cruising in the Caribbean, shore excursion prices on your Alaska cruise might surprise you.

Want to take a helicopter ride to the top of a glacier to enjoy a dogsled ride? It'll cost upward of $700 per person. Excited about an authentic Alaska fishing experience? That's a more than $400 tour. A nearly three-hour scenic train ride on the White Pass and Yukon Route Railroad starts at more than $150 per traveler.

Not only are tours so pricey because Alaska is a remote destination (where it costs more to fly in goods and materials), but also because Alaska is part of the U.S., where wages and tour insurance costs can be higher than in the islands. Plus, many Alaska excursions involve seaplanes, helicopters, specialized boats and other equipment that only add to the price of your onshore experience.

For many, an Alaska cruise is a once-in-a-lifetime trip, and many tours are worth the splurge. But you don't have to spend money hand over fist in every port. Whether you're trying to balance your budget with a few cheaper days or you simply don't have the resources to spend big every day of your vacation, you'll want to seek out cheap or free things to do in Alaska's cruise ports.

Luckily, you have plenty of options. I know because I've spent many a day exploring independently in Alaska's ports. Plus, I consulted with Alaska's tourism representatives to bring you a comprehensive list of free (and cheap) things to do in Alaska on a cruise.

Things to do in Skagway, Alaska, for free and cheap

The historic gold rush town of Skagway, Alaska. REINHARD PANTKE/TRAVEL ALASKA

Skagway is known for its gold rush history, but you don't need a pricey tour to immerse yourself in the town's storied past. Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park has four free museums open to visitors, including the Visitor Center Museum (with interactive exhibits), the Moore House Museum (former home of the first family to live in Skagway, with two restored Victorian-era rooms), the Jeff Smiths Parlour Museum (a former bar with a shady past) and the Mascot Saloon Museum (a restored gold rush saloon).

If you're fully embracing the old-timey vibe, you'll want to belly up to the bar at the Red Onion Saloon. Once, it was Skagway's signature bordello, but now it's the place to sling back an Alaska-brewed beer or snack on apps and pizzas named after former good-time girls. For a reasonable $20 a person, you can sign up for a Quickie Tour of the venue's supposedly haunted brothel museum. Ghost sightings don't cost extra!

One of my favorite things to do in Skagway is stretch my legs on a hike, and there are several trailheads within easy walking distance of your cruise ship. Options include the Upper and Lower Dewey Lake, Reid Falls and Gold Rush Cemetery, and Icy Lake and Upper Reid Falls trails. The National Park Service kindly lists its hikes by distance from Skagway's Historic District, as well as by level of difficulty. No guide is needed, and you don't have to pay to access the trails.

Free and cheap things to do in Juneau, Alaska

GOLDBELT TRAM/FACEBOOK

Window shopping is a popular activity along the main streets by Juneau's cruise docks, but you might be tempted to blow your budget on souvenirs. (Look for sales, especially during end-of-season sailings, if you want to buy gifts for less.)

Typically, you can pay $60 for an all-day pass up and down Juneau's Goldbelt tram*, or if you're in good shape, you can hike from your ship to the top of the tram on the Mount Roberts Trail for free. I did this hike on my recent Alaska cruise, and while it's not technical, it is steep and can be muddy after a rainstorm. Make lots of noise just in case bears are on the trail.

At the top, you can visit the Chilkat Theater to watch a free movie about the region's Tlingit people. If you're too tired to hike back down, you can get a ride back on the tram for $20.

However, the Goldbelt tram has been closed since late April 2026 for maintenance. Visitors can still go to the base of the tram to watch Tlingit artists carve totem poles, weave ceremonial robes and perform cultural dances for no charge.

Other hikes accessible from your ship include the Gold Creek Flume Trail and the Perseverance Trail.

If a stroll through downtown is more your speed, self-guided tours of Juneau come free of charge. The Juneau Voices project features audio installations at 11 spots around town where you can learn about the city through the stories of Native Alaskans. Or grab a map from a tourist welcome center and follow this walking tour to see the city's highlights.

A totem pole in Juneau, Alaska. EYFOTO/GETTY IMAGES

You can also find a PDF guide to Juneau's totem poles online.

If you prefer to get your history and culture indoors (not a bad plan in Alaska, given the temperamental climate), Juneau offers a few museums with modest admission fees that are worth checking out.

The Juneau-Douglas City Museum offers a mix of permanent and temporary exhibits on the area's history and art. Admission is $7, but you might need a cab if you're not up for a 20-minute walk. The museum also runs a free walking tour of the Alaska State Capitol Building.

Also, a 20-minute walk from the port is the Alaska State Museum, where you can learn about the cultures of the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian peoples. Admission is $14. For more information on Native Alaskans and their cultures, visit the Sealaska Heritage Institute's Walter Soboleff Building ($10 admission) and its gift shop, which features local art. Don't miss the signature totem pole and handcrafted clan house.

If you're keen for a scenic uphill walk, head up Basin Road to the Last Chance Mining Museum ($5) to learn about the gold rush in a historic mining building.

*At the time of writing, Goldbelt tram is closed but has plans to reopen soon. Check availability and prices before you plan.

Cheap and free things to do in Ketchikan, Alaska, from a cruise ship

A clan house in Saxman. TRAVEL ALASKA

Ketchikan is best known for its Creek Street Historic District, built on a wooden boardwalk above the salmon-filled Ketchikan Creek. Stroll for free by its historic buildings, shops and restaurants, or pay $10 to stop into Dolly's House for a glimpse into the city's former red-light district.

Not far from Creek Street, the Tongass Historical Museum and Totem Heritage Center immerses you in the area's history for a modest $6 fee (children and military are free). From there, you can embark on a self-guided, 1 1/2-mile Ketchikan Salmon Walk to learn about the fish's impact on the town (and maybe even spot some, in season, at the Salmon Ladder). The walk will take you past the Totem Heritage Center (also $6, or get a combo ticket to both museums for $9), with its collection of 19th-century totem poles rescued from nearby islands.

If you're feeling adventurous, you can skip the tour and head out to the Saxman Native Village on your own. It's 2 miles south of Ketchikan, so it's walkable — but for a few bucks, you can take a city bus there instead. It costs just $5 to wander the park on your own and see the 25 replica totem poles, as well as Native carvers working on new poles using traditional techniques.

Things to do in Icy Strait Point, Alaska, for free or cheap

ICY STRAIT POINT/FACEBOOK

Icy Strait Point is a manufactured cruise port near the town of Hoonah. It was created to offer everything cruisers could possibly want in a port, so all the activities are planned to get you to part ways with your hard-earned cash. That said, there are still plenty of things to do in Icy Strait Point for free or cheap.

A free gondola ride transports visitors from one cruise pier area to the other, but if the line is long or you're afraid of heights, you can walk the same path on nature trails instead. Keep your eyes out for wildlife.

The port has tons of beach access, so you can wander Icy Strait's shores, stop for scenic photos and look for marine life free of charge. At the Hoonah Cannery building, you can browse the Hoonah Packing Co. Cannery Museum exhibits interspersed among the shops, or get tickets to the Native dance show for $20 per person at the on-site ticket booth.

From the cannery, it's a 1 1/2-mile (roughly 30-minute) walk to Hoonah, an actual town where many of Icy Strait Point's workers live and home to Alaska's largest Tlingit village. You can also take an affordable $10 shuttle to town, where you can admire the totem poles and sip a local beer at Icy Strait Brewing.

Things to do in Sitka, Alaska, for free or cheap

The Sheldon Jackson Museum. JOHN ELK/GETTY IMAGES

Sitka offers a compelling mix of Native Alaskan and Russian history and culture, and a self-guided tour of the key sites in town won't cost you much (if anything at all).

You can get a free tour of the Russian Bishop's House, an example of 19th-century architecture during Russia's colonization of Sitka. Baranof Castle State Historic Site (Castle Hill) was occupied throughout the centuries by Tlingit people and later by Russian settlers, and it was the site chosen for the transfer of Alaska from Russia to the U.S. You can check out the views and the informative signs for free.

St. Michael Cathedral National Historic Landmark is an exact replica of the first Russian Orthodox church in the Americas. Because the site has an active congregation, visitors can view its collection of 17th-century religious artifacts for a limited time each day.

Museums with small admission fees include the Sitka History Museum (featuring exhibits on local history) and the Sheldon Jackson Museum (showcasing a collection of Alaska Native artifacts). If exploring old cemeteries is your thing, you'll want to stop at the more than 200-year-old Sitka Russian Cemetery, with gravestones made from the ballast of Russian ships.

The Alaska Raptor Center in Sitka. JOHN ELK/GETTY IMAGES

The Sitka National Historical Park's visitor center and grounds (with walking trails and totem pole installations) are open to all, free of charge. It's a scenic spot for an easy nature walk, or you can come inside to learn more about Sitka's Russian and Tlingit cultures or join a ranger program.

You don't need a tour to gawk at eagles, hawks, owls and falcons at the Alaska Raptor Center. Admission is $16, though it is a bit of a walk from downtown. Watch injured birds relearn how to fly, and take a stroll through the center's forested grounds.

Finally, you can attend various dance performances at affordable ticket prices. The Alaska Storytellers dance show ($15) celebrates traditional and modern Russian dance, while the New Archangel Dancers' performances highlight Russian folk dances. Or, step into a Tlingit clan house to enjoy the music, costumes and choreography of the Naa Kahidi Tlingit Dancers.

Bottom line

You don't need to spend a fortune on tours to explore Alaska's culture and history or enjoy its natural splendor. You'll find plenty of free things to do in port on an Alaska cruise. However, I do encourage you to budget for a few tours, whether it's to travel beyond the port area into Alaska's wilderness or to immerse yourself in an activity, such as dog sledding, kayaking, fishing, or whale or bear watching. You won't regret spending the money on amazing experiences and memories.

Canada’s wildfires could cause more than $2 trillion in climate damage

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ask what a wildfire costs and the answer usually comes back as firefighting bills and burned timber.

In a new projection of Canada’s wildfire costs through 2100, firefighting, lost timber and recreation account for only about 2% of the estimated combined costs.

Nearly all the rest comes from the climate damage associated with carbon dioxide released by the fires.

In what researchers describe as a realistic worst-case scenario, that running total passes $2.1 trillion Canadian by 2100.

Sian Kou-Giesbrecht, an assistant professor of resource and environmental management at Simon Fraser University, led the work.

Her co-authors were Renée Hall at SFU and two scientists at Environment and Climate Change Canada. Their study is a simulation, not a measurement.

A computer model of Canada’s forests, weather and fires ran from 1850 to 2100 under three possible futures.

Then it ran again with the climate frozen at pre-industrial conditions. The difference between the two runs represents the modeled contribution of climate change.

“Societal and economic costs of wildfire are highly dependent on the actions that we take to mitigate climate change right now,” Kou-Giesbrecht said.

Lower warming limits damage

One future keeps warming under 3.6°F (2°C), the Paris Agreement target.

There, climate change had no detectable effect on how much of Canada burned, how many people were exposed, or what it cost by the end of the century. All of those stayed close to recent levels.

The two hotter futures, with warming of 5.4 to 7.2°F (3 to 4°C) and 7.2 to 9°F (4 to 5°C), looked different.

By the end of the century, climate change had roughly doubled the area burned each year in the middle path and tripled it in the hottest.

That is an extra 14.6 million acres (5.9 million hectares) a year in the middle path and 19.5 million acres (7.9 million hectares) in the hottest.

For scale, the record 2023 season burned 37 million acres (15 million hectares), seven times the 1986 to 2022 average.

“In somewhat good climate news, we don’t think we’ll hit a rise of 4 or 5 C, largely because of green energy developments happening primarily outside of Canada,” Kou-Giesbrecht said.

“A global temperature rise between 3 and 4 C is a more realistic worst-case scenario.”

Carbon dwarfs the direct costs

In that middle path, climate change added about $866 million a year in direct costs by the end of the century, almost all of it lost timber.

Over 2015 to 2100, the direct total came to about $46 billion. Every figure is in 2025 Canadian dollars, and future costs were discounted at 2% a year, the rate Canada’s Treasury Board uses.

Carbon emissions account for far greater estimated damage.

Canada already prices the worldwide damage from a tonne of carbon dioxide, from crop losses to sea level rise to illness, as the social cost of carbon.

Applied to the extra wildfire emissions, climate change added $42.2 billion a year by 2080 to 2100 in the middle path and $50.2 billion in the hottest.

Over the century, those come to $2.09 trillion and $2.43 trillion.

Together, the direct costs and carbon damage amount to approximately $2.14 trillion by 2100.

That damage is spread across the whole planet, because carbon dioxide warms it wherever the gas is released.

Exposure more than triples

The team counted a person as exposed when a fire burned at least 1% of a map square about 31 miles (50 kilometers) across, roughly the distance smoke studies use.

This measure reflects proximity to wildfire activity, rather than confirmed exposure to smoke or direct danger.

By the end of the century, climate change added 72,000 exposed people a year in the middle path and 178,000 in the hottest, more than triple the number without it.

The burden also shifts between generations. Someone born in the 1960s had a 9% chance of living through a wildfire exposure.

For someone born in the 2020s, the model estimates that chance at 14% in the low-warming future, 25% in the middle path and 22% in the hottest.

The exposed places were not a cross-section of the country. About 78% of the populated areas that fires reached were primarily Indigenous communities, which make up 17% of populated areas overall.

That held in every future the team ran.

Firefighting and denser settlement did push the other way, cutting burned area by 14% to 26% depending on the path, but the climate effect was far larger.

What the model leaves out

Kou-Giesbrecht said the numbers are likely conservative, and the paper lists why.

The model captures long-term trends, not single extreme seasons like 2023, when more than 200 communities and some 232,000 people were evacuated.

Under the hottest path, an average year by 2100 approaches that season’s total.

The direct costs count only firefighting, timber and a single year of lost recreation in protected areas.

Health care, evacuations, damaged homes and roads, and business interruption are all left out, and so is smoke that travels farther than 31 miles, though Canadian smoke has reached Europe.

A lower discount rate would substantially increase the estimated cumulative costs.

Carbon that may not come back

One more gap matters for the carbon total. The model assumes forests grow back after they burn, and a growing body of field evidence shows boreal forests failing to regenerate after severe fires.

The authors argue that the social cost of carbon itself should include wildfire damage, which it currently omits. A recent United States study estimated the increase at 74%.

How much carbon burned boreal forests can eventually recover remains a major uncertainty.

If more forests fail to regenerate, the long-term climate damage could be substantially greater than these projections suggest.

The full study was published in the journal Environmental Research: Climate.

Why some Amazon communities welcome the oil companies threatening their water

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Oil drilling in the Amazon is often portrayed as a battle between Indigenous communities and powerful energy companies.

But along Ecuador’s Lower Napo River, some communities facing the expansion of a major oil project were negotiating for something unexpected: clean drinking water.

They already depended on a polluted river, and they feared that more drilling would make conditions worse. Yet the company responsible for the new extraction also offered something the government had failed to provide.

Julie Dayot spent eight months in 2016 and 2017 living with Quichua communities while the Ishpingo-Tambococha-Tiputini oil project, known as ITT, expanded through their stretch of the river.

She began the work as a doctoral student in Oxford’s Department of International Development and is now at the University of Exeter‘s Environment and Sustainability Institute.

“When I first met people from Quichua communities, I was surprised that many wanted the oil company to come,” Dayot said.

“I wanted to understand why. Over time, I saw how the costs and benefits of oil extraction are spread unevenly over space and time.”

The river came polluted

The Napo is the villages’ tap. Families drink from it, cook with it, wash in it and brew chicha, the fermented yucca drink, from it.

By the time the project reached them, residents already considered the river polluted by sewage, agricultural runoff and earlier oil drilling upstream.

Older residents still talked about a 1987 spill from the Texaco era, when, as two of them put it, “no one would give us even a gallon of water.”

Fish were getting scarcer, and so was bush meat.

In Vicente Salazar, Dayot found that monthly household cash income ranged from $95 to $140. Residents struggled to afford improvements such as metal roofs and motorized canoes.

These pressures made the company’s financial support attractive long before drilling reached the communities.

Oil became the provider

The state operator, Petroamazonas, committed about $5.5 million in social compensation across the six communities inside the project’s zone of influence, on top of cash payments and jobs.

The funding was intended to support water systems, electricity, transport, schooling, training and agricultural projects.

One village of 480 people negotiated about $1.25 million in projects, while another community of 95 people secured $155,000.

Most of those are things a government normally provides. The company also paid for the health center in Tiputini and donated medicine and fuel for emergencies.

Several people told Dayot that local officials steered communities toward asking the company rather than the municipality.

An anthropologist in the company’s community relations office summed it up for her. “We (also) represent the state, which has been absent here,” he said.

“One particularly striking example is drinking water,” Dayot said.

“Several communities therefore asked the oil company for drinking water systems, both to address an existing problem and because they expected the new extraction to further contaminate the river.”

Oil extraction threatened the communities’ water supply, yet the industry also offered a way to protect it. That contradiction challenges the usual narrative of Indigenous resistance to drilling.

Harm travels, money stays

Only six communities were entitled to compensation, even though pollution could spread beyond the project’s boundaries and affect villages that received nothing.

In Vicente Salazar, just outside the project line, residents called themselves “affected” months before the company arrived. They meant affected by exclusion.

“We are affected,” one man said, “because they don’t give us jobs.”

When the company turned up in January 2017 to announce a new seismic survey, it did so during the monthly village work day, between the beer and the volleyball. There was no outcry.

One woman told Dayot the company needed to hurry up because she had no money for trousers. A man she asked about a vote shook his head.

“It’s not like we are going to oppose,” he said. “What it’s all about is negotiating what we wanna ask.”

When Dayot returned in 2023, she found that all six communities still supported oil extraction, despite the environmental risks.

The cost of acceptance

Dayot’s research focused on a small number of communities along one stretch of the Lower Napo River. Whether the same patterns exist elsewhere remains unclear.

The future of the ITT oil field remains uncertain. Ecuadorians voted in August 2023 to end extraction in Yasuní National Park, but production continued into September 2026, despite the referendum.

The study raises questions that extend beyond the fate of one oil field.

For communities along the Lower Napo River, accepting drilling was not simply a choice between environmental protection and economic development.

It was also a way to secure clean water, jobs, and essential services that had long been out of reach.

“We need to think beyond the decisions themselves to the uneven conditions under which acceptance happens,” Dayot said.

Her findings expose a troubling contradiction: Communities facing the environmental risks of oil extraction may depend on the same industry to provide the basic necessities they need.

The full study was published in the journal Geoforum.

American Airlines quoted $496 for one passenger to fly from Miami to Charlotte, but just $155 per person when booking two passengers on the same flight. That means two tickets cost $310 total—$186 less than buying just one. Traveling alone more than tripled the per-person fare. That is one example from a new investigation into higher fares for solo travelers, and it raises an obvious question: why should traveling alone carry such a steep penalty?

American Airlines Wants $496 For One Ticket, But Just $310 For Two

Thrifty Traveler has published a revealing follow-up on airlines charging solo travelers more. His team examined 5,000 nonstop domestic one-way flights, sampling 1,000 each on American Airlines, United Airlines, Delta Air Lines, Alaska Airlines, and Southwest Airlines.

American charged solo passengers more on 33.5% of the flights sampled, compared with 10.6% on United. The researchers found no such pricing on the flights sampled from Delta, Alaska, or Southwest.

The Miami–Charlotte example is particularly striking: $496 for one passenger versus $155 per person when searching for two, both for main cabin economy. Two tickets cost $186 less than one!

American did not respond to the publication’s requests for comment. United declined to comment.

Why Does Adding A Passenger Lower The Price?

Thrifty Traveler traced the pricing to fare rules requiring a traveling companion to qualify for certain cheaper tickets. Search for one passenger and those fares disappear. Airlines have been distinguishing between customers willing to pay more for decades through advance-purchase requirements, minimum stays, and restrictions on changes. But requiring another passenger is a particularly suspect way to accomplish that.

Presumably, the calculation is that someone traveling alone is more likely to be a business traveler with an expense account (all we can do is infer that since AA and UA declined to comment). Even if that assumption works across a large pool of bookings, it does not necessarily say anything about the person actually buying a ticket. I frequently travel alone and still care very much about what I pay!

Traveling Alone Should Not Require An Explanation

A passenger visiting an elderly parent, taking a solo vacation, or flying to meet a spouse already at the destination needs one seat. Why should that person have to produce a companion to unlock a substantially lower fare?

The airline can describe this as a discount for traveling together. From the perspective of someone who needs only one ticket, the result is the same: a higher price for the same transportation. Even when the example is not as egregious as the AA example above, it still seems particularly punitive.

How counterintuitive that a customer entering the correct number of passengers must now wonder whether asking for an additional seat would lower the total bill. We already spend enough time comparing basic economy restrictions, baggage charges, and seat fees. Now checking whether you are being penalized for traveling alone becomes another part of the booking process.

Check The Price For Two Before Booking For One

When shopping for a solo trip, try repeating the search for two passengers. Compare the per-person price for the same flight and fare product.

If an airline wants substantially more money because I am traveling alone, booking Mr. EXTRA/SEAT may make sense…or more likely, give me another reason to book elsewhere.

CONCLUSION

Airlines are entitled to pursue revenue, and I do not expect every seat on a flight to sell for the same amount. But a pricing system that quotes $496 for one ticket and $310 for two deserves scrutiny…maybe not regularity scrutiny, but at least consumer scrutiny. The frustrating lesson is that searching for exactly what you need may conceal the much lower price available to someone bringing a companion…

For real estate investors and home flippers, one thing is key for remaining successful in a turbulent market: patience.

At least, that’s what South Florida investor, house flipper, and licensed real estate agent Branden Rivero thinks.

Rivero founded his company, Prop Hunters, in 2014. The Miami-Dade-based company has completed hundreds of flips, in addition to building and selling single-family homes.

“I started out just wholesaling, getting affordable properties and assigning the contracts until I was able to build some capital,” Rivero tells Realtor.com®. “I started flipping just one or two houses, and now we usually do about seven to 10 at a time.”

Prop Hunters flipped 25 homes last year, according to Rivero, but they’ve completed between 30 and 40 homes some years. This year’s gross profit is around $200,000 so far, accounting for both flips and new homes.

In his 12 years in business, Rivero has learned to go with the flow of the sometimes volatile Florida real estate market. Looking ahead 90 days to six months is a vital part of being prepared for anything.

“Everything is based on the market. Sometimes you can go full throttle when things are good,” says Rivero. “Then, once you see more turbulent times coming, you become more cautious, you underwrite a little more aggressive—you kind of have to play it by ear.”

Although Miami’s current market feels slow to some, Rivero is optimistic, saying that the real estate market is simply returning to the pre-pandemic norms.

“People are just used to the COVID rush, but I feel like it’s normal, just how it was in 2017, 2018, and 2019. Homes are taking a month, month and a half to sell,” he notes. “Between 2020 and 2024, a house wouldn’t last a week on the market. So, compared to that, it’s a big difference, but I just feel like it’s back to normal.”

In 2018, the median number of days on the market was 63, according to the Realtor.com March 2018 Monthly Housing Trends Report. As of the week ending Oct. 24, 2026, the median days on the market nationally was 64.

Miami comes in slightly slower, at a median of 83 days on the market as of October 2026. That number has decreased from both last month and last year, pushing the number toward the national norm.

These numbers are vastly different from the post-pandemic boom, when, in 2021, the typical home spent just 32 days on the market nationally.

A Realtor.com international report from June 2018 places Miami’s median listing price at $395,000, in line with October 2026’s national median listing price of $414,000. Again, Miami itself comes in a bit higher in 2026 numbers, with the median listing price sitting at $620,000.

One major difference between 2018 and 2026: mortgage rates. In 2018, the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage was 4.7%. This week, the average 30-year mortgage rate is 7.2%.

While many aspects of the market are settling closer to pre-pandemic levels, mortgage rates are responsible for a major shift in affordability.

When Jimmy Chin was in his twenties, living out of a 1989 Subaru Loyale somewhere between Yosemite and the Tetons, he kept trying to explain climbing to his father. His father, a librarian at Mankato State University in Minnesota, would listen, and then say: “Our language is 5,000 years old, and there is no word for what you do.”

Chin has spent the decades since building a vocabulary for it anyway, as a climber, a photographer, and the Oscar-winning director of Free Solo featuring Alex Honnold, and his latest film, Everest: The Other Side, which documents Jim Morrison and Hilaree Nelson’s attempt to climb and ski down the North Face of the world’s tallest mountain.

His new book, Rules to Live By (Celadon), presents other people’s words, but is also part memoir. It’s built from ten conversations with people who have lived on their own terms: Yvon Chouinard, Yo-Yo Ma, Simone Biles, Ridley Scott, Kelly Slater, Annette Bening, Lynsey Addario, Kris Tompkins, Alex Honnold, and Bill King, a retired command master chief of SEAL Team 6. Earlier this month, I sat down with Chin onstage at the Bell House in Brooklyn. It was his first book event, and he confessed he hadn’t reread the book since turning it in. “There’s a reason I’m a filmmaker,” he told the crowd. “Writing is really, really difficult.” What followed covered violin practice, an undisclosed military base, and a missing sock. “If you’re going to do it, do it well”

OUTSIDE: You write that you’re “the only son of an only son of an only son.” That sounds like a lot of pressure.

Jimmy Chin: I started violin when I was three, and it became two hours of practice a day. Then martial arts, two hours a day. Then homework. That was my weekdays, and it was all I knew. There’s a scene in the book where you’re practicing violin, staring out the window at the forest. When did the forest win?

Chin: Anytime I did well in school, my reward was running off into the woods. There were no rules out there. My parents were librarians, so I always had a lot of books, and later, when they asked how I ended up doing this, I said, “It’s your fault. You gave me all these books.” I found climbing in college, and it was the first thing that felt like a calling. Growing up, I literally thought there were three jobs: lawyer, doctor, or professor. So after graduation I told my parents I’d take one year off, get it out of my system, and then pursue my real career. Which was a total lie. Chin: My sister, who went to Stanford and worked at Yale for 18 years, was my huge advocate. I’d ask her how Mom was doing, and she’d say, “Mom just keeps saying, ‘I raised a homeless man.” With my dad, I deeply wanted him to understand me. People see the films and photographs and ask about the greatest risk I’ve ever taken. It definitely wasn’t a mountain. It was being in my early twenties, full of doubt. Chin: I dropped violin after high school and picked up the guitar. The furthest thing I could think of from a concerto in D minor was covering Bob Marley. The whole band was classically trained kids. Everybody was technically skilled, so we could play whatever we wanted.

Chin: [Laughs.] No…we weren’t that good.

How did you choose the ten people in the book?

Chin: My documentaries take four, five, six, seven years. You have to bleed for them. You can only make so many films in a lifetime, and there are so many people I admire who I’ll never make a film about. I wanted the book to be a constellation: totally different backgrounds, totally different careers. After these extraordinary lives, what have you discovered really matters? An Anomaly Among Anomalies

You almost didn’t make Free Solo.

Chin: After Meru, producers kept asking what was next. I mentioned to my friend Evan Hayes, who became a producer on Free Solo, that I’d been thinking about a documentary about Alex. So I called him: “Good news, the film’s green-lit.” He said, “Great, because I’m thinking about soloing El Cap.” I said, “No. Not making that film.” I literally called Evan and said, “I’m out. Done.”

Why?

Chin: Among his friends, none of us would even talk about that idea. We didn’t want to put it in the ether. We’d seen the trajectory. He soloed Moonlight Buttress, which was outrageous. Then he soloed Half Dome. We were talking about breaking speed records on Half Dome, not free soloing it. It wasn’t hard to see what came after that. We just didn’t want to say it out loud. And then he dropped it in my lap. My fear was obvious: What if filming him causes him to fall? That’s not something I could live with.

What changed your mind?

Chin: He was going to do it anyway. I knew Alex really well. I took him on his first international expedition, and we’d done so much together. And I trusted him. Number one, we’re his friends first. Second, we’re climbers; you think like a climber before you pull out the camera. Third, we’re filmmakers. The film’s needs can never supersede Alex’s needs.

Chin: Because it’s just not true. He says, “I feel fear. I’ve always felt fear.” One of the reasons he started soloing was that he was so shy he was too scared to ask people to climb with him. So he climbed by himself. His process was to step outside his comfort zone a little at a time, in a regimented, disciplined way, until the things that scared him were well within it. Chin: Those are really personal decisions. It’s impossible to justify to a child that their parent died in the mountains doing what I do. I don’t know if there’s a right answer. I’ve lost a lot of friends in the mountains, some of them parents. After my friend Hilaree Nelson died, Bill King wrote to me, and I wrote back: “The mountains give us so much life, yet they take so much life away. It never makes sense, but we understand.” He quoted that line in his retirement speech. So it’s not an excuse. I believe your kids want you alive physically, and alive as yourself. How do you tell fear that’s protecting you from fear that’s psyching you out?

Chin: Fear can sharpen your senses or paralyze you. When things are going sideways, panic never serves you. So you break it down: How much of this is perceived risk, and how much is actual risk? That exercise alone brings you back into the present. You set the perceived risks aside, focus on what’s real, and mitigate it. Then you make a plan and execute.

The Question at the Undisclosed Location

I love how all Bill King wanted to know was why you turned around 100 meters from the summit of Meru.

Chin: I got a surprise call from SEAL Team 6 asking me to come talk at the base. They gave me a date, a time, and an airport. I got on a plane, they flew me to an undisclosed location, took my phone. I hadn’t been nervous speaking in years, but these guys roll in and size you up. Someone told me every member of Team 6 had watched Meru. When I finished, a guy in the back cut to the chase: “How did you decide to turn around 100 meters from the summit?” I knew the entire reason I was there was to answer that question. That was Bill.

When you’ve put years into an objective and it’s within sight, and things aren’t right, it takes extraordinary discipline to walk away. On Meru we had seven days of food and were on the wall for 19. We crossed so many of our own lines, deliberately. You set a line, reach it, evaluate, set another. The more you do that, the more invested you are, and the harder it is to turn around.

You and the SEALs share a no-asshole rule. Tell me about that. 

Chin: I asked Bill what trait gets people through training. He didn’t flinch: “Humor.” If a guy is getting his ass handed to him and he’s still cracking jokes, they’re not worried about him. It’s the same in the mountains. Humor changes the environment and brings you back to good, rational decisions. My selection process isn’t as serious as theirs, but these are the people holding a rope for you. You can’t have people who only think about themselves. And you can feel it. In conversation, it’s “I, I, I, me.”

Yvon’s Wisdom

Yvon Chouinard is the closest thing you have to a guru. He tells you, “It’s so easy to complicate your life. It’s so much harder to simplify it.” Have you managed it?

Chin: On expeditions, yes. That’s part of why I love them: the best version of me comes out. We’re brutal about simplifying, because every ounce counts. Everything is about efficiency, even how much stress you allow, even your heart rate. I can stay really calm in the worst situations. But if I can’t find my matching sock at home, it’s all over. In the mountains, when things are going sideways, I’m like, oh, this is casual.

You have kids. Do you worry about whether the wild places you’ve spent your life in will still be there for them?

Chin: It’s what the last pages of the book are about. I think of it with Yvon on one end and Kris Tompkins on the other. They’ve both had a tremendous impact on my life, and I’ve talked with both of them about this a lot.

Yvon always says, “Everybody has different tools. Whatever tool you have in your toolbox, whatever you’re good at, that’s what you should contribute.” It’s not only activism. His example is that if you’re an accountant, there are a ton of grassroots organizations that need accountants. So you ask: What can I contribute? Contribute what you have.

And what’s in your toolbox?

Chin: Storytelling, and a platform. That’s why I made Wild Life after Free Solo. I had the chance to finance a film about conservationists, which I probably wouldn’t have had otherwise. It’s about Doug and Kris Tompkins, who preserved something like 17 million acres over a 25-year dream of creating national parks. I’ve brought my kids down there. Those are the things I hope will make a difference: telling these stories, and putting them in a book. What Yvon and Kris have to say is so much more compelling than what I have to say about it.

The forest sounds empty, but I know it’s not. Earlier today, I’d walked along the lakeshore and seen hoof prints, a cougar track, and coyote scat.

The trees have all turned black beneath the night sky. The only light comes from the full moon creeping through clouds. I’m deep in Washington’s Gifford National Forest and it’s so quiet I can hear my own heartbeat.

No creature is giving its location away by making noise—but we certainly aren’t alone as we stand on the edge of a lake, its waters as dark as midnight. I look at the shadows between trees and wonder what’s hiding in the forest.

The Gifford Pinchot National Forest, which the group frequents on their hunts for Bigfoot (Photo: Emily Halnon)

Beside me, Tanner Hoskins raises a thermal scope to his eye and scans the trees to look for heat signatures. He pauses as he sweeps to the far corner of the lake.

“I think I’ve got something moving,” he says.

For the last six years, Hoskins and a group of friends have been searching the Pacific Northwest’s wilderness for an answer to that very question: What else is out there? 

And I’m tagging along for a weekend to see what happens when I go looking for evidence of the unknown.

About two miles away, as the crow flies, a dispersed campsite is tucked into a grove of pine trees.

On a July night in 2020, decades-long buddies Kelly Stolp and Mike Palagi sat around a fire with Stolp’s sons and Palagi’s fiancée. Flames licked the black sky as they laughed into the night. It was a familiar scene for the friends, who met in the Army in 2005.

Stolp retired to his tent while the fire was still crackling. Deep in the night, he heard a loud, guttural howl—it sounded like a car engine trying to turn over. Then, footsteps. Slow and steady, getting closer to his tent. They seemed too loud to be human. More stomp than step. He could feel each footfall hit the earth. But he knew, without a hair of doubt, they were coming from a two-legged creature.

Stolp grew up on a 35,000-acre ranch in Montana. He’s hunted for decades and recreated outside since he could walk. He went on his first backpacking trip at the age of 12. He’s an avid outdoorsman and familiar with all of the wildlife that roams the West. A grizzly bear once came into his camp. But in that moment, he knew he wasn’t hearing a bear, nor a cougar, elk, or coyote.

Stolp listened as the creature approached his tent, brushing up against the huckleberries just feet away. It was only 50 degrees, but he was covered in sweat. Then, he heard the footsteps disappearing up the dry creekbed that winds past the campsite.

The next morning, he told Palagi, “Something came through camp last night.” It was large and two-legged, he went on. Palagi laughed, said he didn’t hear anything, and pointed at the empty bottle of whiskey as an explanation. Stolp dropped the topic for the rest of the day.

He could feel each footfall hit the earth. But he knew, without a hair of doubt, they were coming from a two-legged creature.

But at 3 A.M. that night, Stolp heard the footsteps again. Thump. Thump. Thump. The same guttural howl was now coming from two different spots.

He sat up and said, “Mike. It’s back.”

He heard a gun racking, then: “Kelly, I’m up.”

“Two legs or four?”

Palagi didn’t hesitate: “Bipedal, man.”

“How big?”

“Really fucking big.”

They rolled out of their tents. Stolp shouted, “We’re armed, show yourself.”

Palagi and Stolp both trained as military snipers and knew how to clear a space. They moved through camp, weapons ready. They found nothing, but the two men, who don’t scare easily, passed the rest of the night in their cars.

“I’ve spent my entire life outdoors and encountered everything the wilderness has to offer,” said Stolp. “But I’d never slept in my truck, not even when a grizzly came through my camp.”

They tried to recreate the stomping sound in the morning. They were not small men. Palagi played football in Montana as a defensive lineman, and Stolp looks like he could have been on a team, too. But even when Palagi jumped with his full body weight, he couldn’t come close to what they’d heard.

Palagi watches the fire at camp (Photo: Emily Halnon)

Stolp headed to the Shriners Children’s Hospital in Portland the next Monday, where he worked as an electrician alongside Palagi, a maintenance engineer. He pulled his coworker, a security guard named Hoskins, aside.

“But you have to promise you won’t tell anyone,” he told Hoskins. He knew how ridiculous the story sounded. He didn’t want anyone to think he believed in Bigfoot.

But Hoskins was more of a believer. While he was growing up, his great-grandmother had warned him about “hidebehinds,” a large, mysterious creature that was blamed for the disappearance of loggers in Idaho. Stolp’s story, when added to ones told by his great-grandmother, made him wonder if the Pacific Northwest was home to similar creatures. Hoskins swore he wouldn’t say a thing. Then he went home and built a website for the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search, telling anyone who found it about his friend’s story—and inviting others to share their own.

Tales of large, mysterious, forest-dwelling creatures have swirled around the world for centuries. Bigfoot’s other most-used moniker, Sasquatch, is an anglicized version of “Sasq’ets”, a word from the Coast Salish People, a tribe that lives in British Columbia. Versions of this mythical being, which goes by many different Indigenous names, have traveled across generations of stories, as both a large, hairy animal and a spiritual guide.

Today, Bigfoot has grown into something else entirely—as has the hunt to prove Bigfoot exists. Its likeness has been slapped on stickers, shirts, and road crossing signs. The Bigfoot subreddit boasts nearly 300,000 members. There are Bigfoot podcasts, conferences, symposiums, and a seven-night Bigfoot cruise that features daily lectures with Bigfoot researchers. Animal Planet released 11 seasons of the popular show Finding Bigfoot, and the Travel Channel has aired 59 episodes (and counting) of the docuseries Expedition Bigfoot.

There’s no question that Bigfoot has captured the imagination of many, but whether Bigfoot has any foothold in reality remains a mystery.

There are thousands of reports alleging Bigfoot’s existence; most of them focus on one creature, though the common belief within the Bigfoot community is that Bigfoot encompasses an entire species, like wolves, bears, or cougars. The Bigfoot Field Research Organization has collected more than 5,000 sightings through its online database. Washington state leads the charge, with over 700 reports. For decades, people have circulated grainy photographs, videos, and stories. But if Bigfoot is real, he (or she) remains elusive and incredibly camera-shy.

Bigfoot hunters, like Hoskins and crew, want science to back them up. “Science wants a body” is a common refrain in Sasquatch circles. Most aren’t actually rooting for the death of Bigfoot, but if he happened to peacefully pass away in an easily findable location, that would really help them out.

Bigfoot hunters might be lacking scientific proof, but they do have some trained scientists on their side, including tenured professors like the late anthropologists Grover Krantz and Jeffrey Meldrum, who both spent years researching the creature. Even Jane Goodall said she was open-minded about the existence of Bigfoot, and believed some of the stories she heard, adding that it was compelling that every continent has stories about a Bigfoot-type creature.

When Hoskins launched the website for the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search, he invited people to submit tales of their own encounters. He shared the site widely through social media channels and online Bigfoot groups, as well as in more general outdoors, fishing, and hunting forums. He also used geotags on Instagram during the group’s early searches, which generated some of the first reports from people who’d experienced supposed encounters near the same areas.

Since 2021, Hoskins and the rest of the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search have received 287 reports via the website and social media, covering everything from footprints and vocalizations to alleged sightings. “You have to take a lot of them with a grain of salt,” he told me. But he finds some reports compelling, especially the ones from people who want to remain anonymous, like loggers, law enforcement, and government employees.

The Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search has grown beyond solely an online database. The crew has headed out regularly, every month or two for the last five years, looking for evidence of unknown, large, bipedal creatures across the Pacific Northwest. The four men behind the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search include Hoskins, Stolp, and Palagi, who all once worked at Shriners together, along with Alberto Quinones, a warehouse manager who married Hoskins’ cousin. They’ve all become best friends, Hoskins says.

At 30 years old, Quinones, who usually sports a black hoodie and mustache, is the youngest of the group. Stolp, 48, is the oldest and a father of two, who commutes to Texas where he manages a multibillion-dollar construction project. He’s been out of the Army for over a decade, but his haircut could probably still pass a military inspection. His Army buddy, Palagi, likes to challenge his friends to cook-offs and lives with his three cats and dog at his home outside Portland. And Hoskins, also a father of two, is the de facto leader. He doesn’t relax until everyone’s made it to camp and always requests texts to make sure everyone got home safe after a trip. He likes to say that he “looks like a cop,” though he was denied a career in law enforcement because of severe scoliosis and an amputated right leg.

The four men don’t seem like typical Bigfoot hunters. For starters, half of them don’t think Bigfoot is real. Palagi calls himself the greatest skeptic of the group, saying, “I’ll drill holes in their theories all day long.” And Stolp is quick to clarify that he “doesn’t believe in that shit.” As for that camping trip in 2020, Palagi has an explanation: “A deer pouncing with both legs.”

And the loud, guttural howl: “A territorial bullfrog on the deer’s back.”

Even Hoskins, who spearheaded the formation of their Bigfoot search, says he’s only at 80 percent on the Bigfoot-believometer until he sees one for himself.

A washout pitches my car sideways at a precarious angle. A tunnel of overgrown bushes and trees scratches at my van. The rutted old logging road I’m following, full of creek crossings and potholes, turns my drive into a carnival ride. Hoskins warned me I’d need the right rig to get into camp to join the crew for a two-night Bigfoot search. I’m driving my 2011 Toyota Sienna, to which, Hoskins tells me, “I’ve never seen a minivan on that road.” But it’s all-wheel drive and equipped with an aftermarket lift kit and beefier tires for extra clearance. So far, it’s getting the job done, even if my heart rate is accelerating with every ditch I have to drive over. I wonder if this will be the scariest part of the weekend.

As I drive farther into the Gifford Pinchot National Forest, the trees seem to grow taller and thicker as the road narrows. I’m 47 miles from the nearest gas station, and each washout feels like a “do not enter” sign. I can’t help thinking that this densely wooded forest in the shadow of Mount Adams is where I’d come if I never wanted to be found.

I’m not a Bigfoot believer. But I’m curious about some of the stories I’ve heard that don’t have an easy explanation. Like, what exactly stomped through the guys’ campsite in 2020, howling like no creature they’d ever met.

The 1.3-million-acre Gifford Pinchot, where I’m meeting Hoskins and crew, is a hotbed for Bigfoot reports. It’s home to the aptly named Ape Canyon, where gold prospectors allegedly encountered seven-foot apelike animals in 1924. There’s also Skookum Meadows, where scientists found a body imprint left in the mud in 2000 that they attributed to an “unknown primate” because they couldn’t connect it to any animal. There were so many Bigfoot reports around here in the 1960s that Skamania County passed an ordinance to protect Bigfoot from harm, punishable by jail time or a hefty fine, which still stands today. The original ordinance acknowledged the increasing volume of “well-armed” Bigfoot hunters and expressed concern that said firearms could threaten both Bigfoot and humans traveling through the area.

Palagi, Hoskins, and Quinones at camp (Photo: Emily Halnon)

Finally, the narrow road opens to a small clearing in the trees, where two vehicles are parked a stone’s throw from a fire ring. Hoskins and Quinones are tossing wood onto a campfire. The ground is damp from 24 hours of rain. This summer’s fire ban has been lifted, and the group is taking full advantage.

At five-foot-two, Hoskins is about a foot shorter than Quinones. His work pants hang loose over his prosthetic leg, and he holds a walking stick in his left arm. He moves around the campsite with confident steps as he grabs his chainsaw and returns to the pile of firewood.

I walk over and see that a folding table is covered with their go-to supplies for Bigfoot hunts: thermal scopes, drones, casting materials, trail cameras, and audio recorders.

Hoskins has a handgun on his hip. Quinones has a shotgun in his car. They’re always armed, says Hoskins, though more for humans than Bigfoot. He mentions the Green River Killer, who murdered 49 women in the Pacific Northwest in the eighties and nineties, and left some of the bodies in the forests, which were later found by hikers.

It’s not unusual for campers to be armed in the woods this time of year. It’s hunting season in Washington. I’ve got a bright pink fleece and neon orange hat packed, thanks to Hoskins reminding me to bring colorful gear. He, on the other hand, is wearing a camouflage jacket and light brown pants. Quinones is dressed in all black.

“Bigfoot hunting is like playing the lottery, and the only way to really play is to go camping.”

As Hoskins fiddles with the thermal camera, he tells me that it took four years after that first encounter for them to find anything that could be pinned to Bigfoot. Fittingly, it was a big footprint, measuring 16 inches from heel-to-toe and 7 inches wide, that they stumbled upon in the woods. That’s roughly equivalent to a U.S. men’s size 26.

“That’s a long time to keep looking with no results,” I say.

“Well,” Hoskins starts. “There are only 2,000 Bigfoot creatures across North America.”

It’s unclear how exactly that estimate was generated, considering there isn’t definitive proof of a single Bigfoot. It’s also unclear what the plural of “Bigfoot” is. Bigfeet? Bigfoots? Bigfootuses?

“There are 30,000 bears in Oregon,” he goes on. “How often do you see one?”

I think back to the last bear I saw. It was in May. And the only one I’ve seen this year, despite spending most weekends, and plenty of weekdays, on trails around my home in Oregon.

“Bigfoot hunting is like playing the lottery, and the only way to really play is to go camping,” says Hoskins.

“I mostly said yes to my first search because I love camping,” says Quinones, who tells me that he grew up in a family that sees the outdoors as more of a threat than a fun pastime. When he moved to Sherwood, Oregon, from Los Angeles as a teenager, his main social options were hanging out in parking lots or around trees. “I chose trees,” he says and gestures at the many pine trees towering over us.

“I’d stopped getting outside until Kelly shared his encounter,” Hoskins adds.

Hoskins says he used to love driving deep into the forest and spending the night outside. But in his twenties, he fell into a different routine. Most of his friends preferred video games or dive bars to camping trips.

After Stolp told him about his 2020 encounter, Hoskins seized the opportunity to drive his rig back into the woods and set up camp. He also lost his great-grandmother in 2021, who was a parental figure through a childhood affected by addiction. He found himself even more drawn to the outdoors after her death.

“I spent weekends in the trees just screaming, crying, and staring at the fire,” he says. “I think being out here is the best thing for you when you’re going through shit; your mind actually has space to process what you’re feeling.”

The Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search hikes into the forest (Photo: Emily Halnon)

He’s not the only one in the group to use these camping trips to process grief, stress, or other hardships. “Some weekends, we’re all just sharing the tough things we’re going through around the campfire,” he says.

“It feels like the only place we can talk about everything, down to the really, really hard stuff,” says Stolp.

“The news is garbage, social media is garbage, everything is garbage,” Stolp continues. “The only thing that feels real and meaningful, other than my family, is going into the woods with my buddies. I think a connection with the outdoors is probably what a lot of the world is missing.”

Hoskins sweeps his arm around the campsite, which, short of the thermal scope and casting materials, looks like any weekend campsite, with camp chairs, packed coolers, and plentiful BBQ fixings. This location is a favorite of the group, I’m told, both for its idyllic setting on an alpine lake and for the ideal Bigfoot terrain that surrounds it: remote wilderness, very few humans, and thick forests. Also important is what they all describe as “weird energy.”

“Something about this camp is just creepy,” says Palagi. “At night, there’s just a weird feeling, maybe it’s because you can’t see very far. But the energy just shifts after the sun goes down.”

They’ve been to this particular spot so many times that they constructed a makeshift outhouse on a tree stump downhill from camp. Though Hoskins warns me not to use it, thanks to the local fire ant population taking a liking to it.

“I think it’s incredibly important to be good stewards of the land,” Stolp tells me, adding that they often pack out more than they pack in.

Hoskins checks the battery on the audio recorder. We’ll leave it out overnight to see if it captures any “vocalizations,” like the howl heard back in 2020. Bigfoot is also rumored to be a wood knocker, screamer, and whistler, depending on whose accounts you read or hear.

“Witching hour is from 11 P.M. to 3 A.M.,” he says.

I pull my hood up as the rain picks up. The shadows are growing long on the forest floor.

Hoskins tells me he was thrilled to see rain in the forecast for the weekend—not just because it’s good for campfires, but because “rain is great for prints.”

Last month, the group found a 12-inch footprint in the forest adjacent to our campsite. That’s equivalent to a size 14, which is less outrageous than the size-26 print, but still suspicious, according to Hoskins.

“I assume they aren’t born eight-feet tall,” he says. “That would be a very painful birth experience.”

He promises to show me where they found the footprint tomorrow.

“Tonight,” he says, “We’ll go down to the lake after it gets dark.”

The Gifford Pinchot isn’t just a hub for Bigfoot activity.

In 2021, a family reached out to Hoskins because their loved one, Glenn Austin Oldfield II, had gone missing in 2015 while camping alone in the Gifford Pinchot. When they saw the group was spending so much time in the same area, they asked if the Bigfoot hunters could keep an eye out for any sign of him.

Inspired by the request, Hoskins reached out to the Washington State Patrol, and they sent him a list of 34 people who’d been reported missing in the Gifford Pinchot since 1979.

It was then that the group launched the Pacific Northwest Missing Persons Project to look for evidence of missing persons and bring closure to cold cases. They realized that the same skills they use to look for abnormal footprints and disturbed soil could help them find scraps of clothing or human bones. They search in the Gifford Pinchot and beyond—wherever their hunt for Bigfoot takes them—or wherever there’s a family in need. Hoskins compiled the names of 500 people missing across Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, and California. Every time they go out, they’re armed with lists of clothing and belongings.

“I’ve been told I can’t do a lot of things. But no one can stop me from being out here.”

They see their work as picking up the trail where search and rescue leaves it, since most SAR squads lack the funding to continue the hunt after a mission moves from a search to a recovery.

“I want these families to get the closure they deserve,” says Hoskins.

Many families have gone years without answers. Some of them tell Hoskins they’ve been moved from detective to detective, while others can’t get anyone to answer their calls.

For Hoskins, searching for missing people gives him the purpose he’s been unable to find in his career. Hoskins was born with severe scoliosis and had 14 surgeries as a kid. His right leg was amputated when he was a toddler. He uses a hiking stick to get around, and has to physically lift his amputated leg over many of the logs that we crawl over throughout the weekend.

He wanted to serve in the military or go into law enforcement, but he couldn’t pass the physical fitness tests. He got within 30 seconds of completing the course Oregon requires for police candidates, but no matter how hard he trained, he couldn’t do it any faster. He was denied by every branch of the military, he told me. When he was young, doctors told him he wouldn’t be able to walk or run.

“I’ve been told I can’t do a lot of things,” he said. “But no one can stop me from being out here.”

The Pacific Northwest Missing Persons Project is currently pursuing three active searches and has a waitlist of over 100 families. They’re mostly limited by time and gas money, Hoskins says; he would take on every case if he could. Last fall, they found human bones during one of their searches. They’re still waiting on law enforcement to identify them.

A curtain of mist shrouds the lake in the early morning. The night before, we’d set up an audio recorder on the shore and searched the forest for heat and sound. I don’t know if it was the campfire stories or the “weird energy” vibes, but I wasn’t upset that Hoskins had that handgun on his hip as we slipped into the shadows of the dark forest. Quinones recounted how they once heard a voice, “clear as day,” saying “hello,” as they walked through the woods. It’s part of the legend: Bigfoot will mimic human voices or animal sounds. We didn’t find any sign of Bigfoot last night. Still, I didn’t get out of my van to pee until the sun was up.

This morning, we climb over the deadfall that guards the lake and bushwhack through its marshy edges. We walk over broken tree limbs, foot-long pinecones, and downed logs, looking for disturbances or anything that seems odd: boulders that have been moved, branches that have been woven together, or footprints that seem too big and too out-of-place to be human.

The lake in the Gifford Pinchot National Forest (Photo: Emily Halnon)

“Basically anything that looks like nature didn’t do it,” says Hoskins. The crew draws from their many years of military service, backcountry hunting, and outdoor navigation to look for clues during their searches.

“I’m constantly scanning 20 feet in front of me, looking for breaks, looking for grass pushed down, watching game trails, seeing if there’s a track,” says Stolp.

As we hike, the guys point at the foliage, which is starting to change color, and the hillside that rises above us. Quinones offers to take me up to the ridge so I can see the view. “It’s just gorgeous out here,” says Hoskins.

I get the sense that if searching for Bigfoot involved cities, smog, and skyscrapers, they’d quickly lose interest.

After a lot of off-trail hiking, we get to the shore of the lake and scan the softer, more malleable ground for prints. There are hoofprints galore. But after searching banks of mud, we spy something much bigger than a hoof.

“That could be a deer butt,” says Hoskins. “But let’s cast it to find out.”

He pours plaster into each depression in the soil. We’ll pick up the hardened casts tomorrow to see if they reveal anything promising, like toes, or a whole footprint.

Hoskins then leads us to where they found a footprint last month. When they discovered it, they had Quinones try to match it.

Hoskins sets the cast (Photo: Emily Halnon)

“He’s our go-to guy when we’re trying to gauge whether a large human could’ve left the print,” says Hoskins of Quinones’s six-foot-two height and 250-pound build. “He couldn’t come close.”

“Our first step is to try to debunk anything that we find,” he says. “I can’t say that it wasn’t a human.” Then, he gestures to the ground, which is covered in debris right up to the mud, before sweeping his arm over the lake, which is more bug bog than pristine water.

“But would you walk around here barefoot?”

Palagi shifts his gaze towards the canopy and asks, “You ever look up while you’re out here?” His tone suggests he’s gearing up to spook me, not to point out the scenery.

It’s the next morning, and we’re walking back to the lake to fetch the cast. Palagi drove into camp a few hours ago while an owl was still hooting from a nearby tree. He had to work all weekend, so he told Hoskins he’d get here before we woke up to make breakfast. I thought there was no way Palagi would show up, just to cook us some eggs. Our campsite was a two-hour drive from his home outside Portland, each way. But I saw his headlights cutting through the night while I was still in my sleeping bag. Before I was out of my van, he had his entire camp kitchen out, along with fixings for breakfast sandwiches, and more wood for the fire.

“There’s definitely a crisis going on for men right now,” says Stolp, as he talks about why he keeps coming back to these woods. “And we’ve found a brotherhood through going out in the woods and looking for something that may or may not exist.”

I look up at the canopy and ask Palagi: “Does Bigfoot like to hang out up there?”

“No,” he scoffs. “But cougars do.”

“There’s definitely mystery out here,” Palagi goes on, as he looks around the trees that surround us.

Palagi tells me he plans to stop telling the tale of that camping trip in 2020. He’s sick of talking about something that he doesn’t believe in.

But he’s still out here—and even the “greatest skeptic of all” thinks about that question when he’s walking around this forest, he says: What else is out there?

Hoskins points to tracks in the dirt (Photo: Emily Halnon)

It’s like a treasure hunt every time they come out, Stolp tells me, like being a kid again. And, minus the loaded firearms, this camping trip is reminiscent of scenes from my childhood, when I’d wander through the woods of Vermont looking for my next pirate ship or dragon’s den in the trees and boulders. The possibilities felt endless every time I stepped into the forest.

And maybe that’s a big part of why these guys come back again and again. Whether we believe in Bigfoot or not, there’s an appeal to searching for the unknown. We’re wired to stare at the ocean, the night sky—and a dark forest—and wonder what’s out there. We like thinking about that question, even if it comes with goosebumps on occasion. But it’s easy to lose touch with that in all of the other wires of life.

Hoskins grabs a toothbrush and goes to work on the cast. He gives a play-by-play while he dusts off the hardened plaster, “Indent, another indent.”

When he holds it up for its dramatic reveal, everyone stops talking so he can tell us what he found. I want it to be a large and indisputable footprint. But I know it’s hard to win the lottery on one ticket.

“It’s an unidentifiable blob,” Hoskins says. “Most of them are.” I’m disappointed, of course, but I realize that even the biggest, most undeniable footprint wouldn’t really answer anything. Not even another size 26 track would stop anyone from coming back to the forest—between deadlines, commutes, and night shifts—to walk through the trees and look for reasons to believe the world is still full of wonder.

Why cheap health insurance plans can backfire for Americans seeking lower healthcare costs

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Healthcare costs are a major concern for Americans, and health insurance premiums are a primary fuel for that anxiety.

Shrinking federal subsidies for Affordable Care Act plans led to large premium increases for millions of Americans in 2025, causing many people to drop coverage. With premiums expected to rise further and Medicaid work requirements set to begin in January 2027, many Americans are left scrambling for affordable options.

We are health economists who study public policies aimed at expanding health insurance coverage. When it comes to our own health insurance, we are fortunate: We are among the roughly 160 million Americans who receive coverage through their employers.

But if you don’t work for a large employer that provides health benefits, finding coverage for your family can be confusing and, increasingly, expensive.

People looking for low-cost alternatives have no doubt encountered several options. They may look appealing and, in some cases, may be appropriate, but they can also carry real risks.

Navigating trade-offs

Historically, people who didn’t have access to employer-sponsored coverage had limited health insurance options. Before the Affordable Care Act went into effect in 2014, insurers could charge you a higher premium based on your health status, exclude coverage for preexisting medical conditions, or deny you coverage altogether.

The ACA eliminated this kind of discrimination, established a minimum standard of 10 essential benefits that health insurance plans had to cover, and required plans to fully cover the cost of preventive care. It also introduced income-based subsidies that could be used to purchase coverage in newly established online marketplaces.

Low-cost plans that aren’t available through ACA marketplaces are essentially vestiges of the pre-ACA health insurance landscape.

Regardless of where you get coverage, choosing health insurance involves trade-offs. The more you pay in premiums, the more comprehensive your coverage will be in terms of how much you must pay when you need care and the breadth of the network of covered providers.

Some trade-offs are clear when you choose a plan, but some lower-cost options may have limitations that are not immediately obvious.

Less costly short-term plans

As their name suggests, short-term, limited-duration plans are designed for people with temporary gaps in coverage, such as those who are between jobs or months away from qualifying for Medicare. These plans, which can be purchased for periods of up to six months, tend to be cheaper than marketplace coverage for two reasons.

First, because they are not subject to ACA rules, they may not provide the full range of essential health benefits. Short-term, limited-duration plans often do not cover maternity care and commonly don’t have an annual limit on out-of-pocket costs. In fact, these plans may cap how much they will pay in a year, leaving policyholders stuck with large, unexpected expenses.

Second, unlike plans offered through the ACA marketplaces, short-term, limited-duration plans are allowed to deny coverage based on your health status. Screening out higher-cost consumers reduces premiums, but if you are not in perfect health, you may not be able to find an insurer that will sell you one of these more limited plans.

Because of a concern that they destabilize the market by siphoning off healthy consumers, short-term, limited-duration plans are banned in five states.

Alternatives that look like insurance

Another seemingly affordable option is healthcare sharing ministries, arrangements through which consumers with common religious beliefs agree to share medical expenses. Their websites resemble those of health insurance companies—for example, they often present different plan options offering different levels of benefits—but they avoid using standard insurance terms like “premium” or “deductible” because these offerings are not insurance.

Because they are not regulated as insurance companies, healthcare sharing ministries are not subject to consumer protections or other state or federal requirements. There is also no guarantee that the ministry will cover your medical expenses when you submit a claim.

Like short-term, limited-duration plans, healthcare sharing ministries may deny coverage based on your health history, exclude coverage for preexisting conditions, and put a limit on the dollar amount they will pay in benefits.

Another lower-cost option available in 14 states is health coverage offered by a farm bureau. These plans are similar to healthcare sharing ministries in that they are limited to a certain group of people—to enroll, you must be a member of the state’s farm bureau, though that does not mean you necessarily have to work in agriculture.

These plans are also not subject to federal or state insurance regulations. This means that, like short-term, limited-duration plans, they can offer lower prices by not offering certain benefits required by the ACA and by screening out high-cost consumers. Unlike short-term, limited-duration plans, there is no limit on how long you can hold farm bureau coverage.

Choosing a health insurance plan often requires fighting through a thicket of jargon.

What should you do?

Although the cost of marketplace coverage has increased dramatically since 2025, for many consumers who need coverage for more than six months, it remains the best option.

If you are single with an annual income below $64,000, a couple with an annual income below $87,000, or a family of four with an annual income less than $132,000, you should still be eligible for a premium subsidy. And you will know that the plan you are purchasing is real insurance that covers a standard set of benefits and is subject to robust consumer protections.

If you are considering an alternative to marketplace coverage, there are a few things you should do to protect yourself:

Make sure you ask for a summary of benefits and coverage. This is a plain-language summary of a health plan’s benefits and coverage with examples of how much the plan would cover for two specific conditions—diabetes and childbirth. Insurers and employers who offer coverage are required to provide this document to anyone applying for coverage. If they cannot provide one, what they are selling may not actually be insurance.

If they do provide a summary of benefits and coverage, read it carefully. As we noted earlier, one reason some options might be less expensive than a marketplace plan is that they provide much less coverage. Don’t assume that they cover all the benefits you would expect from a health insurance plan, or that they will cover the full cost of the care you receive.

Finally, never enter your phone number into a “do you need insurance” website. This area is rife with fraud, including multiple cases in which telemarketers misled consumers into paying hundreds of dollars per month to purchase sham health insurance.

Health insurance is expensive because healthcare is expensive—which means that consumers have to make difficult trade-offs. There is no easy answer, but beware of options that look too good to be true. They very well might be.
