Pope Leo tells Villanova and Notre Dame basketball teams he is neutral. Prevost has other ideas

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

VATICAN CITY (AP) — Pope Leo XIV on Saturday told the basketball teams from his alma mater Villanova and fellow Catholic university Notre Dame — in Rome for their season opener — that in matters of sport, the pontiff is neutral.

Then the pope, who was born Robert Prevost, said his former self might disagree.

“Prevost has another opinion, but we won’t do that now,” he said to laughs during a whooping, cheering audience of 7,000 people from both schools — players, coaches, fans and their families — in the Vatican audience hall.

The teams were in town for the U.S. college basketball season opener in Rome. The Villanova and Notre Dame men’s and women’s teams will play a doubleheader on Sunday at the 11,000-seat Palazzo dello Sport, about a half-hour drive from the Vatican.

The event has been dubbed the Eternal City Tip-Off and was created to honor the first American pope.

Jay Wright, the former Villanova coach who led the men’s team to two NCAA championships and was inducted this month into the National Collegiate Hall of Fame, said “this pilgrimage to Rome and meeting the pope is bigger than the game.”

The audience had been up in the air after the 71-year-old pope underwent lung cancer surgery on Oct. 13 and is still recovering. But Leo kept the appointment ahead of Sunday’s opener.

Leo graduated in 1977 with a Bachelor of Science in mathematics from Villanova, the Augustinian school near Philadelphia. He was born in Chicago — not all that far from Notre Dame, which is located in South Bend, Indiana.

Leo dribbles and poses

Leo, an avid sports fan and tennis player, dribbled a golden basketball presented to him by the Villanova women’s team. And when the towering players from Notre Dame presented him with a jersey and another golden ball, Leo stepped up to the podium where his chair sits to try to gain a few inches (centimeters) on them.

He gamely posed for a photo with the Fighting Irish, holding the banner they gave him “Pope Leo, Play like a champion today” — a riff on the famous Notre Dame sign at the university’s football stadium.

But he gave Villanova equal time, making the “V'” gesture with his hand as chants of “Let’s Go ‘Nova” erupted in the audience hall.

“He is a true Villanovan,” said the Villanova women’s coach, Denise Dillon. “I said, ‘OK everyone: V’s up.’ And he immediately was in with it.”

Sport as a way to saintliness

After mistakenly starting out in Italian, Leo switched to his mother-tongue English and spoke off the cuff, recalling that in his native Chicago, he grew up hearing “Go Irish,” the Notre Dame chant.

“But as you all know, Prevost graduated from Villanova,” Leo said, referring to his past self in the third person.

“So the pope doesn’t take sides,” he said to laughs, before revealing that “Prevost has another opinion.”

In his prepared remarks to the crowd, Leo urged the teams and their families to embrace sports as a way to grow in saintliness.

“No one is born a champion, just as no one is born a saint,” Leo said.

He warned against letting sporting rivalries get out of hand and he said he hoped the fans in Rome this weekend would actually get to know one another as they cheer, “even passionately.”

“In any competition, the members of two teams, even when they have a competitive rivalry, are certainly not enemies,” he said. “I hope, therefore, that despite the competitive nature of college basketball, there will be opportunities for the players and the fans of both teams to get to know each other in your days here in Rome.”

He noted that the game will take place on All Saints’ Day, and that the church teaches Christians to pursue saintliness in every aspect of their lives, including in sports.

“Our true goal, beyond emerging victorious in any one event, is to win the prize of everlasting life,” he said.

Leo and past popes have exalted sports

Like popes before him, Leo has praised the value of sport for both physical and spiritual development, calling sport a “school of fraternity” that nurtures friendship, respect and teamwork. But he also warned about how the win-at-all-cost mentality of sport has contributed to episodes of doping, match-fixing and other forms of corruption.

The Rev. Robert Dowd, president of Notre Dame, acknowledged Villanova had something of an edge in Sunday’s game, considering it counts the pope as an alumnus. But in an interview Saturday, he said, “The pope is a pope for all of us.

“The pope talks about the importance of bridge building,” Dowd said, “and I’m sure he’ll be a bridge builder when we play each other.”

Villanova’s president, the Rev. Peter M. Donohue, said there was “a little extra pride” being with Leo, given his allegiance. But he said what happens on Sunday was not the point.

“No matter what happens on the court, this is the true highlight of the trip,” Donohue said in his introductory remarks to the audience. “Tomorrow we will cheer, we will compete, but today we are simply grateful.”

Philadelphia Archbishop Nelson Perez celebrated a private Mass for traveling fans in St. Peter’s Basilica on Saturday morning, and asked the thousands of people in the pews to give a round of applause to the players. The English-language liturgy was unusual in that it had all the trappings of a papal Mass celebrated at the main altar and televised live by Vatican Media.

Leo is an avid Chicago White Sox fan, and has been known to follow basketball, too.

Asked about the upcoming games on the papal plane during a recent trip to France, Leo said, “They tell me that the people coming to the match, 90% are Villanova and 10% Notre Dame, so the voice of the people of God will be very strong on the Villanova side.”

Inexpensive things to do in Alaska on a cruise

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you're used to cruising in the Caribbean, shore excursion prices on your Alaska cruise might surprise you.

Want to take a helicopter ride to the top of a glacier to enjoy a dogsled ride? It'll cost upward of $700 per person. Excited about an authentic Alaska fishing experience? That's a more than $400 tour. A nearly three-hour scenic train ride on the White Pass and Yukon Route Railroad starts at more than $150 per traveler.

Not only are tours so pricey because Alaska is a remote destination (where it costs more to fly in goods and materials), but also because Alaska is part of the U.S., where wages and tour insurance costs can be higher than in the islands. Plus, many Alaska excursions involve seaplanes, helicopters, specialized boats and other equipment that only add to the price of your onshore experience.

For many, an Alaska cruise is a once-in-a-lifetime trip, and many tours are worth the splurge. But you don't have to spend money hand over fist in every port. Whether you're trying to balance your budget with a few cheaper days or you simply don't have the resources to spend big every day of your vacation, you'll want to seek out cheap or free things to do in Alaska's cruise ports.

Luckily, you have plenty of options. I know because I've spent many a day exploring independently in Alaska's ports. Plus, I consulted with Alaska's tourism representatives to bring you a comprehensive list of free (and cheap) things to do in Alaska on a cruise.

Things to do in Skagway, Alaska, for free and cheap

The historic gold rush town of Skagway, Alaska. REINHARD PANTKE/TRAVEL ALASKA

Skagway is known for its gold rush history, but you don't need a pricey tour to immerse yourself in the town's storied past. Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park has four free museums open to visitors, including the Visitor Center Museum (with interactive exhibits), the Moore House Museum (former home of the first family to live in Skagway, with two restored Victorian-era rooms), the Jeff Smiths Parlour Museum (a former bar with a shady past) and the Mascot Saloon Museum (a restored gold rush saloon).

If you're fully embracing the old-timey vibe, you'll want to belly up to the bar at the Red Onion Saloon. Once, it was Skagway's signature bordello, but now it's the place to sling back an Alaska-brewed beer or snack on apps and pizzas named after former good-time girls. For a reasonable $20 a person, you can sign up for a Quickie Tour of the venue's supposedly haunted brothel museum. Ghost sightings don't cost extra!

One of my favorite things to do in Skagway is stretch my legs on a hike, and there are several trailheads within easy walking distance of your cruise ship. Options include the Upper and Lower Dewey Lake, Reid Falls and Gold Rush Cemetery, and Icy Lake and Upper Reid Falls trails. The National Park Service kindly lists its hikes by distance from Skagway's Historic District, as well as by level of difficulty. No guide is needed, and you don't have to pay to access the trails.

Free and cheap things to do in Juneau, Alaska

GOLDBELT TRAM/FACEBOOK

Window shopping is a popular activity along the main streets by Juneau's cruise docks, but you might be tempted to blow your budget on souvenirs. (Look for sales, especially during end-of-season sailings, if you want to buy gifts for less.)

Typically, you can pay $60 for an all-day pass up and down Juneau's Goldbelt tram*, or if you're in good shape, you can hike from your ship to the top of the tram on the Mount Roberts Trail for free. I did this hike on my recent Alaska cruise, and while it's not technical, it is steep and can be muddy after a rainstorm. Make lots of noise just in case bears are on the trail.

At the top, you can visit the Chilkat Theater to watch a free movie about the region's Tlingit people. If you're too tired to hike back down, you can get a ride back on the tram for $20.

However, the Goldbelt tram has been closed since late April 2026 for maintenance. Visitors can still go to the base of the tram to watch Tlingit artists carve totem poles, weave ceremonial robes and perform cultural dances for no charge.

Other hikes accessible from your ship include the Gold Creek Flume Trail and the Perseverance Trail.

If a stroll through downtown is more your speed, self-guided tours of Juneau come free of charge. The Juneau Voices project features audio installations at 11 spots around town where you can learn about the city through the stories of Native Alaskans. Or grab a map from a tourist welcome center and follow this walking tour to see the city's highlights.

A totem pole in Juneau, Alaska. EYFOTO/GETTY IMAGES

You can also find a PDF guide to Juneau's totem poles online.

If you prefer to get your history and culture indoors (not a bad plan in Alaska, given the temperamental climate), Juneau offers a few museums with modest admission fees that are worth checking out.

The Juneau-Douglas City Museum offers a mix of permanent and temporary exhibits on the area's history and art. Admission is $7, but you might need a cab if you're not up for a 20-minute walk. The museum also runs a free walking tour of the Alaska State Capitol Building.

Also, a 20-minute walk from the port is the Alaska State Museum, where you can learn about the cultures of the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian peoples. Admission is $14. For more information on Native Alaskans and their cultures, visit the Sealaska Heritage Institute's Walter Soboleff Building ($10 admission) and its gift shop, which features local art. Don't miss the signature totem pole and handcrafted clan house.

If you're keen for a scenic uphill walk, head up Basin Road to the Last Chance Mining Museum ($5) to learn about the gold rush in a historic mining building.

*At the time of writing, Goldbelt tram is closed but has plans to reopen soon. Check availability and prices before you plan.

Cheap and free things to do in Ketchikan, Alaska, from a cruise ship

A clan house in Saxman. TRAVEL ALASKA

Ketchikan is best known for its Creek Street Historic District, built on a wooden boardwalk above the salmon-filled Ketchikan Creek. Stroll for free by its historic buildings, shops and restaurants, or pay $10 to stop into Dolly's House for a glimpse into the city's former red-light district.

Not far from Creek Street, the Tongass Historical Museum and Totem Heritage Center immerses you in the area's history for a modest $6 fee (children and military are free). From there, you can embark on a self-guided, 1 1/2-mile Ketchikan Salmon Walk to learn about the fish's impact on the town (and maybe even spot some, in season, at the Salmon Ladder). The walk will take you past the Totem Heritage Center (also $6, or get a combo ticket to both museums for $9), with its collection of 19th-century totem poles rescued from nearby islands.

If you're feeling adventurous, you can skip the tour and head out to the Saxman Native Village on your own. It's 2 miles south of Ketchikan, so it's walkable — but for a few bucks, you can take a city bus there instead. It costs just $5 to wander the park on your own and see the 25 replica totem poles, as well as Native carvers working on new poles using traditional techniques.

Things to do in Icy Strait Point, Alaska, for free or cheap

ICY STRAIT POINT/FACEBOOK

Icy Strait Point is a manufactured cruise port near the town of Hoonah. It was created to offer everything cruisers could possibly want in a port, so all the activities are planned to get you to part ways with your hard-earned cash. That said, there are still plenty of things to do in Icy Strait Point for free or cheap.

A free gondola ride transports visitors from one cruise pier area to the other, but if the line is long or you're afraid of heights, you can walk the same path on nature trails instead. Keep your eyes out for wildlife.

The port has tons of beach access, so you can wander Icy Strait's shores, stop for scenic photos and look for marine life free of charge. At the Hoonah Cannery building, you can browse the Hoonah Packing Co. Cannery Museum exhibits interspersed among the shops, or get tickets to the Native dance show for $20 per person at the on-site ticket booth.

From the cannery, it's a 1 1/2-mile (roughly 30-minute) walk to Hoonah, an actual town where many of Icy Strait Point's workers live and home to Alaska's largest Tlingit village. You can also take an affordable $10 shuttle to town, where you can admire the totem poles and sip a local beer at Icy Strait Brewing.

Things to do in Sitka, Alaska, for free or cheap

The Sheldon Jackson Museum. JOHN ELK/GETTY IMAGES

Sitka offers a compelling mix of Native Alaskan and Russian history and culture, and a self-guided tour of the key sites in town won't cost you much (if anything at all).

You can get a free tour of the Russian Bishop's House, an example of 19th-century architecture during Russia's colonization of Sitka. Baranof Castle State Historic Site (Castle Hill) was occupied throughout the centuries by Tlingit people and later by Russian settlers, and it was the site chosen for the transfer of Alaska from Russia to the U.S. You can check out the views and the informative signs for free.

St. Michael Cathedral National Historic Landmark is an exact replica of the first Russian Orthodox church in the Americas. Because the site has an active congregation, visitors can view its collection of 17th-century religious artifacts for a limited time each day.

Museums with small admission fees include the Sitka History Museum (featuring exhibits on local history) and the Sheldon Jackson Museum (showcasing a collection of Alaska Native artifacts). If exploring old cemeteries is your thing, you'll want to stop at the more than 200-year-old Sitka Russian Cemetery, with gravestones made from the ballast of Russian ships.

The Alaska Raptor Center in Sitka. JOHN ELK/GETTY IMAGES

The Sitka National Historical Park's visitor center and grounds (with walking trails and totem pole installations) are open to all, free of charge. It's a scenic spot for an easy nature walk, or you can come inside to learn more about Sitka's Russian and Tlingit cultures or join a ranger program.

You don't need a tour to gawk at eagles, hawks, owls and falcons at the Alaska Raptor Center. Admission is $16, though it is a bit of a walk from downtown. Watch injured birds relearn how to fly, and take a stroll through the center's forested grounds.

Finally, you can attend various dance performances at affordable ticket prices. The Alaska Storytellers dance show ($15) celebrates traditional and modern Russian dance, while the New Archangel Dancers' performances highlight Russian folk dances. Or, step into a Tlingit clan house to enjoy the music, costumes and choreography of the Naa Kahidi Tlingit Dancers.

Bottom line

You don't need to spend a fortune on tours to explore Alaska's culture and history or enjoy its natural splendor. You'll find plenty of free things to do in port on an Alaska cruise. However, I do encourage you to budget for a few tours, whether it's to travel beyond the port area into Alaska's wilderness or to immerse yourself in an activity, such as dog sledding, kayaking, fishing, or whale or bear watching. You won't regret spending the money on amazing experiences and memories.

Why Your Hex Converter Lies to You: Two's Complement, the 53-Bit Wall, and Radix Magic

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Every programmer eventually encounters that moment of quiet confusion during a late-night debugging session: you cast a signed 8-bit -1 to hexadecimal, expecting something clean and human-readable, only for the debugger to spit back 0xFF. Widen it to a 32-bit register, and it suddenly balloons into 0xFFFFFFFF.


Where did the minus sign go? Why didn't it output -0x01? And why does running 0x20000000000001 through your standard JavaScript parser silently yield the wrong integer altogether?


Number base conversion is often treated as a trivial Computer Science 101 exercise—something solved with a simple parseInt(val, 16) or a textbook division-by-radix loop. But beneath high-level abstractions, radix shifts are inextricably linked to hardware registers, two’s complement mathematics, IEEE-754 rounding traps, and data-encoding strategies.


Here is what is actually happening under the hood when bits change their radix, and why understanding these low-level mechanics prevents catastrophic bugs in production.

1. The Illusion of the Minus Sign: How Two's Complement Works

Hardware processors don't have dedicated silicon cells reserved for storing an aesthetic - glyph. In CPU registers, negative numbers are encoded via Two’s Complement.


In an NNN-bit register, the most significant bit (MSB) acts as a negative weight:

When you assign -1 to an 8-bit signed integer (int8_t):

  1. Invert all bits of positive 1 (0000 0001 →\rightarrow→ 1111 1110).
  2. Add 1 (1111 1110 + 0000 0001 = 1111 1111).


In hexadecimal, each character represents an exact 4-bit nibble. Thus, 1111 1111 maps directly to 0xFF.

c #include <stdio.h> #include <stdint.h> int main() { int8_t small_neg = -1; int32_t wide_neg = small_neg; // Sign extension occurs here printf("8-bit hex: 0x%02X\n", (uint8_t)small_neg); // 0xFF printf("32-bit hex: 0x%08X\n", (uint32_t)wide_neg); // 0xFFFFFFFF return 0; }

The Sign Extension Trap

Notice what happened when widening int8_t to int32_t: the single byte 0xFF became 0xFFFFFFFF.


If the hardware merely padded the higher 24 bits with zeros (0x000000FF), the value would evaluate to +255, silently mutating your application state. To preserve the mathematical value of -1, the CPU performs Sign Extension: it duplicates the MSB (which is 1) across all newly allocated upper bits.


Pro-Tip for Packet Inspection:

When inspecting raw network packets or memory dumps, you can instantly determine if a hexadecimal value represents a negative signed integer without converting to decimal: look at the leading character.


Because 8 through F have their highest bit set to 1:

  • 0x8... through 0xF... →\rightarrow→ Negative (in signed contexts)
  • 0x0... through 0x7... →\rightarrow→ Zero or Positive

2. Arithmetic Shift (>>) vs. Logical Shift (>>>): A Subtle Production Hazard

Understanding two’s complement makes the difference between bitwise shift operators crystal clear:

  • Arithmetic Right Shift (>>): Preserves the sign bit. Vacated positions on the left are backfilled with whatever the MSB was.
  • Logical Right Shift (>>>): Agnostic to sign. It unconditionally backfills vacated positions with zeros.
typescript const value = -42; // Binary: 11111111 11111111 11111111 11010110 (in 32-bit) console.log(value >> 1); // Output: -21 (MSB remains 1; arithmetic integrity preserved) console.log(value >>> 1); // Output: 2147483627 (MSB replaced with 0; immediately converts to large unsigned integer)

In telemetry, hashing routines, and protocol deserialization, accidentally using >>> instead of >> (or vice versa) produces bugs that unit tests with small positive integers never catch.

3. The 53-Bit Wall: Why JavaScript Silently Corrupts Large Radixes

Consider this seemingly benign conversion snippet:

javascript const highSeqId = "9007199254740993"; // 2^53 + 1 console.log(Number(highSeqId).toString(16)); // Expected: "20000000000001" // Actual: "20000000000000" (Precision collapsed!)

Standard JavaScript Number types conform to the IEEE-754 double-precision floating-point specification. Double floats allocate:

  • 1 sign bit
  • 11 exponent bits
  • 52 explicit fraction bits (providing 53 bits of precision with the implicit leading 1)


Any integer larger than Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER (253−12^{53} - 1253−1, or 9,007,199,254,740,991) cannot be uniquely represented. When you convert an arbitrary-large hexadecimal hash or a 64-bit Snowflake ID via standard floating-point numbers, the least significant bits get silently rounded to the nearest representable floating-point neighbor.

The Fix: BigInt and Limbs

Arbitrary-precision arithmetic operates outside the Floating-Point Unit (FPU). Instead of relying on a single hardware register, runtimes allocate heap arrays of 64-bit integer chunks (often called machine limbs):

javascript // Lossless 64-bit / 128-bit Radix Transformations const safeVal = BigInt("0x20000000000001"); console.log(safeVal.toString(10)); // "9007199254740993" (Exact) console.log(safeVal.toString(36)); // "2000000000001"

4. Radix 36: The Workhorse of URL Slugs and Distributed IDs

While base-2 (binary), base-8 (octal), and base-16 (hexadecimal) map directly into CPU word architectures because they are powers of two (21,23,242^1, 2^3, 2^421,23,24), higher non-power-of-two radices serve specialized architectural purposes.


Chief among them is Radix 36 (0–9, a–z).

While Base-64 yields the highest raw density, its reliance on uppercase versus lowercase characters makes it vulnerable to collisions on case-insensitive filesystems and legacy databases. Furthermore, standard Base-64 requires URL-safe variations (RFC 4648) to avoid collision with URI reserved characters.


Radix 36 hits the sweet spot: it is totally case-insensitive, yields compact strings, and requires zero URL encoding.

python def to_base36(num: int) -> str: alphabet = "0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz" if num == 0: return "0" arr = [] while num: num, rem = divmod(num, 36) arr.append(alphabet[rem]) return "".join(reversed(arr)) print(to_base36(1685894123512)) # "hicw5f3g" (Clean slug ready for routing)

5. Visualizing & Validating Complex Edge Cases

When designing custom bitmasks, verifying register overflows, or calculating variable-width two's complement strings, hand-calculating bit shifts invites human error.


If you are debugging packet headers or writing firmware-level parsers, you can cross-reference your math against this interactive Number Base Converter sandbox, which calculates signed/unsigned representations, arbitrary-width sign extensions, and custom radices from 2 to 36 without floating-point precision drops.

Summary Checklist for Systems Developers

  1. Check your signedness: 0xFF is 255 in an unsigned 8-bit byte, but -1 in a signed two's complement register.
  2. Be cautious with bitwise shifts in JavaScript: JS converts operands into 32-bit signed integers before running bitwise operators. >> sign-extends; >>> zero-extends.
  3. Beware the 53-bit boundary: Never parse 64-bit IDs or large hashes with parseInt() or floating-point primitives—always enforce BigInt.
  4. Use Base-36 for safe slugification: When you need human-readable, URL-safe, compact sequential keys without case-sensitivity bugs, reach for Radix 36 before Base-64.

New York court takes up Trump’s latest efforts to toss hush money conviction

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

MANHATTAN (CN) — President Donald Trump will take another stab at getting his historic hush money conviction, which infamously made him the first ever commander-in-chief to be found guilty of a crime, dismissed.

On Thursday, Trump’s personal legal team will argue to a mid-level appellate court in New York that his prosecution was politically motivated, a last-ditch bid to overturn the 34 counts of falsifying business records a jury convicted him on in 2024. 

Manhattan jurors found Trump carried out a sweeping and salacious hush money scheme aimed at quelling bad press during his 2016 presidential campaign. Trump’s former personal attorney and “fixer” Michael Cohen testified at trial that Trump directed him to pay $130,000 to adult film star Stormy Daniels, whom Trump was concerned would publicize that they had a sexual encounter a decade prior — while he was married. 

Cohen said Trump signed off on a monthly plan to repay him, illegally disguising reimbursement checks as standard legal fees. Further testimony revealed Trump often signed those illicit checks from the Oval Office during his first presidential term. 

While Trump could have faced jail time, he was merely sentenced to unconditional discharge early last year after his 2024 presidential win. New York Supreme Court Justice Juan Merchan deemed that the only conceivable sentence for an incoming president.

Still, Trump has been persistent in his efforts to toss the case, which he calls “the most politically charged prosecution in our nation’s history” in legal filings. He claims he was unlawfully prosecuted for official acts he took while president — an argument that has already been shot down by multiple judges who said the hush money payments were personal issues. 

Trump also claims Merchan should have recused himself from the case. In 2020, Merchan donated $15 to Joe Biden and another $20 to two Democratic PACs. And during the trial, Merchan’s daughter ran an advertising firm that worked with Democrats.

These issues created “the appearance of bias,” Trump argues, that should have warranted Merchan’s recusal. Merchan declined to do so on several occasions, relying on the judgment of a judicial ethics panel that found he could still be an objective presider. 

“Like every criminal defendant in a New York courtroom, President Trump was entitled to a fair trial before a properly instructed jury and a neutral judge,” Trump claims in a brief. “Instead, he was convicted after a trial that featured repeated and clear violations of his constitutional rights, federal law, and New York law, presided over by a judge who was required to recuse.”

Trump’s appeal is being handled by his new legal team at Sullivan & Cromwell. They have a tall task ahead of them, according to retired New York Judge George Grasso, who called Trump’s appellate odds “highly unlikely.”

“The First Department [appellate court] would only do that if it thought there was a very substantial legal error by the court that would require it,” Grasso told Courthouse News. “It’s something that is highly unusual.” 

Grasso, who attended every day of the six-week trial as a spectator, said Merchan was right not to recuse himself and was “abundantly fair to both sides.”

“Based on my knowledge of the case and the prevailing law, I would expect the felony conviction of President Trump to be upheld,” he said. 

Trump has been particularly litigious in his efforts to invalidate his conviction. He tried on numerous occasions to move the case from state court to federal court — even after his sentencing — on the basis that he should have been shielded from prosecution for official acts.

 At every turn, those efforts were rebuffed by U.S. District Judge Alvin Hellerstein. 

“There is no way that discussions regarding hush money payments to cover up the president’s relations with Stormy Daniels could be an official act. An affair, or a cover-up of an affair, is not within the ‘outer perimeter’ of the president’s official responsibilities,’” Hellerstein, a Bill Clinton appointee who died at age 92 on Oct. 28, said in his most recent ruling on the matter this past summer.

Several of Trump’s trial lawyers have been appointed to high-level government roles during his second presidential administration. Todd Blanche is now the U.S. Attorney General, and Emil Bove is an appellate judge in the Third Circuit. 

The case was brought by Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg, who said the underlying coverup was part of Trump’s broader scheme to influence the outcome of the 2016 election.
