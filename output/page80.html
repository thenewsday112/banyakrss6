The ultimate aim in Moon Sweepers is to create your own automated factory that makes you profit without your active participation. In simple terms, I am talking about automation and making full use of it. Let’s say the total space on the moon is limited, and an efficient design is going to make things easier.

Most efficient factory setup in Moon Sweepers

For this guide, you need to have access to all the endgame features. Until then, it’s best to do everything manually. For this setup, we will be using Capsule Catchers, Pressure Chambers, and Stone Presses. However, the aim is to maximize the efficiency of Conveyor Belts since you can connect at most 12 of them.

For our optimal setup, we will use two Capsule Catchers, four Pressure Chambers, and eight Stone Presses. However, let’s first understand how the method works by breaking down the setup.

Screenshot by Destructoid

First, place the Capsule Catcher. For maximum efficiency, you want to use four bots for a singe catcher. Next, there will be two sets of Conveyor Belts from the Capsule Catcher.

Screenshot by Destructoid

Place two Pressure Chambers. As I said, there are two sets of Conveyor Belts from the Capsule Catcher: One for Dust, and one for Sand.

Put a Splitter at one of the two Conveyor Belts connected to the Capsule Catcher. Draw two more belts from the Splitter and connect each to the Pressure Chambers. Now, the Capsule Catcher will send Dust to the Pressure Chambers and produce Moon Sand. We have one of our two key ingredients.

Screenshot by Destructoid

Next, place four Stone Presses. We will connect two Stone Presses with one Pressure Chamber. To do so, put a Splitter at the end of the Conveyor Belt that connects the two.

If I name my Pressure Chambers as PC1 and PC2 (suppose), Stone Press 1 and Stone Press 2 will be connected to PC1, and Stone Press 3 and 4 will be connected to PC2. Make sure that they’re connected to the correct paths (Sand must go to the terminals that accept Sand).

Screenshot by Destructoid

Now, put Splitters at the end of the Conveyor Belts that will deliver the Dust from the Capsule Catchers. Put the first Splitter to break the path into two paths, and one more on each to further split the two paths into four.

Each of these four belt paths will be connected to the Dust terminal of your Stone Presses. Now, your Stone Presses have Sand and Dust, and they can produce Moon Stone. This has the best rate of returns in terms of profits.

Finally, use Mergers to combine the output of all four Presses into a single Conveyor Belt that will be connected to your base.

For maximum efficiency, you’ll want to do this setup with two Capsule Catchers, four Pressure Chambers and eight Stone Presses. The method remains the same, but the design is just going to be more complex.

An alternate way to do the same setup is by using the garage. This applies if you are carrying most of the dust in your vehicles. In that case, you’ll want to replace the Capsule Catches with Garages. When you unload the dust, it will kickstart the same process that’s shown in the method above. However, this method is less optimum in my books as it isn’t fully automated.

Backbone of a beast – charting Nvidia's rise to networking titan

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia is more than just a leader in graphics and AI hardware. It has also become a titan of the networking industry with bigger datacenter Ethernet sales than any other vendor.

In Q2, Nvidia's networking revenues topped $17.5 billion. To put that in perspective, Cisco's revenue during that same period, across all products, was $17.3 billion.

GPUzilla's arrival as a networking power feels sudden, yet Jensen Huang started working towards this status in 2019 with the $6.9 billion acquisition of long-time networking partner Mellanox .

At the time, Mellanox wasn't a big name in Ethernet switching. The company was mostly known for its ConnectX NICs and InfiniBand switches. Outside of high-performance computing (HPC) and supercomputing clusters, and a big deployment at Microsoft, InfiniBand wasn't widely deployed in hyperscale datacenters.

Buying Mellanox allowed Nvidia to reposition itself as a system builder, which means something different in the context of distributed compute. Nvidia understood its datacenter customers weren't interested in buying GPUs or even servers; they wanted clusters of systems. For that, Nvidia needed interconnect and Mellanox's InfiniBand fit the bill.

Relentless co-design

Over the years, Huang has talked about co-design – designing components and software to work together for specific set of jobs rather than to be the best GPU, switch, or NIC. Its work with Mellanox is a prime example of that approach.

According to Dell'Oro Group analyst Sameh Boujelbene, Nvidia's decision to integrate compute and networking became key to the company's success.

"Customers choose Nvidia for its tightly integrated compute and networking architecture, optimized for AI workloads," she told The Register. That integration makes deployments easier and speeds time to market.

Ben Mabey, CTO of pioneering AI and machine learning company Recursion, told The Reg Nvidia's integration schemes won him over.

Founded in 2013, nearly a decade before ChatGPT made AI the subject of dinner-table discussion, Recursion sought to apply machine learning running atop GPUs to the problem of drug discovery.

This work involved churning through massive datasets which, Mabey tells us, now total more than 50 petabytes in size.

"When you think about our workloads, it makes LLMs workload training seem easy compared to the amount of data that has to be moved around," he told The Register.

"In the biological space and sciences in general, it's not well appreciated just how big the data actually is," he added. "The internet's big, but in the grand scheme of things, the text that people are training on is actually pretty small, versus the petabytes of data that we have of these images."

Recursion's first cluster was a system called BioHive Zero which it cobbled together from GTX 1080 graphics cards originally intended for gaming systems. It used Ethernet to connect GPUs and ran into the limits of conventional networking.

So, for its second cluster, an A100-based machine with around 300 accelerators, Recursion went with Nvidia's Quantum InfiniBand switches and ConnectX NICs.

As Mabey explains it, buying its compute and interconnects from the same vendor vastly simplified optimization and troubleshooting. When Recursion reached out to Nvidia for help tuning the system, they were able to consider it as a single system, rather than discrete compute and networking resources. This level of integration had only previously been offered by boutique supercomputer builders like Cray or Atos (Eviden/Bull/whatever it gets renamed next).

A wider Spectrum

Nvidia hasn't always listed networking revenue as a single line item in its financials. However, based on CFO commentary and a little back of the napkin math we can fill in the blanks.

Nvidia's networking revenues have grown steadily since the Mellanox acquisition. At two moments, its revenue shot up suddenly.

The first came in the 2023 calendar year (FY24) after OpenAI kicked off the AI gold rush with the launch of ChatGPT.

The second spread across 2024 and 2025, when Nvidia started selling Blackwell-generation GPUs and then started to ship them in large quantities.

"I see two major inflection points, both coinciding with the Blackwell platform ramp: the introduction of NVLink switches and the accelerating adoption of Spectrum-X Ethernet switches for AI back-end networks, driven by growing deployments of Nvidia GPU clusters and increased attach rates of Nvidia switches, NICs, optics, and cables," Boujelbene said.

It was around this time that users started deploy to Ethernet switches in large volume across Nvidia clusters. Elon Musk's Colossus supercomputer was one of the earliest examples.

As we reported at the time, the first half of the 200,000-chip H100 and H200-based cluster came online in 2024 and, at the time, was among the largest ever clusters deployed using Nvidia's Spectrum-X Ethernet switches.

Meta has also made extensive use of Nvidia's Spectrum switches in its own training clusters, including at its million-GPU Prometheus site in New Albany, Ohio.

There are a couple reasons Nvidia's Spectrum-X offering is doing so well.

First, through a combination of Spectrum Ethernet switches, superNICs, and some clever software optimizations, Nvidia was able to replicate many of the qualities that made InfiniBand so attractive while also benefiting from significantly higher radix and interoperability.

Ethernet switches, including Nvidia's own, have historically offered more ports. The higher the radix, the fewer switches are necessary to support a cluster at a given port speed.

This often gets misconstrued to mean that InfiniBand can't scale beyond clusters of a certain size, which isn't necessarily the case. But for clusters exceeding a certain number of GPUs, Ethernet is often more economical because it requires less equipment while achieving similar utilization.

That said, Nvidia's Spectrum-X isn't exactly unique. Broadcom, Cisco, AMD and others have managed to achieve utilization similar to InfiniBand through various investments in smartNICs and switches.

Nvidia's main differentiator, as Mabey and Boujelbene noted earlier, is the level of integration between the compute and networking.

The next frontier

Then Blackwell hit the market, enabling much greater scale and creating a surge in Nvidia's networking revenue.

Prior to Blackwell, Nvidia's NVLink and NVSwitch interconnect tech had largely been limited to single systems with up to eight GPUs. With the launch of the GB200 NVL72, these NVSwitches were broken out into dedicated appliances. (And yes, we remember GH200 NVL256 and NVL32, but from what we can tell these were never shipped in any meaningful volume, if at all.)

For every NVL72 rack system sold, Nvidia also sold nine NVSwitch trays, which enabled the system's six dozen GPUs to function as a single enormous GPU. This effectively guaranteed steady networking revenue growth: even if customers didn't buy Nvidia's scale-out switches, if they wanted NVL72 racks, they were automatically buying NVSwitch.

"We estimate that NVSwitch now accounts for roughly one-third of Nvidia’s networking revenue, up from virtually zero less than two years ago," Boujelbene told El Reg. "This underscores how quickly NVLink switching has become a major growth engine for Nvidia’s networking business."

Nvidia is now moving to grow its networking business even further with a new licensing program.

Until recently, Nvidia's NVSwitch tech was limited to its own accelerators. However, with the introduction of NVLink Fusion early last year, Nvidia opened the interconnect tech up to third parties to license.

Many chip designers, including Marvell, Qualcomm, Arm, Fujitsu, MediaTek, and d-Matrix, have announced plans to develop CPUs and XPUs using NVLink Fusion. The adoption of the interconnect opens the door to additional NVSwitch sales, as the chips using NVLink Fusion still rely on them for chip-to-chip communications, and there's no other place to get them, as Nvidia has yet to license out NVSwitch.

"NVLink Fusion allows Nvidia to extend its networking footprint beyond its own GPUs into the rapidly growing custom accelerator market. I see it as a strategic move to capture a larger share of AI networking spending, even in clusters that do not use Nvidia GPUs," Boujelbene said.

Nvidia's networking ambitions don't stop there.

"Nvidia continues to pursue opportunities in emerging networking domains as network requirements evolve to keep pace with increasingly demanding AI workloads," Boujelbene added. "The latest example is scale-in networking, designed to address the new requirements of agentic AI, while innovations such as CPO will help overcome growing bandwidth, power, and scalability challenges." ®

What’s Halloween without a little fun-gi. Arguably the most creepy of all fungi, Xylaria polymorpha can look like decaying zombie fingers creeping up from the forest floor. Commonly called dead man’s finger fungus, it and other similar sinister-looking fungi in the genus Xylaria grow throughout the United States. 

Take a look at at nine creepy pictures of this fungus—if you dare.

Dead man’s fingers are usually spotted at the bottom of decaying or dead trees. They break down deceased wood and release nutrients into the soil so that new plants can grow. Image: NPS/Bradburn. The finger-like shapes themselves are the fungi’s sexual reproductive structures. Image: Khlongwangchao via Getty Images. Like a real human finger, their exteriors can be rather stiff and strong. That firmness helps them to release spores and increase their chances of reproducing. Image: NPS Wheeler.
Xylaria are incredibly opportunistic genus and can release these spores for several months or even years. Image: NPS/Stiteler. The mushrooms grow from the forest floor among dry autumn oak leaves, with a mossy tree background in soft focus. Image: grechanyk via Getty Images. Simply plucking a dead man’s finger from the base of a tree doesn’t get rid of the fungi itself. It is merely the tip of a much larger organism.  Image: NPS. The creepy fungus is found on six continents. Image: Dr. John A Horsfall via Getty Images.
Some species of  Xylaria are known to cause black root rot, but that is typically a problem on trees or shrubs that were stressed already. Image: Riski Wayuningsih via Getty Images. Don’t worry. It’s completely harmless to humans, despite its walking dead-like appearance. Image: imageBROKER/Frank Leo via Getty Images.

5 things ancients knew about finding happiness

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Below, June Cohen shares five key insights from her new book, The Great Remembering: Powerful Ancient Practices―And the New Science Bringing Them Back.

Cohen is a pioneer in media and technology, best known as the co-creator of TED Talks, Masters of Scale, and Meditative Story. During her 11 years at TED, she cohosted the flagship event—working with hundreds of speakers on their talks—and launched TED Talks online. She’s earned more than 100 industry awards, including 67 Webby honors, 8 Apple “Podcast of the Year” awards, and a Peabody.

What’s the Big Idea?

Ancient cultures worldwide developed wisdom systems: embodied practices passed down by example that helped people navigate hardship, build connection, and find happiness. Modern science is now proving they work.

Listen to the audio version of this Book Bite—read by Cohen herself—in the Next Big Idea app, or buy the book.

1. An epic physical challenge can help you through an epic emotional one.

The first wisdom system that fascinated me was the epic physical challenge: a practice of pushing the body to its limits. To the Western mind, it doesn’t always make logical sense. But it was everywhere in the ancient world. In boys’ coming-of-age rituals, the boy might go on a hunt or swim an icy river. That physical challenge marked the emotional transition from boy to man. Or think of pilgrimage—a challenging journey—often taken during a difficult transition, like when you need to release grief or seek forgiveness. There is this ancient pattern of physical challenges pairing with emotional ones.

We still do this today. You get divorced, so you run a marathon. You recover from cancer, so you climb a mountain. We don’t know why, but we reach for something hard and physical when we face something hard and emotional.

New science tells us our instincts are exactly right. It’s the science of “hormetic stress,” or good stress. This isn’t emotional stress—it’s the physical stress of something genuinely dangerous: extreme exertion, fasting, intense heat or cold. When your body encounters these challenges, it triggers a chain reaction of renewal that goes down to the cellular level. One of these processes is called autophagy—sometimes described as “spring cleaning” for cells. The scientist who discovered it won the 2016 Nobel Prize in Medicine.

But the most interesting thing about an epic physical challenge is what happens in the brain. If you have read Michael Pollan’s book on psychedelics, How to Change Your Mind, you know the most sought-after effect of psychedelics is neuroplasticity—the brain’s ability to create new connections. An epic physical challenge also creates neuroplasticity. Mark Mattson of Johns Hopkins told me, “We and others have evidence that your nerve cells actually grow and form new synapses in response to physical exercise”—and to a lesser extent, intermittent fasting. This isn’t just the motor cortex; it includes regions like the hippocampus, which governs learning and memory. The neuroplasticity from an epic physical challenge might help rewrite your story when you’re going through a big emotional challenge.

That might be why so many people you know run marathons, go on pilgrimage, or sign up for weeklong fasting, silent meditation, or darkness retreats. The ancients knew that taking on an epic physical challenge when you’re faced with an epic emotional challenge is one way to make yourself happy.

2. There’s a right way (and a wrong way) to take on epic physical challenges.

Think of this as a public service announcement from your ancestors: Across cultures and across time, the same elements determine whether an epic physical challenge is performed successfully.

  • Structure. An epic physical challenge is dangerous. It can kill you if you’re not careful. You need guardrails that push you without harming you.
  • Meaning. Who will you be on the other side? What transition will it see you through? Symbolism is a huge part of why it works.
  • A coach. Someone who knows the ropes and believes in you.
  • A witnessing community. People who can say, “You’ve done it. You’ve served us.” We humans have a deep need to be witnessed and to serve.
  • Nature. Thousands of studies show that just 20 minutes in a green space can boost you biologically.
  • Recovery. Cells repair, and brain cells grow during recovery, not during the challenge itself. If you skip the recovery, you skip the growth.
  • The squad. The people you train with, strain with, and cross the finish line with.

These different elements combine to create something holistic and human. Each of us needs slightly different things at different times to help us through tough transitions. These elements transform an epic physical challenge from something you endured into a wisdom system—something that works on you at every level.

3. Your body is a powerful pathway to your mind and your mood.

Ancient festivals were extraordinarily physical: People traveled great distances, climbed mountains, fasted, stayed up all night, danced for hours. The message was clearly that spiritual transformation begins with the body.

If that sounds too mystical, here’s the science: The single clearest explanation, in one word, is endorphins. The ancients didn’t use that word, but they had an exquisite understanding of how endorphins work and how essential they are to our happiness.

You’ve probably heard of endorphins as our natural pain reliever—the body’s own opioid. You may know them as the source of the “runner’s high,” that flood of good feeling that long-distance runners experience mid-run. (I ran cross-country in high school and never felt anything like a high during a run, but I did notice the post-run glow, that sense of Oh, I can handle life. It turns out the “post-run high” is actually more common.) That’s what endorphins feel like: a quiet confidence, a lift. Robin Dunbar at Oxford told me they’re the most effective antidepressant you can get, and hundreds of studies back him up.

But exercise isn’t the only way to access endorphins. We get them any time we encounter physical pain—including extreme heat or cold, as in a sauna or cold plunge. We get them during sex. And, most fascinatingly, we get them through synchrony with other people.

Modern Western culture tends to treat the brain and body as separate, with the brain firmly in charge. You might trace that assumption to René Descartes, the 17th-century philosopher who declared mind and body to be distinct substances. One scientist I interviewed put it bluntly: “He has a lot to answer for, because there is no mind-body distinction.” The ancients knew this. Science is finally catching up.

4. Don’t go it alone.

Let me start here with a story. It’s a story about ultramarathoner Lisa Hallett. She’s setting the pace for all six in her group as they descend in darkness toward the Carbon River. Lisa can smell the river long before she can see or hear it. It’s the 18th hour of a continuous 150K run around Mount Rainier in Washington state. She and her team will climb and descend a total of 27,000 feet before they sleep again.

Lisa is hungry—her food ran out hours ago—and hunger makes it hard to regulate your temperature. She told me, “Am I cold? Am I hot? It’s too much. And then you start spiraling. I can’t do this. This is too hard.”

Then, three miles from the next aid station, she hears her 15-year-old daughter giggling. Her daughter Heidi and a friend have run in to meet her, carrying peanut butter and jelly and snacks, fresh and positive. Lisa feels a rush of gratitude and a surge of new energy. She picks up the pace as her group runs in unison to the campground.

This is not just emotional. It is physical and measurable. Along with the runner’s high, there is something Robin Dunbar’s lab at Oxford identified as the rower’s high: When people row together rather than alone, they produce twice as many endorphins.

And here’s the best part: You don’t have to row or run to get it. We produce endorphins any time we synchronize with other people—singing in harmony, line dancing, laughing together, or even eating a shared feast (apparently you have to eat until you’re painfully full for it to trigger the effect). This is why motorcyclists ride in packs, why yoga feels easier in a class than in your living room, and why group singing has been shown to function as an antidepressant.

5. Modern science is validating what you already know in your bones.

When I first began this investigation, I expected tension between ancient wisdom and modern science. What I found instead was an emerging partnership. Many scientists are using modern tools to validate the traditional systems they grew up with, and intuitively know to work.

Scientists in Finland proved the profound health benefits of the sauna and cold plunge. Muslim scientists have been proving the health benefits of intermittent fasting, as practiced during Ramadan. Scientists in Japan have shown the physiological health benefits of forest bathing.

I didn’t grow up with the sauna or Ramadan. I learned about those benefits from studies. But I did grow up in a house surrounded by trees that my father planted himself. When I read forest bathing studies, I thought: I know that works.

That’s been the response I’ve heard most often since starting to share this work. After one talk, a couple came up to me. The woman said she had elbowed her husband during my section on group dancing: “See—we should dance more.” Her husband had elbowed her right back during the nature section: “See—we should hike more.” They both already knew what made them happy. The science just gave them permission to act on it.

And the beauty of all of this is: You probably kind of already know this stuff. Because while the science and history might be new to you, you probably already know in your bones that these things make you happy. When we look through this dual lens—on one hand, this is what people have always done, and on the other, this is what science tells us—it gives us a powerful mirror to hold up to our own lives, and remember.
