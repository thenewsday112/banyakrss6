Luciano Spalletti is ready to shift from a 3-5-2 to a 3-4-2-1 at Genoa, with Alajbegovic among the options behind the striker. With eight players injured and Napoli due on Sunday, he expects his team to stay broadly the same.

Luciano Spalletti is ready to tweak his shape at Genoa without tearing up his team. On the eve of the trip, the Juventus coach said one very small positional change turns the 3-5-2 into a 3-4-2-1. Anyone, he added, can fill the two roles behind the striker.

He named Alajbegovic, Boga and Zhegrova as options in that zone. Kolo Muani was his example of the profile he has in mind. Spalletti said the forward can play partly as a number 10 and partly as a wide forward. Sarr has recovered and is available.

Tuttosport expects Alajbegovic to possibly replace Kolo Muani behind Woltemade, and sees Cambiaso as the only left-flank option with Celik injured.

Napoli on Sunday shapes the plan for Genoa

The fixture list explains the caution. The trip to Genoa is the first of two games in five days. Spalletti is weighing a few rotations partly because of the second of those games, Sky Sport reported. Asked about Napoli, the coach said everything has to be taken into account. He still expects the team to stay broadly the same.

Spalletti also said his players are fully focused and have the right mentality. That follows a mixed run. Juventus lost at home to Lazio on 18 October. Afterwards Spalletti said his side had shown fragility that starts in the mind. He added that tactical systems change nothing without hunger, passion, courage and character. A draw with Rennes followed.

The win at Lecce began to make up for both results. McKennie volleyed in a Douglas Luiz free kick in the 29th minute. Douglas Luiz added the second four minutes before half-time.

Genoa’s set-piece threat meets a strong Juventus record

Spalletti described Genoa as an extremely hard opponent, citing both the atmosphere at their ground and their tactics. He warned they can use their physical strength at set pieces and also run in behind defences. Above all, he said, the side carries Daniele De Rossi’s fighting spirit.

The warning on dead balls fits recent meetings between the clubs. Six of the last ten goals between the clubs came from set pieces: two from corners, one from a throw-in and three from penalties.

Vanja Matijevec, 79, March 3

Vanja Matijevec belonged to a generation of Slovenian alpinists who pushed onto some of the biggest walls in the Himalayas, but one of the defining moments of his climbing life came when he chose not to summit.

On his legendary 1979 expedition to Everest, Matijevec gave up his own planned summit attempt to help Stipe Božić, Stane Belak and Ang Phu, who had become stranded above 8,000 meters during their descent. Božić later remembered Matijevec’s selflessness as helping him survive after he lost a crampon high on the mountain.

Born in Ljubljana on October 14, 1946, Matijevec first encountered the Slovenian mountains as a child, tending cattle on pastures above Tržič. He came to alpinism later, after meeting climber Jože Hočevar through his field work, and began training as a mountain guide in 1975. In 1979, Matijevec, Lado Vidmar and Blaž Oblak made the first ascent of Lucifer (WI4+), a roughly 200-meter frozen waterfall in Gozd Martuljek. The climb, completed with equipment Matijevec later remembered as rudimentary, helped usher in the era of modern ice climbing in Slovenia.

But Matijevec’s ambitions stretched far beyond Slovenia. In 1981, Matijevec and Franček Knez climbed to the rim of Lhotse’s immense South Face, then considered one of the great unsolved problems of Himalayan alpinism. Over the course of his life, Matijevec accumulated more than 30 first ascents, including a direct new route up the West Face of Trisul in 1987. Despite his achievements, his peers remember his kindness more than anything. Fellow Slovenian alpinist Viki Grošelj, who had known Matijevec since their early climbing days, remembered him after his death as a distinctive personality and, simply, “the right man in the right place,” he told Slovenian newspaper Delo.

We Need to Move Past the “Shut Up And Die Like a Man” Mentality in the Mountains

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“Shut up and die like a man!” sounds like a line from a bad action movie, but we all heard it on the radio from the paragliding competition director. The director was yelling at a terrified pilot who thought he was falling to his death. The pilot had accidentally keyed open his radio’s shoulder mic, allowing his frantic screams to be broadcast across the competition frequency.

The unfortunate pilot begging to god to not die had collapsed his wing and plummeted in front of my eyes. Twenty-one years later, the memory of this sight and soundtrack is unforgettable, and so was the director’s barking, because there was clearly no sympathy for what the pilot thought was his final, horrific ride. Miraculously, the pilot recovered his wing and flew out to land and reflect on his life choices alone.

Most pilots didn’t think the competition director’s statement was out of line for the time. Many still don’t. I see terrible parallels between flying culture and some facets of climbing’s; in alpinism especially, some see death as an attractant, or an inevitability. Over my 40 years as an alpinist and 35 as a pilot, I’ve watched climbers experience gut-wrenching trauma—then do their best to ignore it, too scared or proud to deal with the ugly, messy truth. This avoidance sends a message to younger climbers, much like the competition director’s statement so long ago: Suck it up, move on, and die with honor if things go bad.

But living with trauma doesn’t have to be that way.

***

When my late friend Ken Wallator was 21, he climbed Mount Belanger in the Canadian Rockies with his girlfriend Heidi. They reached the top of the difficulties, unroped, and walked along a low-angle snow ridge to the summit of the peak. But the snow ridge was actually a massive, overhanging cornice of windblown snow, and it collapsed as they stopped to take a photo. Ken somehow managed to leap to safety but Heidi fell hundreds of meters down the face.

Ken, a brave bear of a man, downclimbed the face, found Heidi somehow still alive, and started carrying her out to a distant hut. She died before they reached it.

In the aftermath of Heidi’s death, I watched Ken receive a surprising amount of blame from grieving members of the climbing community. Didn’t he know better than to walk on a cornice? Why weren’t they roped up? Ken was already wracked with guilt from the accident. When his community piled on, their condemnation lit an internal firestorm.

Ken Wallator at a belay in the Canadian Rockies. (Photo: Will Gadd)

Thirty years later, in 2019, Ken helped me re-roof my house. He still had the strength of a bear and I watched him haul 100-pound loads of shingles up my rickety ladder. In a rare moment of emotionality, he told me how he still hadn’t gotten over Heidi’s death, or the climbing community’s blame. We talked more about trauma, and I realized only then how that day on Mount Belanger broke my friend in a way that never healed.

As two young, hungry alpinists, Ken and I didn’t know what mental “help” looked like 30 years ago, and Ken was too much a product of his culture to take it 30 years later. Despite his immense physical and mental strength, Ken died by suicide a few weeks after our talk.

***

More recently, Will Stanhope’s death has brought to mind this idea of “asking for help” once more. For years Stanhope had battled desperately with alcohol. He was facing various charges at the time of his death—including assault and sexual assault—while still succeeding on difficult routes as a professional climber and mountain guide.

In our last real conversation, Stanhope talked about needing to “harden up and stop letting the carnage get to me” one minute, then sobbed about the “death camp” of hard alpine climbing the next. Like Ken and me, he had lost many friends in the mountains.

“You and I [watched] our mentors and friends die, one after another, as if everyone around us was climbing their way to oblivion,” his friend Kieran Brownie wrote in an obituary. “We couldn’t return to the mountains, we couldn’t return to each other.”

Stanhope carried a heavy weight in the final months of his life, made even heavier by grief, guilt, and irreversible mistakes. After a lifetime of hard solos and even scarier roped climbs, it seemed impossible that Stanhope would ever fall off an easy 5.11. But when he did just that last April and succumbed to a head injury, to me and other friends, Stanhope’s death felt more related to the weight he carried in his head than the climb itself. His family and friends made truly profound and sometimes wrenchingly difficult efforts to help him, but, like falling, the weights of addiction and trauma develop their own momentum.

Will Gadd (left) and Will Stanhope (center, in white) surrounded by friends after a day of climbing in China.

Hard drinking, hard living, and celebrating dangerous acts—usually lauded as “bold”—are hallmarks of every mountain sport I’ve ever done, from kayaking to flying to skiing to climbing. Our literature is full of stories celebrating those who climbed hard, drank hard, and died silently.

I can’t help but wonder if our literature strives to make these deaths seem somehow valiant and necessary, like dying in the mountains is a noble sacrifice rather than the tragedy it really is. The classic mountaineering texts rarely explore the trauma that comes after seeing a friend die. They barrel ever onward to the summit.

***

Military veterans and first responders have a trauma-treatment framework that climbers should strive to adopt. Soldiers are not considered “weak” if they are affected by bloody combat. Paramedics are not ridiculed for taking leave after responding to a grisly car wreck. These are normal human responses, and professional groups typically have protocols for helping those involved in traumatic situations. These groups have no other choice; a traumatized team simply cannot function.

Those professional protocols weren’t there for Ken, Will, that terrified pilot, or many others, including me.

When I was 19 I tried with every tool I knew to save a fellow kayaker stuck just inches from the surface in his boat. I couldn’t reach him, and he drowned in front of me as I tried to remember how to rig a better haul system. I had nightmares for years of the paddler chasing me in the dark beside that river.

Only decades later did I seek help, after a divorce and a lot of behavior I understood even less than that terrible accident. I’m lucky I survived and sought belated help. Ken and Will and many others didn’t. Many more in our community—your friends and mine—are barely standing today, carrying crippling loads. They need help putting those loads down.

The pain of trauma does not disappear with six or 600 beers. Doing dangerous climbs or flights or other high-stimulus experiences temporarily distracts from the weight, but doesn’t solve it. There is no pack heavier than one filled with trauma you can’t put down.

One of the smartest things I’ve ever heard about trauma came after we failed to resuscitate a dying man at the base of an ice climb a few years ago. A professional responder on the scene could tell I was shaken, and looked me in the eye and said, “Post traumatic stress is normal; post traumatic stress disorder is optional—if you get help. Get help.”

I did.

***

I see real change today, from my wife Sarah Hueniken’s Mountain Muskox foundation to the American Alpine Club’s Grief Fund, two organizations built to support mountain-trauma survivors.

But I also still see a lot of the “Shut up and die like a man” attitude, and the celebration of “bold” yet physically unimpressive climbs that are often little more than Russian Roulette. Huge, low-angle alpine routes with immense serac and avalanche hazard are not that cool, in my book. And I see many climbers who still romanticize the wrong parts of climbing, whether that’s meaningless objective hazard or the very idea of “suffering silently.”

We need to recognize that a mountain death doesn’t end with the funeral; often that’s just the start of a very long and difficult process for everyone involved. In climbing more people die on the descent after the summit than on the climb itself.

At times I’ve felt deeply angry about our outdoor tribe’s celebration of “bold” hazards, and our denial of the after-effects of engaging in high-risk sports where participants die regularly. We often celebrate the bold free solos or dangerous alpine routes where everything clicked into place. We never celebrate the dead parent who doesn’t show up for their kids’ birthdays.

But the truth is that I laughed when the paragliding competition director said his infamous line. I promised myself that if I was in that situation in the future, I’d shut up. That became my normal. Today, I hope I’d speak up on the comment, and reach out to the guy who almost died that day. Because I’ve been him, and it sucks, and I’m trying to do better.

I’m less angry today when I think about Ken, Will, or others who have left the game, and just hope that our culture continues to evolve in the same way the first responders did, and that we ultimately celebrate survival more than dying silently. We can do better, if we want to.

Finally, if, like me, you’ve been through the trauma wringer, it often takes help to sort it out. We’re not tougher than the veterans or first responders even if our culture says we “should” be—we’re just less educated about intense trauma.

The goal is to return to full function and fly in life, not get stuck in the inevitable mental dust devils of mountain death. Peace to those hurting, it can get better with help. Substances and more dangerous living may distract us short term, but long term the combo is deadly despite the romantic words we historically place on both. I write these words for the future Wills and Kens, they are me, you, our friends.

Shutting up is not a solution, because silence is death.

What It Takes to Be Free

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We spend our lives trying to find our power and our freedom. It is not easy: We live in a culture that has distorted the meaning and measure of power, confusing visibility with value, status with substance, attention with love; we live in a universe where free will may be an illusion, where chance deals the vast majority of the cards — the family and epoch we are born into, the body and biochemistry we live in, the people we cross paths with, the accidents of chemistry and happenstance we call love. The great double-edged question is how to keep power from becoming a prison and how much freedom we can find between the narrow parameters of our givens. But somewhere deep down, in the marrow of our language, we seem to know: The word power shares its Middle Latin root with possibility — its only measure is that which liberates the possible in us; our only power is our freedom.

Art from An Almanac of Birds: 100 Divinations for Uncertain Days, also available as a stand-alone print and as stationery cards.

“Nothing is more unbearable, once one has it, than freedom,” James Baldwin wrote. It is unbearable largely because it demands a shedding of the self with all of its attachments — to ways of being, to ideas about how the world ought to be, to particular interpretations of reality. Such attachments are shackles best broken by what Irish Murdoch has so splendidly termed unselfing, key to each of the five kinds of freedom she identified in literature and life. For Murdoch, freedom takes radical unsefling — only then are we truly free to both be ourselves and love each other:

Freedom is exercised in the confrontation by each other, in the context of an infinitely extensible work of imaginative understanding, of two irreducibly dissimilar individuals. Love is the imaginative recognition of, that is respect for, this otherness.

This is why, for Octavio Paz — who thought deeply about freedom — love is “a knot made of two intertwined freedoms.” And if love is our fulcrum of belonging, then belonging itself is woven of the same paradox — something Maya Angelou articulates in one of her finest interviews:

You only are free when you realize you belong no place — you belong every place — no place at all.

Art by Ofra Amit for The Dark Wood of the Golden Birds by Margaret Wise Brown

In this sense, freedom is the hinge between being and belonging that swings open the door to being ourselves — which, yet again paradoxically, must include the freedom to change, to confront the illusory solidity of the self, its ceaseless mutability. Psychoanalyst Karen Horney formulates this perfectly in her pioneering work on the fundaments of personal growth:

We do not need an inner strait jacket with which to shackle our spontaneity, nor the whip of inner dictates to drive us to perfection. There is no doubt that such disciplinary methods can succeed in suppressing undesirable factors, but there is also no doubt that they are injurious to our growth. We do not need them because we see a better possibility of dealing with destructive forces in ourselves: that of actually outgrowing them. The way toward this goal is an ever increasing awareness and understanding of ourselves. Self-knowledge, then, is not an aim in itself, but a means of liberating the forces of spontaneous growth… And as we lose the neurotic obsession with self, as we become free to grow ourselves, we also free ourselves to love and to feel concern for other people.

Then, and only then, can we contact the soul beneath the self, which is the only real locus of our freedom. Vassily Grossman — who spent a thousand days as a correspondent on the frontlines of the Holocaust — captures this beautifully in a passage from his almost unbearably poignant reckoning with life and death:

What constitutes the freedom, the soul of an individual life, is its uniqueness. The reflection of the universe in someone’s consciousness is the foundation of his or her power, but life only becomes happiness, is only endowed with freedom and meaning when someone exists as a whole world that has never been repeated in all eternity. Only then can they experience the joy of freedom and kindness, finding in others what they have already found in themselves.

If You Spot OTR on a Truck, Here’s What It Means

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Trucks are full of weird and useful features that most people overlook without knowing their real purpose. Some have long spikes on their wheels. Others might feature amber lights on top. You might even see diamond shaped signs. But what about OTR written on a truck?

What can look like a government code can have a specific meaning in the trucking industry. Learn what OTR means, how long-haul work compares with shorter routes, and why the context matters.

What Does OTR Mean When It’s Written on a Truck?

In trucking, OTR stands for “over-the-road.” It refers to long-haul trucking, where drivers transport freight across long distances, often through several states or into Canada. According to trucking company Schneider, OTR drivers can spend weeks away from home. Many drive trucks with sleeper cabs that provide a place to rest between shifts.

OTR describes the type of work, not a specific truck. It’s also not a required marking. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) requires certain identifying information, including a company’s name and USDOT number, on interstate commercial trucks, but OTR isn’t part of that requirement.

The letters can also mean something different depending on where they appear. In the tire industry, OTR stands for “off-the-road,” referring to heavy-duty tires used on construction and mining equipment. OTR Diagnostics is also the name of a maintenance tool that helps truck owners check engine problems and other vehicle information.

What Does an OTR Truck Driver Do?

OTR truck drivers transport freight over long distances, often making several stops along the way. Their jobs can also involve inspecting their trucks, handling delivery paperwork, and sometimes helping load or unload cargo.

Because these trips can last for weeks, drivers often sleep in their trucks or stop at other places along their routes. The FMCSA limits how long commercial drivers can drive without taking required breaks and rest periods.

Drivers who operate most full-size tractor-trailers need a Class A commercial driver’s license (CDL). Depending on the company and route, drivers may spend several weeks working before returning home.

What’s the Difference Between OTR, Regional, and Local Trucking?

OTR drivers cover the longest distances and may spend weeks away from home. Regional drivers work within a particular area of the country, often returning home weekly or more frequently. Local drivers generally stay closer to home and return after each shift, although they can still work long hours.

There’s also dedicated trucking, where drivers regularly transport freight for the same customer or between the same locations. These routes can be local, regional, or long-distance, depending on the company.

The exact distances and schedules vary between employers, so the terms don’t always describe the same mileage or amount of time away from home.
