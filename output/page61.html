Why some Amazon communities welcome the oil companies threatening their water

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Oil drilling in the Amazon is often portrayed as a battle between Indigenous communities and powerful energy companies.

But along Ecuador’s Lower Napo River, some communities facing the expansion of a major oil project were negotiating for something unexpected: clean drinking water.

They already depended on a polluted river, and they feared that more drilling would make conditions worse. Yet the company responsible for the new extraction also offered something the government had failed to provide.

Julie Dayot spent eight months in 2016 and 2017 living with Quichua communities while the Ishpingo-Tambococha-Tiputini oil project, known as ITT, expanded through their stretch of the river.

She began the work as a doctoral student in Oxford’s Department of International Development and is now at the University of Exeter‘s Environment and Sustainability Institute.

“When I first met people from Quichua communities, I was surprised that many wanted the oil company to come,” Dayot said.

“I wanted to understand why. Over time, I saw how the costs and benefits of oil extraction are spread unevenly over space and time.”

The river came polluted

The Napo is the villages’ tap. Families drink from it, cook with it, wash in it and brew chicha, the fermented yucca drink, from it.

By the time the project reached them, residents already considered the river polluted by sewage, agricultural runoff and earlier oil drilling upstream.

Older residents still talked about a 1987 spill from the Texaco era, when, as two of them put it, “no one would give us even a gallon of water.”

Fish were getting scarcer, and so was bush meat.

In Vicente Salazar, Dayot found that monthly household cash income ranged from $95 to $140. Residents struggled to afford improvements such as metal roofs and motorized canoes.

These pressures made the company’s financial support attractive long before drilling reached the communities.

Oil became the provider

The state operator, Petroamazonas, committed about $5.5 million in social compensation across the six communities inside the project’s zone of influence, on top of cash payments and jobs.

The funding was intended to support water systems, electricity, transport, schooling, training and agricultural projects.

One village of 480 people negotiated about $1.25 million in projects, while another community of 95 people secured $155,000.

Most of those are things a government normally provides. The company also paid for the health center in Tiputini and donated medicine and fuel for emergencies.

Several people told Dayot that local officials steered communities toward asking the company rather than the municipality.

An anthropologist in the company’s community relations office summed it up for her. “We (also) represent the state, which has been absent here,” he said.

“One particularly striking example is drinking water,” Dayot said.

“Several communities therefore asked the oil company for drinking water systems, both to address an existing problem and because they expected the new extraction to further contaminate the river.”

Oil extraction threatened the communities’ water supply, yet the industry also offered a way to protect it. That contradiction challenges the usual narrative of Indigenous resistance to drilling.

Harm travels, money stays

Only six communities were entitled to compensation, even though pollution could spread beyond the project’s boundaries and affect villages that received nothing.

In Vicente Salazar, just outside the project line, residents called themselves “affected” months before the company arrived. They meant affected by exclusion.

“We are affected,” one man said, “because they don’t give us jobs.”

When the company turned up in January 2017 to announce a new seismic survey, it did so during the monthly village work day, between the beer and the volleyball. There was no outcry.

One woman told Dayot the company needed to hurry up because she had no money for trousers. A man she asked about a vote shook his head.

“It’s not like we are going to oppose,” he said. “What it’s all about is negotiating what we wanna ask.”

When Dayot returned in 2023, she found that all six communities still supported oil extraction, despite the environmental risks.

The cost of acceptance

Dayot’s research focused on a small number of communities along one stretch of the Lower Napo River. Whether the same patterns exist elsewhere remains unclear.

The future of the ITT oil field remains uncertain. Ecuadorians voted in August 2023 to end extraction in Yasuní National Park, but production continued into September 2026, despite the referendum.

The study raises questions that extend beyond the fate of one oil field.

For communities along the Lower Napo River, accepting drilling was not simply a choice between environmental protection and economic development.

It was also a way to secure clean water, jobs, and essential services that had long been out of reach.

“We need to think beyond the decisions themselves to the uneven conditions under which acceptance happens,” Dayot said.

Her findings expose a troubling contradiction: Communities facing the environmental risks of oil extraction may depend on the same industry to provide the basic necessities they need.

The full study was published in the journal Geoforum.

A sky-high Vegas bar, a chic new Marriott in South Africa and more hotel news

Here in New York City, it's one of the absolute best weekends of the year: Halloween and the New York City Marathon. Last year, a team of TPG staff came out to the race to cheer me on while I ran, which was a very caring way for my work family to spend their free time.

And speaking of free time, Club Med recently commissioned a study of 2,000 Americans and Canadians to find how people actually feel about their free time — and what they do with it. One statistic in the report really resonated with me: 81% of North Americans miss the sense of wonder they had as kids more than the free time they had then.

So this week I'm sharing some hotel news that speaks to that sense of wonder we all apparently long for, like a chic new hotel at the very tip of Africa to a revamped, sky-high cocktail bar with views of the iconic Las Vegas strip.

Here's the hotel news you need to know about.

Edition opens in South Africa

MARRIOTT

One of the most anticipated hotel openings of the year, The Cape Town Edition, is now open. Set on the V&A Waterfront, the stylish hotel is the brand's first outpost in Africa. At the property, guests can stay in one of 142 rooms and suites, enjoy art and design selected as "a reflection of Africa's creative landscape," and eat at numerous dining and bar outlets. And for people like me who love to relax, the hotel has a spa.

One of New York's most famous hotels is getting a spa

THE MARK

In November, New York's celebrity-loved hotel The Mark is getting its first spa: The Mark Wellness & Longevity Spa by Augustinus Bader. The Mark is already The Met Gala's fashion hot spot, plus home to caviar-loaded baked potatoes at Caviar Kaspia, so it only makes sense to have a luxe spa. The spa, the first Augustinus Bader-branded location in the U.S., will feature three treatment rooms, state-of-the-art and science-backed treatments, plus in-room massage services. The opening comes hot on the heels of The Mark's recent room updates, making the hotel as fresh — and relaxing — as ever.

W Las Vegas revamps and reopens a dreamy rooftop bar

MARRIOTT

In 2024, Marriott took over the former Delano hotel at Mandalay Bay and converted it into the W Las Vegas. Now, two years later, the W brand is really making the hotel its own with the relaunch of Skyfall Rooftop Bar & Lounge. Situated on the 64th floor and offering panoramic views of Sin City, Rockwell Group recreated the space with bold interiors accenting the floor-to-ceiling windows. In the hotel's lobby, the Living Room bar has also been reimagined.

"Together, these spaces sophisticatedly blend glamour, futuristic energy, and immersive nightlife to capture the true spirit of Las Vegas while embodying the bold, playful quality signature to a W hotel," W Las Vegas said in a statement shared with TPG.

This Delaware hotel wants you to embrace the 'Year of the Horse' — literally

HOTEL DU PONT

Going back to how we spend our free time, Club Med's report also shared that 88% of people experienced at least one positive impact the last time they challenged themselves to try something new. If you want to feel that positive impact, it might be time to go ... to Delaware.

Hotel Du Pont in Wilmington has launched the Equestrian Escape Package, which comes with equestrian-centric loaner pieces, like riding boots and jackets, from The Frye Company; a private riding lesson at the 200-acre Carousel Park Equestrian Center; and a gourmet picnic from the hotel's Le Cavalier French brasserie.

Thompson Rome is finally open

HYATT

We've all been patiently waiting for the 69-room Thompson Rome, the first property from the brand in Italy, to open its doors. Well, that day has finally come. The hotel, right near Piazza Venezia and other major attractions, features restored elements from the hotel's original building, a contemporary Mediterranean restaurant, a rooftop cocktail bar and a lobby bar.

It’s scary how watchable these stories are

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I’ve never been great at the whole Halloween thing. By the time I remember it’s happening, all the good sweets are gone from grocery store shelves, so any visiting trick-or-treaters get butterscotch suckers, breath mints or candy corn. I don’t do costume parties. I never got the point of carving pumpkins.

But movie and TV marathons? Sign me up – along with a lot of other homebodies. The horror genre is bigger than ever, according to a San Francisco Chronicle report that says this year’s domestic box office reached nearly $1.3 billion as of the start of October.

Horror’s surge has also yielded more films designed to play with our perception as opposed to the “Saw”-style torture porn dominating years past. Think “Obsession” or “Backrooms,” both of which are among the 10 highest-earning movies of 2026 thus far. That’s good news for anyone who isn’t partial to splatter flicks or slashers but still enjoys a creep show that makes you think, especially ones that are more thoughtful than scary. If that’s your speed, the following options may be perfect for your reclusive Samhain.

“Black Zombie”

I will bet good money the first bong-ripping undergrad to declare that all horror is metaphor cited George Romero’s “Night of the Living Dead” as a prime example. That’s not wrong; Romero’s 1968 benchmark established the modern cinematic zombie as a stand-in for mindless capitalist consumption. But “Black Zombie” rewinds to the monster’s Haitian roots to examine an even darker origin tied to that country’s shared history with ours.

You may be wondering why I’d recommend a documentary in a horror roundup, let alone one that explains how the modern movie flesh gobbler began as a manifestation of white fear. The answer is that Maya Annik Bedward’s film doubles as an impressive cinematic syllabus.

By braiding America’s media history with its national history, Bedward and her assemblage of academics, creatives, authors and Vodou practitioners invite us to think differently about the prejudices driving movies like, say, Victor Halperin’s 1932 effort “White Zombie,” or 1943’s “I Walked with a Zombie.”

“Black Zombie” opens with a truth that has long escaped those fascinated by the shambling undead. To some, they’re simply mindless hunters. But Haitians understand the horror of zombies to be one of dispossession, a curse enacted by enslavers that binds victims to mindless servitude, denying them liberation in the afterlife. But long after Romero transformed them from blank-eyed puppets to ravenous murder machines, Bedward’s film demonstrates how Hollywood’s version of the zombie impacts America’s perception of Vodou, Haitians and immigration.

“Black Zombie” not only asks us to think differently about an overexposed subgenre, but also thoughtfully examines Vodou’s central role in the revolution that drove out French colonizers, and France’s imposition of debt to punish Haitians for winning their freedom.

All of this informs the zombie’s continued proliferation across popular culture, in films like “World War Z” and shows such as “The Last of Us” and “The Walking Dead.” They’re terrific entertainment, certainly. But each is, in its own way, built upon decades’ worth of misrepresenting Haiti’s syncretic faith to the detriment and demonization of a people. At the same time, “Black Zombie” recognizes this subgenre’s cultural resilience, arguing that recognizing the zombie’s true roots broadens its narrative possibilities.

Proving that point, one of Bedward’s subjects, a Vodou priest, says he grew up loving Wes Craven’s “The Serpent and the Rainbow,” despite its message that visitors to his country risk having their nuts nailed to a chair.  “It’s a fantastical world to scare children,” he says, before adding that his main problem is its inaccurate messaging about his faith. “We say, ‘Wow – Haitians are devils.’ That’s what we’re taught.”

I’d nearly forgotten about that movie. Now I might rewatch it for the first time in decades and expect to be horrified for entirely different reasons.

“Carrie”

Stephen King’s classic may be more than half a century old and has undergone multiple adaptations, but it endures because its central moral that high school is hell never ages. Still, Mike Flanagan’s interpretation has divided the audience. Some, including King, appreciate Flanagan’s kinder portrait of Carrie White (Summer H. Howell) as an innocent unjustly sequestered from the world by fundamentalist parents who repudiate her extraordinary power. Others disagree with Flanagan’s alteration to the story’s climactic scene, which shifts culpability for the prom massacre from Carrie to a character created expressly for the series.

Pretty much everyone questions the season finale, which hints at taking an “X-Men”-style turn for its recently greenlit second season.

As someone who accepts nothing is sacrosanct now, not to mention a fan of the creator’s other adaptations, including “The Fall of the House of Usher,” I appreciated Flanagan using Carrie’s story to critique ignorance, knowledge suppression, and the dehumanizing effect of social media.

Besides, nearly all the key elements of “Carrie” remain. The bullies are still awful, and the famous “plug it up” shower scene in Brian DePalma’s 1976 standard-setter is worsened by her tormentors posting Carrie’s frightened sobs on social media.

Some changes retain the spirit of past performances. Carrie’s mother Margaret (Samantha Sloyan) is still an abusive religious zealot fond of locking her daughter in a prayer closet, for instance. But after the law forces her homeschooled girl to join the festering sin den that is their local public school, Margaret throws herself into advocating for parents’ rights, entirely disregarding her teen’s desire for a “normal” adolescence.

Part of the additional fun Flanagan has with this marquee property is strewing cultural Easter Eggs throughout. Some nod to what’s happening in America, such as when Carrie discovers the school library and gravitates straight to books that have been banned from classrooms and community libraries across the country. Another winks at “Army of Darkness” fans; look for the logo on Margaret’s work uniform.

By the time we reach Carrie’s bloodbath, we may be left to question whether she’s simply a bullied victim pushed beyond her limit or some version of a Final Girl empowered to vanquish tormentors who earn their reckoning. Even if you don’t ultimately see Howell’s Carrie that way, it’s a fine dance with one of horror’s oldest friends.

“Yaga”

For adults, Halloween is an excuse to mingle sensuality with morbid themes. Why cosplay as a grizzled cop when you can be a flirty detective? Why settle for pretending to be a humpbacked wiccan when you can troll for hookups as a sexy witch?

“Yaga” has it all ways, resplendent with softcore interludes dangled between clue-finding excursions and unexpected chicken violence. The latest infatuation bait from Crave, the Canadian streamer that brought us “Heated Rivalry,” also gives us a new view of Hudson Williams and his naked hindquarters.

Only now, they’re humping away in a mystery taking place in a verdant seaside community called Whittock. There, dirtbag P.I. Charlie Rapp (Noah Reid) is drawn into a tense partnership with Carson (Clark Backo), a no-nonsense cop fatigued with her colleagues’ disrespect.

At first, Carson is repulsed by Rapp’s slimy overstepping, but she slowly learns not to loathe him. Implied in this, perhaps, is that a spell has ensnared the two as they probe the disappearance of rich playboy Henry Park (Williams). This pursuit leads them to encounter free-range poultry in odd places, along with strange symbols, a variety of nubile temptresses and a roommate, Logan (also Williams), who resembles Henry to an unsettling degree.

The show’s title has nothing to do with John Wick. It refers to the mythical Slavic crone, Baba Yaga, who either gives heroes a boon or lures wayward children to a forest hut that moves around on bird legs. Playwright Kat Chandler’s interpretation leaves open the question of whether our impressions of its witch are unwarranted or dead right, further muddling our judgment with the introduction of Carrie-Anne Moss’ university professor, Katherine Hargrove.

Inside Tall Timber: You Can Only Reach This Hand-Built Colorado Estate by Train or Helicopter

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There are only two ways to reach the 200-acre Colorado property known as Tall Timber: by train or helicopter. But back when octogenarian matriarch Judy Beggrow was just a girl, there was a third way—by horse.

Tucked inside the 1.8-million-acre San Juan National Forest near Durango—and surrounded by protected public land with no public road access—the estate is now on the market for $25 million.

Judy, her son, Johnroy, and his wife, Dionne, have listed it with Max Cawal.

Its centerpiece is the three-story timber lodge Judy and her late husband, Denny, built by hand.

It went up in a single summer and opened as a resort in 1972. Expanded in the mid-1990s, the lodge has 21 bedrooms, 22 full bathrooms, and 10 half-baths, laid out more like a boutique mountain hotel than a family home.

A three-story great room anchored by a river-rock fireplace opens onto decks with views of forest, river, and surrounding peaks. The dining and gathering spaces are served by a commercial-grade kitchen built for feeding a crowd.

Guests arrive to Tall Timber by train or helicopter. (Woods Photography) The property comes with exclusive river frontage throughout its 200 acres. (Woods Photography) At the center of the property is a timber lodge that was hand built by Judy and her husband in the 1960s. (Woods Photography) A private deck leading to the master suite (Woods Photography)

Cabins and staff quarters add 18 bedrooms, 15 full baths, and two half-baths, bringing the property total to 39 bedrooms, 40 full baths, and 13 half baths.

Then there are the details you won’t find on most estates. The property has an old-fashioned soda shop and ice cream parlor, a vintage-style barbershop, a resort-style pool and hot tub, and a three-story greenhouse where the family grows 35,000 flowers.

It has extensive frontage on the Animas River, and one of the longest zip-line courses in the country runs through its trees.

The sale also includes use of a nearby 10,000-square-foot private hangar with a one-bedroom apartment. By helicopter, local ski hub Purgatory Resort is about five minutes away and Telluride about 20 minutes away.

But the real story of this property is set deep in the canyon itself. Over the past century, it has been a mining-era outpost, a family dude ranch and hunting destination, a five-star resort that shared top honors with the Ritz-Carlton, and a family-run zip line.

Judy has been here for all of it.

"My father had a reputation in the canyon," says Judy, who spent a day in July walking me through the land her family has worked for 80 years.

Her father, Ross McCausland, was an electrical engineer who helped develop the automatic transmission during World War II and met his wife, Ethel, on a train from Chicago to Denver.

In 1946, the couple opened Ah! Wilderness Guest Ranch, an 80-acre dude ranch a mile south of what is now Tall Timber. Ross later described it as “a place where children and adults could play together.”

The family ate venison, kept a garden and dairy cows, and raised pigs. In the lean early years, they boarded the train with sandwiches and homemade cream puffs to sell—a venture that yielded what was sometimes their only income. Ross led hunting trips that included a train ride into the canyon, a week of hunting, a personal chef, and lodging, all for $175.

Back then, the Durango & Silverton Narrow Gauge Railroad, which today is a tourist line carrying some 200,000 riders a year, only ran about once a week, mostly hauling freight out of Silverton.

“The train really didn’t like passengers back then,” Judy says. So her father built his own custom motorcars to run on the tracks, seven in all.

As a girl, Judy chased the train on horseback. She also watched scenes from “Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid” being filmed on the line in the late 1960s.

The family has a number of collectibles that will convey with the sale, including Oscar Schaaf’s 1930s Ford truck (Woods Photography) An authentic soda fountain bar, bought by the family from a now-closed Durango drugstore where Marilyn Monroe is rumored to have sat after long days filming the 1949 movie, “A Ticket to Tomahawk" (Woods Photography) The dining room inside the main lodge, where the family hosted guests during their years as a five-star resort (Woods Photography) One of 39 bedrooms on the property, which can be found in the main lodge and in private cabins throughout the 200 acre parcel (Woods Photography)

The ranch was built with help from a neighbor, Oscar Schaaf, who ran a one-man sawmill. His older sister was Olga Little, Colorado’s first female jack packer, who once roped 18 snow-stranded miners together and led them on a 16-hour trek to safety. A nearby mountain bears her name.

When Judy and Denny bought the property in the late 1960s, the land had no power, so the young couple dug the holes and set the poles to bring electricity from the nearby Tacoma hydroelectric plant. Next came water. Judy remembered playing as a child in a freshwater stream that ran down from the mountain, so she and Denny hiked up the peak behind today’s lodge and laid pipe to bring spring water down to the homesite.

Tall Timber opened to guests in 1972 and went on to earn five-star and five-diamond ratings from the Mobil Travel Guide and AAA. Judy did all the cooking, sharpening her skills in food and wine classes at Epcot, where they kept a second home for the off season. With 10 guest cabins, the resort hosted up to 30 guests at a time, served by a peak staff of about 40. 

The resort was no stranger to celebrities. Christie Brinkley once stayed 10 days—something she wrote about in a tell-all piece in People magazine in April 2025. On Tuesdays, a helicopter carried guests to picnics above 11,000 feet at the nearby alpine lakes known as the Jewel Lakes, where chefs waited in tall white hats.

Humans weren’t the only guests, either. Welcome baskets warned visitors to close their windows at night, and one evening a mother bear turned up on a guest’s couch, eating an apple. She later taught her cubs to pop off window screens and raid the rooms for fruit.

The family also became the first private party in Colorado to successfully rewild a juvenile deer, found abandoned beside its dead mother. During hunting season Philippe wore a red scarf so no one would mistake him for game, and tennis balls on his antlers kept him gentle with the dogs.

In the mid-2000s, the family added a zip line through the trees. When visitors showed more interest in “playing in the trees” than in staying overnight, it became the property’s main business, and the last resort guests left in 2008. This past summer, at the height of its final season, the zip line drew 60 to 80 riders a day.

Now, the family is ready to move on.

Judy is preparing to move to Florida. And with Johnroy and Dionne’s only daughter in college, the couple wants to spend summers traveling rather than running the business. They’d love to see a buyer revive Denny’s vision of a boutique five-star destination, but they’re realistic.

“If Kevin Costner buys it, pick a celebrity,” Johnroy says. “In the end, it will be the same thing: someone enjoying it with their families.”

Dionne goes briefly quiet when asked for her favorite memory of living here.

“Waking up one Christmas morning to a proposal,” she says, glancing over at Johnroy.

It’s hard to picture this place in winter, with the canyon so green in summer and Judy’s flower arrangements filling baskets on the porch. But like everything here, winter is steeped in stories: snowmobiling, Christmas trees as tall as the great room can hold, a canyon blanketed in white beyond the windows while a fire roars in the three-story chimney.

Whoever buys the place will inherit the seclusion of a wild Colorado canyon and a property built to host a crowd.

Much of the hard work is already done, from the power lines and spring water the Beggrows brought in by hand to the commercial kitchen and staff quarters that once kept a five-star resort running year-round.

The property could serve as a private family compound, an executive retreat or, subject to approvals, a return to hospitality.

During the summer, the river, pool, and zip line take center stage. In winter, the slopes at Purgatory await. The buyer will also inherit the stretch of track that is the only way in other than by air. For years, the Beggrows rode the train or ran their own homemade cars on the rails.

When asked what getting in and out was like in those early years, Judy answers without a hint of fear.

“We derailed a few times,” she says.
