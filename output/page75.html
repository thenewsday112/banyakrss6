It’s that spooky time of year and so we’re taking a look at the 10 scariest Premier League managers to ever appear on the touchline.

This weekend’s Premier League football falls on Halloween so what better time to look back over the archives and find the 10 scariest men to ever manage in the league.

Let’s get into it.

10. Graeme Souness

They say players are a reflection of their manager and so perhaps it is no surprise that Kieron Dyer and Lee Bowyer fought each other while under the tutelage of Souness.

Souness is proper hard. You know the kind of hard that almost causes a riot when he plants a Galatasaray flag in the centre of Fenerbahce’s pitch.

Why is he so low then you may ask? That’s because he has got a heart of gold behind all that.

In his retirement years, he met a 14-year-old girl who suffers from a rare skin disease, Epidermolysis bullosa, and went on to swim the English Channel to raise money, getting £1 for charity.

9. Sir Alex Ferguson

It’s an obvious candidate with Ferguson as his hairdryer treatment is well known in football lore.

Get on the wrong side of him and he would give you the vengeful fury of a Scotsman who previously worked in a rough pub in Glasgow.

Wayne Rooney, a particular favourite target of Fergie, recalled a time when they were winning 1-0 away at Portsmouth and despite Rooney scoring, Ferguson “battered” him at half time.

Rooney, being the combustible character he is, gave it back and Ferguson let it be known who was in charge. The paint on the walls probably needed redoing.

8. Jose Mourinho

The reason why Jose makes the list is not because he is physically intimidating, but you’d imagine it’d be like playing against Jigsaw from the Saw movies.

Mourinho is a master of the mental mind games and would be able to manipulate you in such a way that you’d turn into Jack Nicholson in The Shining.

One day you’d wake up and you would have fully descended into madness with master Mourinho pulling the strings.

No thanks.

7. Iain Dowie

Is comparing him to Sloth from The Goonies harsh? Probably yes. Does he look him? Also probably yes.

Aside from his menacing look, Dowie was known for having a short fuse. He fell out with Simon Jordan and Robbie Savage (understandably) but also let his players know when they had wronged him.

In a recent interview with Planet Football, Clinton Morrison recalled that Dowie “had a bit of a temper where he could lose it, and he’s one you wouldn’t go toe-to-toe with for sure”.

6. Roy Keane

With us safe in the knowledge that Keane doesn’t know where we live, dare we say there is a soft centre to his supposed hardman shtick?

Keane obviously has very high standards and will let you know if you fall below them but his media appearances in his latter career have shown he does at least have some kind of heart and wit behind the tough exterior.

But even with that in mind, he is in the bottom 10 of managers we would want to cross.

And don’t you dare let your phone ring near him.

5. Paolo Di Canio

Can you name another Premier League manager whose actions led to a club changing the locks on their training ground?

When Di Canio resigned as Swindon boss in 2013, he reportedly waited three days before engaging in a midnight raid of the League One side’s training ground.

Di Canio and three members of his coaching staff went under the cover of darkness to “retrieve” mementoes of his 21 months in charge.

His strangeness was not limited to Swindon either. Whilst at Sunderland, he reportedly introduced a rule that players could not talk at lunchtime. He banned fried breakfasts (fair enough) and fell out with players like Lee Cattermole.

And there are few things scarier than someone who is unpredictable.

In 2012, against Swindon, Di Canio subbed off his 21-year-old keeper just 21 minutes into a game against Preston. With the score at 2-0, Di Canio then proceeded to tear into this young player, describing it as ‘one of the worst players I have ever seen in a football match’.

Good luck to anyone who gets on the wrong side of him.

4. Ian Holloway

The reason Holloway is so high is you’d never know where you stand with him.

One minute he’s your best mate, the next he’s got a face of thunder.

He was famously outspoken whilst in charge at Blackpool and he has only got more eccentric in recent years.

The Bristolian is the current manager of Swindon and, after speaking in a tone that would not be out of place at Sunday dinner with your grandmother, he out of nowhere started banging his chest, chanting “D M W”.

Then with eyes as wide as a vinyl record, he shouts “DEAD MAN WALKING.”

How can you reason with a man like that? Knowing that is somewhere in him, lurking, waiting for a moment to strike. That is the kind of frightening behaviour that keeps you up at night.

3. Felix Magath

What’s more haunting than looking scary? Looking friendly but being anything but.

Magath looks like a friendly German uncle you may have. He passes you sweets under the table and ruffles your hair but anyone at Fulham in 2014 will know this Danger Mouse lookalike was anything but warm and cuddly.

After the relaxed nature of Martin Jol, Fulham’s owners sought to install discipline into the team and so appointed Magath.

His credentials were there. He won the Bundesliga on three occasions. He was named German Football Manager of the Year in 2003, 2005 and 2009. He’d been at clubs like Bayern Munich but Magath came with a reputation of not only being a hard taskmaster, but also a crazed one at that.

In Germany, he gained the nickname Saddam after the fallen dictator for training methods that included sending the Bayern squad running through Alpine woods in the summer only to then hide their water bottles.

Whilst at Fulham, Brede Hangeland was given a recovery programme for a thigh injury only for Magath to send a kitman to a local Tesco and buy a block of cheese. According to Magath, the cheese had soothing effects and Hangeland was told to rub it on his injury. He soon left for Brighton.

He would have three training sessions in a day, one of which was solely devoted to running, and even Premier League footballers could not cope.

And while Hannibal Lecter also shared that innocent-at-first, unnerving-underneath quality, at least his methods brought results. Magath managed 20 games at Craven Cottage and lost 12 of them.

2. Marcelo Bielsa

There’s a reason Bielsa is known as El Loco.

Google his name and try and find a single photo where he does not have a face like thunder.

He stays up late, poring over VHS while his wife presumably asks if he fancies watching a movie tonight. The Leeds United hero is famed for being able to watch two matches at the same time and what kind of sane person can do that?

But the scariest story about him comes back in 1992. When faced with angry Newell’s Old Boys fans outside his home, Bielsa walked out to meet the mob.

He steps out of his house in his pyjamas, walks towards them and reveals a live hand grenade in his hand. Leave, he says, or he pulls the pin.

The fans scarper as he shouts “Do you still want to talk?”

That makes us ask the question of why does he even have a live hand grenade in his home? But one question we don’t need answering is, is Bielsa scary?

1. Nigel Pearson

There’s few managers where you feel an actual threat of violence but Pearson fits that bill.

Just watch the famous Ostrich press conference and see the eyes of a killer.

This is a man who was rumoured to have fought off a pack of wolves barehanded during a holiday in Romania in 2007.

He later cleared it up and said that was wrong, it was German Shepherds. Much easier then.

Pearson is frightening because he could batter you with ease and not even blink an eye.

Arsenal vs Liverpool: Arsenal travel to Anfield on Sunday looking to maintain their strong start to the Premier League season and end a long wait for victory at Liverpool’s home ground.

A blistering start to the season has seen Mikel Arteta’s side win seven of their first eight league games, leaving them tied on points with Manchester City at the top of the table and six points ahead of Liverpool going into the game.

However, the Gunners have not beaten Liverpool in the Premier League at Anfield since 2012/13. Arteta will be aware that the Reds are starting to gel under Andoni Iraola.

They have won three of their last four league games after a lacklustre start and had their first league win at home this season with their 2-0 win over Brighton.

Arsenal face Liverpool side finding its attacking rhythm

After replacing Arne Slot over the summer, Iraola brought his impressive three-year spell at Bournemouth to Anfield. However, Liverpool have struggled to adjust to the new manager, losing 3 of their first 4 league games.

But they have recently improved and are looking to play a much faster style of football. As Adrian Clarke notes, they are pressing more, not sitting back as much and wanting to attack quickly when they win the ball.

They have been the most direct attackers in the Premier League. They have pace to spare within their attack, with players like Alexander Isak and Cody Gakpo providing a threat in advanced areas.

Isak comes into the game in good form after finding the back of the net six times in his first eight league games. He has scored in three successive games in the Premier League for the first time since joining the club.

Florian Wirtz will also play a key role in Liverpool’s attacking play. The German has been consistently creating chances for the team playing behind Isak in a 4-2-3-1 system. He has created nine chances against Brentford.

Arsenal will need to limit the space available to Liverpool’s forward players. Allowing Isak and Gakpo to combine freely could make an already difficult away fixture even harder.

Arteta receives positive Arsenal injury news

Arsenal have had some positive news on their squad ahead of their Merseyside trip. Declan Rice and Riccardo Calafiori are both back in training.

Christos Tzolis has also trained after missing October. Noni Madueke has also not been ruled out by Arteta ahead of Sunday’s match after he missed the Fleetwood game.

They also have their own fitness issues to contend with. Iraola confirmed that Dominik Szoboszlai and Victor Munoz are both available after suffering injuries earlier in the month.

Jeramie Frimpong will be missing due to a hamstring issue but should be back after the international break. Hugo Ekitike, Geovanni Leoni and Conor Bradley are all still sidelined with longer-term injuries.

The presence of Rice will be key for Arsenal. He has been one of the best progressive midfielders in the Premier League, along with their captain, Martin Odegaard.

Arsenal aim to end lengthy Anfield winless run

Despite losing just one of their last eight Premier League clashes against Liverpool, Arsenal are not as strong on the road.

The Gunners have not won any league games in their last 13 trips to Anfield. They have drawn five and lost eight. Their last win was a 2-0 victory back in the 2012/13 season.

They have also never gone 14 games unbeaten away from home in the league. That would be a great achievement.

However, there is good reason to believe they can do it. They have only conceded five clean sheets in the league this season. They have faced the least number of shots on target and the fewest expected goals.

Liverpool have also been on a strong run defensively with four clean sheets in their last six league games, so Arsenal must make their chances count.

A win would provide another key result against a direct rival, improving their position at the top of the Premier League table and finally putting an end to one of the clubs’ longest-running away day woes.

About Supabase

Supabase is the Postgres development platform, built by developers for developers. We provide a complete backend solution including Database, Auth, Storage, Edge Functions, Realtime, and Vector Search. All services are deeply integrated and designed for growth.

About the Role

We’re looking for a Product Manager, Administration & Governance to join our Product Team and make Supabase the default starting point for software built inside a company.

Supabase already spreads from the bottom up: someone has an idea, creates a project, and builds something useful. Inside most companies, Supabase starts the same way: someone has an idea, creates a project, and builds something useful. The next step is making that momentum company-wide: one platform where anyone can start building, and where IT can see what’s running, who owns it, and what it costs.

You’ll own how companies run Supabase as their internal development platform: approved by IT and easy for anyone in the company to use. You’ll make it possible for people and the agents they work with to start building without waiting on infrastructure, while giving platform, IT, and security teams the controls they need to say yes. The goal is to make the approved path the easiest path, from an early experiment to a production application, with the controls companies need built in.

What You’ll Be Responsible For

  • Own the product strategy and roadmap for internal innovation, from a company’s first IT-approved rollout to adoption across teams and business units.

  • Make getting started self-service, whether someone creates a project in Supabase, through an internal developer portal, or with an agent. Give builders the access and infrastructure they need without a ticket for every project.

  • Steer scattered usage into a manageable company-wide platform. Give companies one view of everything they run on Supabase. Shape how ownership, delegated access, and boundaries work so teams can move independently as usage grows.

  • Partner closely with platform, IT, security, and finance teams to understand what it takes to approve Supabase and keep it approved. Translate their needs into practical identity, policy, audit, and spend controls that don’t slow builders down.

  • Define the path from experiment to production, including project ownership, access, and the guardrails that change as an application becomes important to the business.

  • Work with Engineering, Design, Sales, and Customer Success to turn early customer rollouts into a repeatable experience, rather than a new custom integration for every company.

  • Define what success looks like, instrument it end to end, and let the data guide what gets built next—from time to first project and self-service adoption to production usage and expansion across the company.

You Might Be a Good Fit If You

  • Have 7+ years of product management on developer tools, infrastructure, or internal developer platforms.

  • Have built self-service products that developers love and platform or IT teams trust. You understand both the person trying to ship an app and the team responsible for how it runs.

  • Can reason about identity, permissions, organization boundaries, and usage attribution, and turn those systems into workflows people can actually understand.

  • Have a strong point of view on how agents change who can build software inside a company and what an internal development platform needs to make that possible.

  • Know your way around controlled experimentation and data analysis, and can connect easier adoption to sustained product use.

  • Are biased toward speed and would rather ship, learn, and iterate than perfect a spec.

  • Build with what you own and form opinions through first-hand use, from creating a project to configuring the policies around it.

  • Use AI tools to compress research, drafting, and feedback synthesis so you can focus on judgment calls.

  • Work async by default, making decisions in writing in a fully remote team.

What We Offer

  • Fully Remote

    We hire globally. We believe you can do your best work from anywhere. There are no Supabase offices, but we provide a WeWork membership or co-working allowance you can use anywhere in the world.

  • ESOP

    Every team member receives ESOP (equity ownership) in the company. We want everyone to share in the upside of what we’re building together.

  • Tech Allowance

    Use this budget to set up your ideal work environment—laptop, monitor, headphones, or whatever helps you do your best work.

  • Health Benefits

    Supabase covers 100% of health insurance for employees and 80% for dependents, wherever you are. Your wellbeing and your family’s health are important to us.

  • Annual Off-Sites

    Once a year, the entire company gathers in a new city for a week of connection, collaboration, and fun. It’s a highlight of our year.

  • Flexible Work

    We operate asynchronously and trust you to manage your own time. You know what needs to be done and when.

  • Professional Development

    Every team member receives an annual education allowance to spend on learning—courses, books, conferences, or anything that supports your growth.

About the Team

Supabase was born-remote and open-source-first. We believe our globally distributed team is our secret weapon in building tools developers love.

  • ~400 team members

  • 60+ countries

  • 20+ languages spoken

  • Over $1B raised (including our $500M Series F)

  • 540,000+ community members

ALAMO, Tex. – Eustorgia Santos de Torres has lived here for four decades. Last year, she turned to a trusted Rio Grande Valley nonprofit for help navigating the citizenship application process. The same nonprofit — La Unión del Pueblo Entero, or LUPE — then helped her register to vote, and she cast her first ballot as a citizen in the primary election last March.

Santos de Torres, 67, is retired after years cleaning houses and working as a healthcare aide. She speaks mainly Spanish and is the primary caretaker for her husband, Nemorio, who is blind. “Yo soy sus ojos,” Santos de Torres said — I am his eyes.

But last month, Santos de Torres, a new voter, struggled to help Nemorio, an American-born citizen, understand and cast his mail ballot for the November election. All the information on the ballot is in English, she said, and the letters are small.

So Santos de Torres helped Nemorio to the car and made the familiar 7-minute drive to LUPE’s headquarters in the neighboring town of San Juan to ask for help with her husband’s ballot.

This time, though, LUPE workers reluctantly turned the couple away.

In 2021, Texas’ GOP-dominated state Legislature passed a sweeping and controversial overhaul of election laws known as SB1. Among other changes, SB1 made it illegal for anyone to receive compensation for helping voters fill in their ballots.

That not only makes it illegal for paid nonprofit staff to help voters, but also criminalized common past practices, such as organizations giving volunteers gas money or stipends. Violations are a felony punishable by up to 10 years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000.

In the wake of the law, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, a Republican currently running in the state’s nationally watched U.S. Senate race, investigated and prosecuted voting rights advocacy groups and organizers in heavily Latino communities across the state.

Groups such as LUPE say they have been forced to alter or stop some voter outreach as they worry about running afoul of the law. LUPE used to send workers out walking blocks to help voters. Now it has to instead ask volunteers to do more limited versions of that work.

Latinos and other non-English speaking voters will be key to the outcome of the state’s high-profile U.S. Senate and House races this year. But the law means such voters, as well as those who are disabled and elderly, have fewer options for help.

LUPE is known for providing one-on-one assistance to people in the Latino community, so the restrictions on helping with voting are a real pain point for the organization, Executive Director Tania Chavez Camacho said. Texas has strict rules limiting who is eligible to use mail ballots, but people over 65 may do so. LUPE helps around 30,000 people a year, she said, including people with disabilities and many older people who, each year, “want to vote by mail and they come in wanting help.”

Now, Chavez Camacho said, when the group holds public town halls for residents to instruct them on how to vote, if a voter pulls out their mail ballot to ask for help, organizers must ask the voter to leave so paid employees aren’t near the ballot.

And if workers for LUPE’s advocacy arm are canvassing for candidates and a voter shows them a mail ballot and asks for help filling it out, workers have been trained to say they can’t and walk away, Chavez Camacho said.

“We literally have to turn them away,” Chavez Camacho said. “We’ve had to do a lot of training with our staff for the past three years because they were so accustomed to helping folks one-on-one and now they can’t.”

Santos de Torres did what she could to help her husband, who mailed back his ballot about a month ago.

“But I don’t think it’s right that LUPE can’t help us,” she told a reporter in Spanish. “That law is not right. It’s excluding people who need help.”

“What if,” she added, “my husband didn’t have me?”

‘A person who doesn’t know how to read has to do it on their own’

The Rio Grande Valley, where LUPE is based, covers roughly 4,300-square-miles across Hidalgo, Cameron, Starr, and Willacy counties along the Mexico border at Texas’ southern tip. It’s a diverse community with some residents who primarily speak Spanish. Younger generations of residents easily switch between English and Spanish in conversation.

Many households are made up of families whose members have mixed legal statuses, including some who have lived in the area for generations. The Valley also has a history of activism as the birthplace of the Texas farmworker movement of the 1960’s. Farmworkers – who harvested cotton, vegetables and fruits — organized and marched for roughly 400 miles from the Valley to Austin to demand fair wages and better working conditions. Mexican-American farmworker labor today remains key to the area’s growth and agricultural economy.

But due to the lack of affordable housing, Mexican Americans for years purchased land they could afford in rural areas outside of city limits, often lacking basic infrastructure and public services. Those communities are known now as the colonias.

Alamo, where Santos de Torres lives, and San Juan, where LUPE is based, are neighboring cities. The landscape varies depending on the side of town. In the southern parts of the cities, closer to the border with Mexico, modest residential homes with green front lawns adorn along palm tree-lined streets. Shopping centers, chain restaurants, stadiums, and commercial developments lie to the north of the cities, along Interstate Highway 2, reflecting the transition from the once primarily agricultural community to a growing metropolitan hub. The Valley has for years relied on its trade of goods and services with Mexico. It has a population of more than 1.4 million people and 90% of them are Hispanic or Latino, according to Census data.

About 24% of people in the Valley live below the poverty line, which is more than double the national rate. And about 30% of Valley residents don’t have health insurance – one of the highest uninsured rates in the country. The area also has one of the highest rates of diabetes in the country; Nemorio Torres’ blindness is a complication of the disease.

LUPE and other advocacy groups have long worked with residents in the colonias and all over the Valley, providing resources to find affordable housing and medical treatment, or helping with complex procedures such as filing taxes. Voter education and outreach was part of the mission.

Now, instead of providing one-on-one assistance, LUPE hosts group sessions with a screen projector showing what mail ballot applications and mail ballots look like. Facilitators point at the screen indicating what the instructions say and explaining how the forms should be filled out.

The limitations are frustrating, Chavez Camacho said, especially “si una persona no sabe leer” — if a person can’t read. “It doesn’t matter that we’re doing those sessions because a person who doesn’t know how to read has to do it on their own and we can’t help them to know where to mark,” she said.

Instead, LUPE’s workers and volunteers now focus on letting people know key deadlines are coming up. They also have fewer volunteers willing to guide and interpret for voters at the polls, she said, because the law now requires such people to sign an oath affirming the voter is qualified to receive help because they are disabled or need an interpreter, and detail their relationship with the voter. They must also affirm they are not being paid by a candidate or political committee, which would be illegal. Volunteers fear any misstep while assisting voters could get them in trouble, Chavez-Camacho said.

“We really feel that the people are not getting the support they need,” Chavez Camacho said.

Election and voting law experts say the courts have interpreted “compensation” prohibited from going to volunteers by SB1 as any type of benefit. That interpretation is so broad that it makes it difficult for voter outreach organizations to know whether they are violating the law, said Zachary Dolling, litigation director at Texas Civil Rights Project, which works with groups to design trainings in voter outreach that comply with the law.

Dolling said some organizations historically provided unpaid volunteers dinner, T-shirts, or pens, and others have given volunteers college credit for their work.

“All of those things are items of value” and could be considered compensation, he said.

Advocates warned state leaders of added burdens for people with disabilities

Texas Republicans passed SB1, the sweeping overhaul of election law, in part responding to false claims and conspiracy theories about the 2020 election.

Republicans claimed the law would increase public trust and confidence in the elections. Democrats argued the legislation would disproportionately burden voters of color and people with disabilities, and staged two walkouts and a 15-hour Senate filibuster in their failed attempt to defeat the bill.

Despite lawsuits from voting rights organizations including LUPE, the League of United Latin American Citizens and organizations representing Texans with disabilities, a federal appeals court ultimately upheld key provisions of SB1, including the restrictions on voter assistance.

Other attempts to expand voting help for people with disabilities were meanwhile unsuccessful.

In 2023, Gov. Greg Abbott, a Republican, vetoed a bipartisan bill that would have expanded vote-by-mail access for people with disabilities — specifically people who are blind or paralyzed and need assistance marking their ballot — by allowing such voters to request an electronic ballot and use a computer to mark their choices. Similar options in Texas are already available for military overseas voters and astronauts, but not for voters with disabilities.

The challenges Santos de Torres and her husband have faced are an example of what advocates worried about under SB1, said Lydia Nunez Landry, statewide coordinator for REV UP Texas, which works to get out the vote among people with disabilities.

“That was our worst fear and now it’s happening,” she said. Nunez Landry has muscular dystrophy, uses a power wheelchair, and needs help doing basic tasks such as opening the refrigerator.

The state has made voting “just so impossible and so arduous for people — and now people are also terrified of helping us,” she said.

An increased ‘sense of anxiety’

Groups across the state have been changing their voter outreach work to comply with SB1.

Asian Texans for Justice conducts voter outreach in Asian communities, focusing on San Antonio, Houston, Austin, and North Texas.

The stricter rules associated with voting by mail under SB1 have created “a sense of anxiety” in those communities, Executive Director Nabila Mansoor said. She said that the process is now much more complicated for elderly voters, who must provide one of two forms of ID on their application and pay close attention to pages of instructions that are written in font that is small and hard for them to read. In addition, an error could force them to restart the whole process of applying for a ballot.

“That anxiety comes from this kind of fear that they’re going to do something wrong,” Mansoor said. “Whenever you have that kind of fear, it is one more barrier for that person to actually go out and vote.”

Now that Asian Texans for Justice can no longer provide one-on-one help, they’ve turned to providing checklists for voters, she said, which she believes is less effective when some voters may need help reading and understanding the ballot and instructions

Mansoor said she believes that SB1’s goal was to make it harder for people to vote.

“It means that we have to be more careful. It means that we have to dot our i’s and cross our t’s. It means that we have to take good records. It means that we have to be very thorough in terms of our training of our volunteers,” she said, adding that the organization assembled a legal team to defend against challenges to their work as well.

In the meantime, volunteers for LUPE and other groups say they are doing their best to fill the gap.

Maria Gomez, better known as Doña Mary, of Pharr, worked as a farmworker for years in Hidalgo County and has been a member of LUPE since the organization was founded. Gomez, 78, was involved in the farmworker movement years ago. She now ensures her elderly friends and neighbors are aware of election deadlines, know where to vote, and have a ride to the polls if they need one.

After she drops off her grandkids and great-grandkids at school, she makes her rounds around the Colonias in San Juan in her small red SUV. She chats up her neighbors, and asks them what they need help with or if they need resources or a ride. She’s always fielding calls.

“People don’t vote because sometimes they can’t leave their home and because they might not have access to that piece of paper that they need to sign to receive their ballot,” Gomez said in Spanish, referring to voter registration forms and ballot-by-mail applications.

One of her friends, 80-year-old Teresa Corona Garza of San Juan, has relied on Gomez for rides to the polls for some time now. Corona Garza struggles to stand for long periods of time and can’t see very well out of her left eye. Even though she qualifies to vote by mail, she prefers to go to the polls in person.

“There’s so much on there you have to fill out, and those letters are small,” Corona Garza said in Spanish. “No, I prefer to go, quiero ver que mi voto cuenta,” she said – I want to see that my vote is counted.

The laws have gotten so complicated and strict that Gomez worries state leaders may even try to prevent volunteers like her from providing rides to the polls.

“I don’t know what’s going to happen, if they’re going to keep making this harder for people,” she said, “but I would like to see people who want to vote to be able to do it.”
