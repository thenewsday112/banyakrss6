Access, Not Chances: How Como Took Manchester United Apart – LC

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Como beat Manchester United 4-1 — and in this case the scoreline really is a fair expression of the game. For long stretches the match was one-sided, not in terms of chance volume but in terms of access: Como dictated in every phase where the ball went, who received it, and under what conditions it had to be played on. United never got on terms in any of those phases.

Cesc Fàbregas’s side showed two things that rarely go together. Out of possession, a pressing structure with clear direction, isolation of one side, and man-marking that applied near the ball only for the action in progress while everything around it was already speculating on the second ball. And in possession, a positional game of remarkable technical maturity that ties opponents in, switches play, and works systematically into the space behind them. The 4-1 is therefore less an outlier than evidence of work that has been built up over recent seasons.

This analysis goes through the phases one by one: first Como in possession and United’s response, then the work against the ball from the high press to defending the box, and finally the goals.

Como build up, United can’t get near

In build-up, Como moved away from their nominal 4-2-3-1 and sat closer to a 4-3-3, with the eights as the real variable: very free in their movement and above all active in the half-spaces. The base structure stayed stable — a flat four plus a six in front of them, sometimes ahead of the line, sometimes slightly behind the forward line.

Close to their own goal, Como consistently involved goalkeeper Butez as an additional central player in the build-up. That created a five-against-four against United’s front line — awkward on paper, more awkward still in execution, because Como didn’t use the overload to play through but to tie opponents in. From there, the same shape kept appearing on both the right and the left: centre-back, full-back, a player from the half-position in the eight space, and a six alongside. A diamond that wasn’t an end in itself but had a purpose: the diagonal route out.

Como’s build-up structure. From a 4-1 (flat four), the half-positions with Paz and Perrone are added. Small triangles and diamonds emerge. The three attackers ahead of them create additional options in behind.

One detail deserves particular attention. At times the full-backs stood not wide but flat, in a half-position. That sounds like a nuance, but it had a clear function: it vacated the wing and created the space the eights could drop into — Paz and Perrone did so regularly. If you don’t occupy the wing yourself, you force the opponent to guard it anyway.

ManU got no access near the ball and left large diagonal spaces open on the far side.

For United’s midfielders this was close to unsolvable. Tielemans, Mount and Andrey Santos spent the whole game struggling to establish any responsibility at all, let alone get into duels. When an eight dropped out, they couldn’t follow, and if one of them was tied in by it, a two-against-one opened up on the far wing. Como didn’t create that situation once; they created it serially.

The decisive mechanism lay in receiving and relaying the ball. The player in the deeper position usually worked with two touches: the first took the ball backwards, the second relayed it to the free teammate. At the end of every one of those actions stood a player with the game in front of him — and from that open position he could switch play.

What made it possible were the preparatory movements. Paz and captain Da Cunha first lured their markers in the wrong direction and then worked with the head start they’d bought. That head start isn’t only space, it’s above all time: time to control the ball first and relay it second, rather than having to do both in one touch.

A scene in the 17th minute is typical. Mason Mount steps out, Mazraoui goes into the duel at the same time — nominally United are in an overload here. Only neither of them really gets to the opponent, because both are too late. Paz can therefore calmly take his touch backwards and switch play directly with the second, dissolving the pressure before it had even formed.

When the build-up ran down the right, it showed in a recurring pattern: Paz and Da Cunha took the first touch backwards or inside and then switched with the left foot out of a closed body position. The players they found were systematically in open positions — Milla, the far-side six, or the wing-to-wing connection directly.

In the second half, Como adjusted the shape and more often played with two sixes, to form the diamonds faster and switch to the far-side six faster. Couto stood out in this: the right-back repeatedly played the chip into the six or ten space — six or seven times in a row during one spell. It was mainly Paz who received the ball there and could launch the attack.

In the second half Como focus on those diagonal spaces and look for them mainly with chipped balls.

Especially striking inside the diamond are the rotations between the players. Because of the connections between them, they moved in rotation depending on running path, space and ball movement. The same recurring spaces were re-occupied. Under pressure on the ball carrier, the outer players pushed flat or ran in behind the opponents; the highest player in the diamond burst out of the cover shadow and became the reference point in depth. On a deep pass, the two wide players released and joined the depth, able to offer themselves again immediately in an open body position. If the pass went via the flat wide players, the spaces in front of the new ball carrier could be run into. Every rotation, every positional adjustment was coordinated with the others and, in the end, produced a stream of pressure-resolving passes while Manchester United chased the press in vain.

Transition and final third: the Z into the opponent’s back

Como bridged the middle third exceptionally quickly, via dribbles at speed and diagonal passes. Behind it stood a recognisable principle: first tie the opponent to one wing, then get behind them via the diagonal, and in the final third do the same thing again. In sum, that produced the shape of a Z — build-up on the right, transition on the left, finish from the right again.

In this example Como pin ManU in the press with their short passing. Thanks to the extra attacker on the ball-near side they can play through the pressure and switch.

The connections between wingers and striker carried these attacks and were deliberately kept flexible. The wide players sometimes stayed wide, sometimes came short, sometimes tucked inside. Douvikas accordingly either waited deep in front of the line for the chipped ball or, when space opened up on the wing, received the deeper pass and then ran in behind along the wing as soon as the ball sat on the full-back’s open foot. That flexibility produced the overloads on one side, and the overloads produced the central overload.

In the final third one element was added that is rare in fast transition teams: patience. Como drew opponents towards them with dribbles and short passes, used back-passes when needed, and then switched in front of the block. Da Cunha, Baturina and Paz were the three who could tie opponents in and then find the diagonal. They created the positions on the diagonal, were available to receive, and used their space to apply pressure. The block was usually tied in with short touches, the switch and the position behind the opponent prepared, and quick finishes sought.

The intensity in possession shouldn’t be underestimated. Practically every attack involved at least three or four players, the width was occupied, the centre likewise, and there were multiple options throughout. What they looked for almost every time was the space behind the opponent on the far side — exactly the zone that is closed last when a block shifts across.

Paz brought the right profile for this pattern. A strong left foot cutting inside from the right, he repeatedly dropped into the right-hand blind side of his opponents, got shots away there with few touches, and racked up the most shots of the match. That the finishes generally came off few touches isn’t a detail but the consequence of the time won further back.

As the move continues, Como again look for the blind side of the defenders. Here Douvikas and Paz are positioned in behind, with Da Cunha joining in front of the back line. After briefly pinning the opponents, the switch reaches Da Cunha, who misses the target from distance.

What carries all of this is the technical execution. Soft passes, sharp ones when the moment called for it, consistently well timed and played into the right foot — for ninety minutes. Passing that secure at that tempo is what makes a variable concept actually retrievable.

Why United never got into the duels

United’s basic problem out of possession was the hybrid. They pressed half man-oriented, but with a plus-one in the last line — a construction that forces compromises in both directions. It demands access up front that it doesn’t fund at the back.

There were certainly moments when it worked. When Dalot pushed through into the press on the right and passed his man backwards, a clean man-orientation emerged all the way up to the front — and with it a clear assignment for Mount, out of which the ball could be won. But that stayed the exception. Most of the time Dalot sat too flat out of possession, so United couldn’t trigger the duels up front and went into the press a man short.

Mount was the most conspicuous case in this respect. He barely got into a duel across the whole match because the decision to step out always came too late. Against a team that works with preparatory movements, a late decision is practically the same as none: the opponent already has the head start before the journey even begins.

On top of that came a problem on the far side. There, United’s winger frequently failed to protect the depth — usually Mbeumo, who jogged back rather than closing the space. But that was precisely the target zone of Como’s diagonal switches. It allowed them to change sides almost at will and regularly find an open structure on the other side.

The second half brought no improvement. The distances to opponents stayed large, and centrally what was actually intended still didn’t materialise. Michael Carrick’s double change at half-time was meant to bring more physicality to the six with Carlos Baleba, but it didn’t solve the problem — the assignments still didn’t work, the duels in the centre still didn’t happen, and the wide positions dragged the eights out further, which opened new spaces.

In the end the energy simply wasn’t there to press with the necessary intensity. United’s press became increasingly static, and a static press against an opponent with this passing quality yields not a single ball win — it only hands them the starting positions they’re looking for anyway.

Como’s press: steering and isolating

Como started the game in a high press and sustained it above all in the first half, with isolated spells after the break too. It was triggered on back-passes and goal kicks — and, remarkably, by their own goals: after every strike the team immediately pushed high again instead of managing the lead in a deeper block.

The steering ran consistently towards United’s left build-up side. The reason is personnel: Harry Maguire played there with his weaker left foot as the outside foot, and next to him stood another right-footer in Noussair Mazraoui on the left. Two right-footers on one build-up side means the ball always has to be taken back inside to play on — exactly where Como were closing the door. The press was usually triggered by the left-sided forward, with the ball ending up at either Maguire or Mazraoui.

Pressing structure after the change in the second half.

In the centre Como defended man-oriented, with both sixes pushing very high and taking flat passes into United’s midfielders out of the game. That removed the short route through the middle as an option and left the long ball. But the long ball triggered nothing for United: the second balls went to Como practically every time.

That isn’t chance, it’s running work. The sixes consistently tracked the negative pressing paths back, anticipated the long ball early, and were in front of the line before it arrived. Luis Milla covered the most ground of any player on the pitch in this pattern — recovery runs after being played past, doubling up on the ball, attacking the second ball. When the long ball came from Maguire or one of the full-backs, a six was regularly in front of the line with an intense sprint, sealing the centre, taking away the diagonal follow-up passes and attacking the loose balls.

Defending the long ball and extra support on the second ball.

Once the ball was on one side, that side was isolated. The near-side forward had access to the centre-back and dropped back a touch when the pass went to the full-back, so that he covered centre-back and six simultaneously and closed the square pass. The far-side forward marked the goalkeeper. That served two functions at once: it took away United’s escape route via the keeper along with the switch, and it kept a player in position for the moment of the turnover.

The double pivot worked in a deliberate half-position. The near-side six stayed tight to his man, the far-side one dropped slightly behind him — securing the space and at the same time establishing access to the far-side centre-back in United’s back three.

The last line defended largely man-for-man in the high press, and very tightly. But the decisive detail is the one that carries the whole concept: the man-marking near the ball applied only to the action in progress. Around it, players were already releasing from their assignments and orienting towards the second ball. That is only retrievable at very high intensity — and it’s the reason United’s forced long balls produced not a single usable moment.

Midfield press: the half-position and the half-time adjustment

When Como dropped a line, the man-orientation at the second height stayed exceptionally high. Here too the back-pass served as the trigger to push up as a unit and tip back into man-marking. If United were in a closed body position, the Comaschi had already pushed up far enough to attack the back-pass at the moment it was played — a pattern that led directly to the 3-0. The half-position was at work there again: closing down level with the centre-back while simultaneously prepared to push through onto the next player deeper.

In the first half Como still had problems assigning the half-positions against ManU’s 3-2-5. In this example Fernandes moves to the wing, Como fail to establish responsibility and access and are played through at mid-height.

Up to half-time, though, Como had a structural problem with this. The pure man-marking didn’t come off cleanly, because of the two sixes in front of the line, one should really have tucked in and taken over the half-position for the centre-backs. That didn’t happen consistently. The consequence: a centre-back stepped out, the space behind him stayed unoccupied, and that was exactly what United kept attacking.

The pattern of pressing onto Manchester United’s left side continued in the second half, now with Dalot at full-back. The press was still triggered by Douvikas, with the ball-near winger pushing up as a second striker to press the centre-back. Paz focused on the visitors’ ball-near six and Couto pushed through onto Dalot. Through the central connections the game was isolated on one side.

At half-time Como adjusted. One six moved directly into the line, with the far-side winger pushing in onto the six — they now pressed out of a 5-2-1-2. Lucas Da Cunha pressed through from the right onto Maguire, while Nico Paz picked up United’s six. The central forward pressed the central centre-back and from there carried on to the goalkeeper. The far-side wide midfielder and the remaining six were poised — one onto United’s free six, one onto the far-side centre-back — again in that half-position between opponent and the space to be protected.

ManU building up high against the man-oriented Italians.

The effect was immediately visible. Como controlled the half-positions, got access right into the last line throughout, and set the rhythm of the match from then on. The adjustment solved exactly the problem the first half had left open, without changing the underlying principle.

Deep defending: box packed, long shot on offer

In the final phase Como defended from a very deep line inside the box. That left barely any space behind the line to play into — which United hardly looked for anyway. The visitors attacked little depth and rarely occupied the width in the final third, so Como could stay compact without being punished for it.

Accordingly, a lot of players stood very centrally in and in front of the box. That produced numerous cover shadows and a numerical superiority around the ball, which allowed them to steer actively within the block rather than merely react. At times they deliberately steered inside, to guide the opponent into the prepared congestion and cover from a deeper position.

Deep back line in the box, centre closed and play steered inside. The box defending allowed the English side shots from distance.

The crucial point is that Como left the gaps open not despite this set-up but because of it. If you pack the box and secure the depth, you give up the space in front of the line — and that is exactly where United were supposed to shoot from.

The numbers vindicate the decision. Taking the conceded goal out of the box, Como allowed nine shots from outside the penalty area across ninety minutes, worth roughly 0.24 xG combined; only three of them were even on target. On top of that, of the four shots inside the box, two came from at least 14 to 15 metres and effectively belong in the long-range category as well. United accepted the invitation, and the shooting quality was perfectly decent — it’s just that with that scoring probability over a whole match, the most likely outcome is precisely what happened.

The goals

Tracking the ball for the first goal.

1-0, Nico Paz (2′) — The goal comes before United are even set. Como take the goal kick quickly and play straight to the full-back, luring the visitors into the press — only not cleanly. Mason Mount triggers the pressure and is played past, and on the second ball he goes into the duel as well instead of holding the middle and looking for the space where he could pick the ball up. That is exactly what opens the centre, the space for the second ball is there, and Paz can play it through.

What follows is the transition principle in its purest form: a quick switch to the diagonal side, then short passes against a very deep line. Matheus Cunha doesn’t pick up the player behind him and leaves the ten space open, where Paz is standing. He needs two touches because neither Cunha from above nor Maguire from his distance gets into the duel. Lammens, for his part, doesn’t defend the goal with his cover shadow but takes the near post — which leaves the far corner to a strong left foot. The curled finish into it is quality, though.

2-0, Anastasios Douvikas (49′, assist Paz) — Same zone, same cause. Bryan Mbeumo doesn’t choose to shut the centre but closes the centre-back, which creates the space for Da Cunha, who drives forward and plays Martin Baturina in. Centrally, Mount again fails to pick up his man. Out wide, Amad Diallo, introduced at half-time, generates no pressure centrally, so Mazraoui can’t take over the deep wide player in the first place.

The result is Paz behind the opponent, in the ten space, dribbling unopposed at goal. Matthijs de Ligt establishes no reference to Douvikas’s run in behind and slips as well — a one-against-one with the goalkeeper that Douvikas finishes coolly.

3-0, Nico Paz (57′) — The only goal that comes directly out of the work against the ball. Como are in the midfield press in a closed position with access to the inside lane, win the ball there and punish a lapse of concentration in the visitors’ build-up. At the end, Paz is alone in front of Lammens again.

4-0, Jacobo Ramón (76′, assist Kaiki) — This goal comes from a longer possession spell and shows the assignment problem in its clearest form. Mazraoui is too narrow and has no access to the wide player. Diallo stands in the space with access to neither the central player nor the full-back nor the winger — an orientation that doesn’t work in any direction.

Como switch to Máximo Perrone, who can slide the ball out wide because the space is simply free. From there comes the cross from an open body position, and in the centre Como run a two-against-one at the end of which Ramón scores.

4-1, Joshua Zirkzee (90′) — Como’s only lapse, and one in a phase in which the team had already dropped very deep. United get the ball to the wing, it’s two against two in the box, and the cross is initially only feinted. Zirkzee orients himself well forwards at that moment, while Como’s defenders are already stepping out after the feinted cross. Diallo’s ball in behind the line is strong, the offside call is razor-thin in his favour, and Zirkzee takes two touches before finishing one-against-one with the goalkeeper. A blemish, not a structural problem.

The common denominator — Three of Como’s four goals come in the same zone: the space in front of and behind United’s line that nobody takes responsibility for. Mount is involved in two of those scenes through an assignment he never establishes, Diallo in two more after coming on. Nico Paz is at the heart of three of the four goals, twice as scorer, once as the provider — always out of the opponent’s blind side.

Conclusion

Como’s 4-1 has one common cause across every phase, and it lies less in any single idea than in its execution under continuous load. Out of possession they steered, isolated and speculated on the second ball — with man-marking that applied near the ball only for the action in progress and was already dissolved around it. In possession they tied opponents in, switched play and worked systematically into the space behind. Both require eleven players to anticipate a teammate’s decision, and both functioned over the full distance.

The connecting detail is the half-position. In the press, the far-side six stood between opponent and the space to be protected; in possession, the eights worked out of the same zone, and the full-backs occupied it flat in order to vacate the wing for exactly those players. Como, in other words, occupied the position in both directions from which two jobs can be done at once — which is why the side could neither be played past in the press nor be pinned in the build-up.

For United the diagnosis is more uncomfortable than the result. The problem wasn’t individual errors but that the team never, in any phase, got into the duels it needed. The mixture of half man-orientation and a plus-one in the last line funded no access up front, and without access every decision is made too late — from Mount in the centre to Mbeumo failing to secure the far-side depth. Carrick’s double change at half-time was meant to bring physicality to the six with Carlos Baleba, but it changed nothing about the assignments, and in Diallo a player came on wide who reached neither one opponent nor the other in his space. That the energy ran out in the end was a consequence rather than a cause.

That leaves the context. A 4-1 against Manchester United is no proof in itself, and Como’s finishing was exceptionally efficient on the night. What showed regardless of the result, though, is a team with a recognisable, rehearsed answer in all four phases of the game — and one that put a technical maturity on the pitch in possession that you wouldn’t readily credit to a side promoted two years ago.

Nothing about Shibuya HiFi is loud. From its unmarked front door to its low-lit lounge, the Seattle vinyl bar is a subtle, pensive ballad of a place. So much so, in fact, that on the Wednesday night that we visited for a sold-out event, the sparsely populated lounge area was nearly silent.

That is intentional, said general manager Jerry Stotler. Wednesday nights are closed to everyone but ticket holders. “These are what we call deep listening sessions,” he explained, “so when you walk in, there isn’t any music playing. We want to ensure a pristine listening environment when we’re in the hi-fi room.”

Seattle has many vinyl bars, but Shibuya, open since 2023, sets the local standard for premium listening experiences. Every inch of its intimate sanctum contains the story of the venue’s principal owners, Quentin Ertel and Brian Rauschenbach. It’s named for the Tokyo district known for its jazz kissa, cafés dating back to post-war Japan where visitors listen to vinyl on premium sound equipment. Rauschenbach’s parents met in such a place nearly six decades ago, Stotler said.

Ertel, an interior designer, painstakingly curated the room. Cedar planks line the walls. Louis Poulsen chandeliers hang from the ceiling, along with several plush, crescent-shaped loveseats, acquired from Benaroya Hall when the concert hall underwent a redesign. A pair of deep seats covered in nubby upholstery sit close to this sacred space’s altar — a vinyl-stacked media console holding a vintage receiver and turntable, flanked by two ATC SCM50 loudspeakers. But one set of equipment reigns over everything: a pair of 1976 Klipschorns.

Shibuya’s schedule favors favorites such as D’Angelo’s “Voodoo,” Radiohead’s “Kid A,” or Charles Mingus’ self-titled opus. But on this Wednesday, the venue hosted a special listening session for Michael Kiwanuka’s fifth album, “Fudge,” a week before its Oct. 23 release.

Stotler humbly acknowledged the venue’s good fortune to be included on the very short list of people and organizations invited to preview the record, a relatively recent development. Previous pre-release sessions have debuted work by the Beastie Boys’ Mike D and Thundercat.

But I’m getting ahead of myself. While waiting for the magic to start, we listened to the hi-hat riff of ice cubes click-clacking as Stotler worked a cocktail shaker. Once the listening room doors open, all drinks, distractions and shoes must be left at the door. “So enjoy a cocktail in advance of the session to relax and reset,” he calmly says, sliding one of his potions across the bar to a fellow patron. “Then we’ll go back and drop the needle.”

I’ve been curious about the recent rise of listening bars. Life has gotten faster, noisier and increasingly disconnected. Low-friction digital convenience has sapped life and music of their organic weight.

Places like Shibuya, then, are a natural fit for what Ertel describes as a renaissance of meaning, driven by vinyl’s resurgence. “There’s a search for new ways to be together, but also, I think, just new ways to be,” he said when I caught up with him by phone a few days later.  “We live in sort of this two-dimensional world of scrolling. Everything’s flat and fragmented and fractured. When you have something that has kind of a wholeness to it, that is also linked to something that most of us love so much, which is music, it makes sense that people want vinyl records again.”

Younger audiophiles revived the medium from the dead starting in 2008 for many reasons, the simplest being that digital downloads don’t include eye-popping cover art. By 2020, vinyl record sales revenue hit $596.2 million, surpassing CDs ($531.1 million) for the first time since 1986, according to the Recording Industry Association of America. Listening bars, meanwhile, have popped up worldwide. A little later, a back injury forced him to slow down and think about what he’d do differently — a “divine download,” he called it during a recent phone chat, that transformed into a monthly series of phone-free listening sessions that have so far taken place in Portland and Seattle venues.

Coinbase wants six futures contracts to stop expiring on October 26. The CFTC letter it leans on covers five of them by design, and its own footnote points away from the sixth.

The Division of Market Oversight issued Letter 26-29 on October 3, 2026. The CFTC announced it two days later, on October 5, in Release 9308-26. Coverage then focused on the headline that exchanges can now strip expiry dates from perpetual-style index futures. The Merkle flagged the relief's hard October 20 stop on October 19, and outlets like Finance Magnates and FinanceFeeds ran the same window. That coverage, however, predates what Coinbase filed next. This breakdown sets the October 19 filing beside the letter's text, because the two don't line up cleanly.

Meanwhile, the CFTC is working on retail crypto leverage through a different door. As NullTX's earlier breakdown shows, the actual-delivery ANPRM (2026-20888) takes comments until December 14. Perpetuals on registered exchanges skip that process. Instead, they move through staff letters and self-certification.

What Coinbase Actually Filed

Coinbase asked for the relief on October 1, and the Division granted it two days later. The letter says staff won't recommend enforcement if a DCM drops expiry dates from existing perpetual-style contracts, as long as the DCM meets eight conditions. First, it must gather feedback from holders of open positions. Next, it must give at least five calendar days' notice and let them close out under the old terms. It must also disclose the risks and change no other material term. Finally, it must file under the CFTC's rules, notify the Division and certify compliance.

Coinbase's Submission #2026-67 then names six contracts: US500, Tech100, Defense10, AI10, China10 and Coin50. It says everything except the expiry stays the same, and that the exchange knows of no substantive opposition. The contract list reached the CFTC by email on October 16.

Where the Waiver Stops: Four Checkpoints

1. The Asset-Class Limit

Letter 26-29 defines its scope by asset class. A footnote shuts out any perpetual-style contract referencing something other than a broad-based security index. Coin50, by contrast, follows the Coinbase 50 Index, and Rule 1148 in the filing itself calls it a futures contract based on a cryptocurrency. In other words, the letter's scope and Coin50's own rule text point in opposite directions.

2. The Letter That Ran Out

Crypto contracts did have their own relief. Letter 26-19, issued June 12 to Bitnomial and Coinbase Derivatives, covered digital commodities only and carried the same eight conditions. Even so, it says its positions expire on June 30, 2026. It rests on the Commission's May 29 order and its perpetuals policy statement.

3. The October 20 Cutoff

Letter 26-29 sets October 20, 2026 as the day its no-action positions expire. It doesn't say whether implementation itself must happen by then, or only the notice and filing. Coinbase filed on October 19, one day early, but picked October 26 to go live. That choice matters. Ten business days from October 19 lands on November 2, and Coinbase specifically asked to skip that wait. As a result, going live on October 26 depends on the relief still applying.

4. The Stay Alternative

Without relief, the Division's own words make the path clear. Letter 26-19 says certifications that remove expiry dates will generally draw a stay under Regulation 40.6(c). A stay then gives the Commission another 90 days to review and opens a 30-day public comment period.

No Public Response Yet

Neither side has explained the gap. Searches of news coverage and official channels turned up no statement from Coinbase or the CFTC on whether Coin50 sits inside Letter 26-29, on what authority covers it after October 20, or on why the filing's October 26 date falls after the letter's expiry. We also found no CFTC stay notice and no extension or amendment of Letter 26-29. Likewise, the Coinbase posts we found cover other perpetual launches, not this conversion.

That silence doesn't mean a problem exists. Coinbase may be relying on authority the documents don't mention, and the CFTC may read the letter's dates differently than we do. Still, the paper trail shows a crypto-based contract sitting under relief written for stock indexes, with a start date that falls after that relief expires.

Why October 26 Matters

Three developments would settle it: a CFTC stay notice, a new or amended staff letter, or a clarification from Coinbase Derivatives.

Whichever arrives first tells us whether the first U.S.-listed crypto index perpetual goes live on schedule or lands in a 90-day review.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has told prediction-market operators Novig and ProphetX not to list a set of sports event contracts the two companies had already self-certified, according to Front Office Sports. The agency also asked both firms to explain how the markets would comply with federal commodities law.

A CFTC spokesperson confirmed that letters had been sent to both companies. “Any derivatives contract offered by a CFTC-regulated exchange must be compliant with the Commodity Exchange Act,” the spokesperson said.

Micro contracts and scorigami

The two letters target different products. Novig was told that its micro contracts, such as a market on which player opens the scoring in a basketball game, are open to manipulation. ProphetX was told that its scorigami contracts, which settle on whether a game finishes with a scoreline that has never occurred in that sport, do not meet the Commodity Exchange Act’s definition of a swap.

Sources told Front Office Sports that the letters describe the filings as false self-certifications, the term used when an exchange certifies a contract that may not be permitted under the statute. Neither set of contracts had been listed, so no customer has traded on them.

ProphetX framed the request more narrowly. A company spokesperson said CFTC staff “requested that we voluntarily defer listing the Scorigami contracts pending further review, not that we withdraw them.” The company said it complied, has since supplied further analysis on the contracts’ compliance and economic utility, and remains in contact with agency staff. Novig did not immediately respond to a request for comment, the outlet reported.

A signal to the wider market

Under the current framework, exchanges can self-certify and list new contracts without prior approval, and the CFTC can step in afterward. The agency would not say if it has ever before told an operator to hold back a self-certified sports contract. One source described the letters as “kind of a warning shot” for the sector.

The move does not appear to be limited to two companies. Senior CFTC officials have also spoken with DraftKings and FanDuel and cautioned that listing micro contracts could bring the same response, according to the report. Other exchanges have voiced frustration with the agency’s stance, pointing out that many state regulators allow comparable in-game wagers at licensed sportsbooks.

The letters follow a separate round of CFTC inquiries last month into incentive programs at Kalshi, Polymarket, Rothera and Novig. They also come ahead of a final rule the agency is expected to issue after its June proposal, a 267-page notice that would restrict in-game props and limit markets on high school sports. The proposal sits inside a wider dispute over how sports contracts are treated under federal rules, with several states arguing those products should fall under gambling law.

Leagues have pushed for limits

One source said the Novig and ProphetX markets were exactly the type of event contracts professional leagues have objected to most. On October 19, the NBA tightened the rules on player props and micro bets for its sportsbook partners, among them markets on who scores a game’s first basket, Yogonet reported. Those league rules cover traditional sportsbooks, not prediction markets.

It is not clear what the CFTC would do if either company went ahead and listed the contracts. Both firms have the chance to make their case to the agency, and the outcome is likely to shape how far other exchanges push into single-play sports markets before the final rule lands. Sports already account for most of the activity on the largest platforms, as shown in recent data on NFL trading volume across prediction markets.

What’s Downshifting? The Trend That’s Helping Homeowners Do More With Less

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Sometimes less IS more. At least, that’s what one home and lifestyle trend would have you believe.

A house can demand more time and money than homeowners expect. Spare bedrooms fill up with things nobody uses, the yard needs another afternoon of work, and there’s always something that needs fixing. Downshifting is one way homeowners are trying to change that.

Here’s what downshifting means, how it’s different from downsizing, and whether it can actually save money.

What Downshifting Means and How to Get Started at Home

Downshifting is pretty much what it sounds like. It’s a decision to slow things down a bit, spend less, and get rid of some of the things that take up too much time or space around the house.

Permaculture writer David Holmgren covers the idea in his book RetroSuburbia, which describes downshifting as “a conscious choice and action to shift to a lifestyle with less consumption, complexity, stress and status seeking.” The idea is to make everyday life a little easier to manage, whether that’s by cutting expenses or having fewer responsibilities.

It sounds a lot like downsizing, but the two aren’t quite the same. Downsizing usually means moving to a smaller house or getting rid of belongings. Downshifting doesn’t necessarily involve moving at all.

One family might trade a large house for a smaller place that needs less maintenance. Another might stay right where they are, stop buying things they don’t need, or spend less time working on the house.

Homeowners can start by looking at what’s taking up the most time or money. Maybe that’s a garage full of things that haven’t been used in years, or a spare bedroom that’s become a storage room. Clearing out those spaces can mean less to clean and look after.

Other changes can be pretty small, too. Borrowing a tool instead of buying one, fixing furniture rather than replacing it, or starting a small vegetable garden are all examples. The Environmental Protection Agency also recommends buying less and reusing things to help reduce waste.

Even looking for ways to lower utility bills, like sealing drafty windows or keeping up with heating and cooling maintenance, can be part of downshifting.

How Downshifting Can Affect Household Expenses and Daily Life

One reason people consider downshifting is to save money. Buying fewer things and using less energy can certainly help, but moving to a smaller home doesn’t automatically mean smaller bills.

“New homeowners are often surprised by the costs of owning property,” according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The agency recommends putting together a budget that includes property taxes, insurance, repairs, and other expenses beyond the mortgage.

There are some practical things to consider, too. A smaller house might not have enough room for a growing family, and moving farther away could mean a longer commute. Even growing vegetables or repairing things around the house takes time.

Still, for some households, having fewer things to pay for and maintain can leave more time for family, hobbies, or just enjoying the house they already have.

And that’s really the point of downshifting. It doesn’t have to mean selling a house or getting rid of everything. Sometimes it’s as simple as deciding the house doesn’t need another project or another piece of furniture.

Musician Allie Teilz says becoming a mom made her “bigger, not smaller”

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s dinnertime at Allie Teilz’s house, and the music is already on. Perhaps a film score courtesy of Italian classicist Morricone, maybe some Italian disco easing in behind it. One of the twins, age 3, is already dancing to it. The phones go in the ceramic cow on the counter for the remainder of the evening, where her almost-8-year-old likes them. There’s a projector, a record player, and a wall of VHS tapes with Yellow Submarine and The Land Before Time on heavy rotation. Teilz calls it, half-joking, a ’90s-electronics household. 

She is, for the record, impossibly cool. A vinyl DJ and producer who came up in Los Angeles nightlife sharing booths with the likes of Grace Jones and Daft Punk, she now scours thrift stores and estate sales for those VHS tapes and describes herself as “a mother first and an artist second.” She is a mother of four who weaves those two versions of herself authentically into one. 

We talked for an hour, and I could have gone three more. Somewhere in the course of our call she made me wish, for a second, that I could do the whole motherhood thing over again. My kids are grown and nearly grown, and I am not in the market for a redo. Still, the childhood she is building for hers, steeped in art and music and curiosity, had me calculating.

Her debut album, Come to Mommy, out now, is the sound of her reclaiming the artist within after the babies arrived. It’s atmospheric, irresistibly dance-able and laced with an electronic vibe that’s both throwback and futuristic. The title turns “come to daddy” on its head. The culture asks what Daddy wants. Teilz would rather ask what Mommy wants, and she answers without flinching. “Mommy wants joy,” she says. “She wants to have fun and dance. She wants pleasure and joy.”

We can let motherhood deepen the art

Teilz doesn’t treat her kids as the thing she makes art around. She treats them as the reason her art got deeper. “Becoming their mom,” she says, “only made everything bigger, not smaller.” Motherhood taught her that “the deepest creativity can come from having very little time and knowing exactly what matters.” 

There’s a surrender in becoming a parent, she says, a soul handing itself over to lead someone else through life, and for her it cracked the work open instead of closing it down. It “liberated me in creation,” she says, and gave her “a deeper well to pull from.” She has taken to calling motherhood “the highest form of art I’ve ever experienced.”

It changed what she wants to make, too. She looks at her own work through her children’s eyes now and holds it to a single north star. What do I want to see more of in the world? Certainly not  “AI art and lobotomy chic,” her encapsulation of the soulless, machine-driven work she’s watched creep in. She wants her kids to have what she had, the feeling of hearing Talking Heads for the first time and being free, the permission to flail around in  an imaginary version of David Byrne’s giant suit and stay in “that beautiful state of play.” Come to Mommy is her attempt to put that feeling back into the world, and she credits her kids as the inspiration. “They’re the greatest teachers,” she says.

We don’t have to choose a singular version of ourselves

Most women are familiar with the shift that happens when you become a mother. The self gets smaller to make room for everyone else, and for a while Teilz felt it, sliding toward the motherhood she’d watched other women vanish into. The mom groups where everyone gave and gave without ever taking their own needs into consideration. The careers set down and never picked back up. The inability to truly answer the question, “Who am I?” She calls out the insidious culprits that get woven into culture. “We’re indoctrinated by The Giving Tree and The Rainbow Fish,” she says, the children’s books that sell self-erasure as generosity. Make yourself a stump and you’ll be loved. Give away your sparkles and you’ll be accepted. “That’s such a dangerous thing,” Teilz observes.

For a long stretch, her own priorities became submerged. What pulled her back was simple. Boundaries. A flip phone for the hours she refuses to be reachable. She points to a line she saw online, “have boundaries or get an autoimmune disorder,” and says that her health was what finally made her stop. “My kids don’t need a mom who disappears into anything,” she says. “Even motherhood.”

We get to decide what their childhood feels like

Teilz builds her kids’ childhood the way she builds a record, out of specific, deliberate, slightly magical details. When her daughter earned a leader award at school and got to choose the celebration, she asked to make “ice cream mountains” with scoops stacked into a castle on parchment paper, modeled on the shop Teilz loved as a girl. Ridiculous for the sake of being ridiculous. The memories Teilz recalls most fondly from her own childhood aren’t the cruise or the big trip. They’re the one-bedroom apartment and her mom letting the kids pick a favorite ice cream before the first day of school. Now she makes the small, arguably more impactful kind of memories for her own crew.

There’s also a healthy helping of wonder. The kids get sent out to the garden to pull parsley or a tomato so their hands are involved in making dinner–she’s been teaching them to grow things from seed to fruition for years, because watching a baby artichoke push up from a seed you watered is the kind of small miracle she wants them to appreciate. Books work the same way. She still talks about a woman she once watched on the subway, reading beside her small daughter while the car churned around them, the two of them just reading, an image that stuck with her long before she had kids of her own. Now one of her proudest moments is her daughter climbing onto the couch to get lost in Harry Potter. “Stay curious about the world,” she tells other mothers. “Keep reading. Stay interested in what matters to you.” 

None of this came easily. Come to Mommy was written through a brutal stretch that included a traumatic birth, one that nearly cost Teilz and one of her sons their lives, and the album is partly the record of her finding the way back. The final track, Back From the Dead, was written for the twin she almost lost. It’s her way of telling all of her kids that they can come back from the worst thing. Making art out of it was a way to process, and she says, proof to her children that you can take something that breaks you and “make something beautiful out of the ashes.”

Near the end, I painted an image for her. A mom pulls into the driveway and sits in the car a minute before going in, the album still playing. What does she want that woman to carry inside with her? Teilz didn’t hesitate. She wants her to “steal the fire” of her own passion, to think of the song she danced hardest to at her best friend’s 13th birthday, to feel held, to have a reason to dance. Mostly she wants her to feel more like herself.

Not many people tell women that the self doesn’t have to shrink to make room for mothering. Teilz spends a whole album saying it.

There was a time when buying a cheap new car was relatively simple. The answer might have involved crank windows, manual door locks, an AM/FM radio, and seats upholstered in something that looked suspiciously like industrial carpeting.

Today, the idea of a truly low-budget, no-frills vehicle is getting hard to find. Even the least expensive new cars come with touchscreens, driver-assistance systems, connectivity features, multiple airbags, and enough electronic wizardry to make a 1990s luxury car look like a farm tractor by comparison.

That raises a reasonable question: Can you still buy a genuinely inexpensive, basic vehicle in 2026? The short answer is yes, but “basic” no longer means what it used to.

The No-Frills Car Isn’t Really No-Frills Anymore

Before we talk about what no-frills, low-budget vehicles are available, it’s important that we set a baseline for what that kind of car is in today’s market. 

Modern safety and fuel economy technology are two of the biggest reasons today’s inexpensive vehicles don’t look anything like their ancestors. A new car sold in the United States must meet extensive federal safety requirements. That means airbags, stability control, rearview backup cameras, crash protection, and a long list of other requirements.

Consumers have also developed expectations. Try selling a new car without air conditioning today and see how quickly the internet catches fire. Nobody wants to return to the days when a buyer had to choose between air conditioning and an AM/FM radio. Budget buyers today expect power windows, remote locks, smartphone connectivity, and decent climate control.

The result is an interesting middle ground. Today’s cheapest cars aren’t truly bare-bones. Automakers have figured out how to put a surprising amount of technology into relatively inexpensive vehicles without necessarily making them luxury cars.

The $20,000 Car Is a Unicorn

For years, the sub-$20,000 new car was the entry point for budget-conscious buyers. Models like the Mitsubishi Mirage, Nissan Versa, Chevrolet Spark, and various tiny hatchbacks gave shoppers an inexpensive way into a new vehicle.

Many of those cars are gone now. The market has steadily moved toward larger vehicles, more equipment, higher prices, and greater complexity. That doesn’t mean inexpensive cars have disappeared. It means the definition of inexpensive has moved upward. A buyer looking for a new vehicle in the low-$20,000 range can still find options, but those vehicles aren’t exactly stripped-down A-to-B appliances anymore.

Even inexpensive cars now tend to have infotainment screens, Bluetooth, backup cameras, automatic emergency braking, lane-departure warnings, and other features that were once the exclusive territory of expensive luxury models. 

“More Expensive” Is Not Actually a Problem

The economy in the U.S. has changed significantly. Not just from COVID and various political changes, but also from the general mindset of most Americans. When we compare pricing from 30 or even just 15 years ago, we often mentally skew the results due to our perspective of that change. It’s important to remember that, thanks to inflation, a dollar from 2011 is not a dollar in 2026. That ‘11 dollar is now $1.49. Inflation compounds and that 15 years has meant a 49 percent decrease in value for our money. 

Knowing that, we can compare the average new vehicle purchase price (currently about $51,000) today with the average 15 years ago ($30,500). Then we can compare those values to the average household income from both eras: $50,000 in 2011 and $87,500 in 2026. Doing that, we find that things haven’t changed much. 

The average new vehicle price seems very high compared to 15 years ago, but it was about 60 percent of the average household income in 2011 and is still that for 2026. 

Are Used Cars Where the Real Bargains Live?

Yes and no. If your definition of low-budget means “I need reliable transportation and I don’t care whether it has the latest technology,” the used market is still where you’ll get a good deal. But a good deal doesn’t always mean good value. Shopping for a used vehicle is a whole discussion unto itself and countless books, videos, and articles have been made detailing the do’s and don’ts.

What’s important and relevant here is to realize that while a three- to seven-year-old compact car can provide much of the transportation value that new economy cars once offered, it will come with some downsides. The trick is to shop based on condition and ownership history rather than simply finding the cheapest four-door vehicle.

What’s more, the used car market has changed significantly since the COVID blip. Up until just before the shutdowns began, the used car market remained relatively steady in pricing versus the new car market, with long-term changes largely following inflation. During COVID, used car prices jumped by about 49 percent as demand for vehicles collided with a lack of new vehicles due to closures. Afterward, as new cars began to once again appear on dealership lots, the used car market eventually saw less demand and adjusted accordingly.

That adjustment, however, wasn’t back down to pre-COVID numbers. It’s still 17 percent higher than it was. And it seems to have decided to hold steady there. The reason used cars are much more expensive is, again, supply and demand. This time between used car availability and demand from buyers wanting them. There are fewer used cars being sold because owners are keeping their used cars longer. This disparity and its higher price result has been a big driver in new car sales for the past few years. 

So, Can You Still Buy One?

Yes, but you have to adjust your expectations. If your definition of “no-frills” is “nothing I don’t need,” the new-car market still has possibilities. If your definition is “give me an engine, four wheels, a steering wheel, and absolutely nothing else,” you’re probably shopping used or waiting on the upcoming Slate pickup truck.

For now, here are the five lowest-cost new vehicles you can buy in 2026, ranked by base model price tag and not including shipping and destination charges:

  1. Hyundai Venue, $20,550 – the subcompact size and lower-cost interior materials are the primary reason for this car’s low price tag. 
  2. Chevrolet Trax, $21,700 – as a subcompact SUV, the Trax has a long-lived engine that’s cheap to build and a lack of interior amenities to keep its price low.
  3. Kia K4, $22,290 – previously the Forte, the K4 has long been Kia’s lowest-cost vehicle and has kept competitive through high volume global sales.
  4. Nissan Kicks, $22,430 – the Kicks replaces the Versa as Nissan’s budget vehicle, but at a higher cost due to its SUV styling.
  5. Nissan Sentra, $22,600 – a long-lived and well-accepted sedan, the Sentra is now the lowest-cost car in Nissan’s lineup.
