I bought a smartphone last month without visiting a single brand’s website. An AI assistant compared five options for me, explained why one suited my fitness style better than the others, and I jumped over to Amazon and bought it. The smartphone company may know I bought its product, but it doesn’t know what I told the AI assistant, which features I prioritized, or what other options I considered.

What catches leaders off guard isn’t that customers ask AI for recommendations. That debate is over. It’s that marketing organizations built around campaigns, calendars, and customers who do their own searching, comparing, and deciding struggle to adapt to AI-mediated customer journeys. When AI assistants influence those decisions, brands can lose visibility into the journey long before a purchase happens.

When I talk to leaders about building AI-first businesses, marketing is usually the last function they consider, almost an afterthought once they sort out technology and operations. Marketing deserves first consideration. That means rethinking how marketing operates, how brands reach customers through AI, and what capabilities teams need to support both.

From campaigns to a continuous growth engine

The first change is structural. Marketing in an AI-first business needs to operate continuously across the customer lifecycle rather than through isolated campaigns tied to a calendar.

According to McKinsey, human-agent teams, where AI systems handle always-on orchestration and people set direction and judgment, can improve marketing ROI by up to 30%. 

Instead of asking, “What happened last month?” you need to ask, “What’s changing right now, and what should the response be?” Insight moves from something you generate in a retrospective to something you act on live. This also changes what leaders measure. Counting emails sent or campaigns launched tells you almost nothing useful in this model.

Tracking bottom-line impact matters, but it’s only half the job. If AI automation saves a marketer a fifth of their time and nobody decides what that time should be spent on, the business gains nothing — it simply watches the savings disappear into the void of looking busy until 5 p.m. 

Leaders need to set explicit value goals from day one, watch relentlessly whether they’re meeting those goals, and actively redirect whatever time and budget AI frees up into the work that actually grows the business. A dashboard that only counts activity will tell you everything except whether any of it mattered. 

Marketing to machines, not just to people

Leaders must accept an uncomfortable reality: AI assistants increasingly influence which brands their customers consider. As AI assistants filter, summarize, and recommend on a person’s behalf, a brand must be accessible and trusted by machines before it reaches the person asking. The principle is similar to optimizing for search engines, but now extends to language models and AI agents. Just as companies optimized for how Google ranked them, they now need to optimize for how an AI system understands, verifies, and recommends them.

Some in the industry call this AEO. Others call it GEO. Both approaches build on SEO’s emphasis on discoverability and credibility, focusing on how AI systems find, interpret, and recommend brands. That requires building a knowledge engine — a structured base of machine-readable signals that includes detailed technical specifications, verified expert input, and information that an AI system can actually ingest and check, rather than infer or guess at.

This is an important piece of infrastructure for brands that want to influence how AI systems understand and recommend their products. It also demands a new kind of internal ownership — someone responsible for ensuring the brand is represented accurately in AI discovery systems and that the underlying data is structured so machines can interpret it.

Rebuilding the organization around AI-specific capabilities

The third change concerns the capabilities marketing organizations need to support this shift. McKinsey identifies five pillars: continuous insights, scaled creativity, hyperpersonalization, agentic commerce, and always-on orchestration.

Hyperpersonalization deserves particular attention. Done properly, with agents dynamically deciding the next best message or offer for each individual customer in real time, it can increase revenue by 5% to 8%. This goes beyond inserting a customer’s first name into an email. It’s a genuine 1:1 journey, built and adjusted as the customer moves through it. 

Creativity, too, must function as infrastructure rather than as a manual production line. On-brand assets should be generated and refined in minutes, not weeks. However, protecting the brand at this speed requires codifying voice and visual identity into reusable AI guardrails, moving them out of people’s heads and into the system.

Talent is where most leaders will feel the change most directly. Forward-thinking teams are creating hybrid roles that combine marketing expertise with AI and data capabilities. This includes people who blend cultural judgment with data fluency to decide how a brand shows up across a customer’s fragmented journey, and specialists who own the data models that make individualized experiences possible at scale. Titles will vary across organizations, but teams need to develop these skills rather than assume they already exist.

Marketers still need instinct, judgment, and creativity. What changes is where they apply those skills — continuously rather than episodically, with an awareness of AI intermediaries and a greater focus on building systems.

This change doesn’t announce itself like a new platform launch. There’s no keynote moment, no single Tuesday when AI assistants begin influencing more purchasing decisions. It happens quietly, one purchase at a time, the way mine did with a smartphone.

So the question I’d leave you with isn’t whether this affects your business. It already does. It’s whether your marketing organization would notice a valuable customer journey it never even saw.

Vox launches its first app with an eye toward building paid memberships

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Vox has launched its first mobile application, the publication best known for its explanatory journalism announced Wednesday morning.

The app, which puts Vox’s text, audio, and video in one feed, is the company’s latest bet on growing paid membership. Users can choose between a home tab of featured pieces or a chronological feed of the latest stories. While the app is free to download and browse, it’s catered toward serving Vox journalism to paid subscribers in one place. Vertical videos and podcasts are free to watch and listen to, but only paid subscribers can read text stories and listen to podcasts ad-free.

Vox declined to share specific paid membership numbers, though the company said in May that it had “tens of thousands of paid subscribers.” The program was launched in May 2024. James Murdoch — son of media mogul Rupert Murdoch — purchased Vox.com along with the Vox Media Podcast Network and New York Magazine for more than $300 million in May 2026.

Growing membership is a “key priority,” managing editor for audience and membership Bill Carey said. Vox believes launching the app is one way to do that. A Vox membership costs $5 per month, or $60 per year, and offers ad-free versions of podcasts and unlimited access to all of the website’s contents.

Strengthening its relationship to its audience is only becoming more important for Vox. The app launch comes at a time when news publishers are increasingly trying to keep users on their own platforms amid growing preferences for AI summaries, news creators, and industry-wide traffic declines.

“The move toward developing our membership program in the last couple of years has really been about understanding how important it is for us to build more community and to foster that loyalty [of our] audience,” Carey said. “When we were looking at that, it really felt like an app was a good way to continue pointing in that direction.”

Vox isn’t the only news outlet focusing on mobile apps. The Verge — Vox’s former sister publication under Vox Media — named traffic declines as one of the reasons why it plans to launch its own app next year. The Wall Street Journal’s head of digital, Taneth Evans, recently said that the Journal makes its app more useful to users by offering all of its mediums in one place. Evans also said that news publishers “need to give people reasons to open an app beyond simply offering news.”

“Websites will become a service for agents,” Evans said. “Apps are gonna be the thing that humans will connect with.”

Vox declined to provide website traffic numbers, but data from Comscore shows that the website’s unique visitors decreased by 36% year-over-year between September 2025 (7.4 million) and September 2026 (4.8 million). Archived data from SimilarWeb shows the site had 20.8 million visits in September 2021.

Vox is one of the most popular news brands on YouTube with more than 12.7 million followers. The publication has one million Instagram followers, plus Substack subscribers and paid Patreon members. Vox launched its Patreon in November 2025, though its two video series exclusive to the platform have not been updated since January.

“Even if there wasn’t a decline in search traffic, as everyone has been seeing — and we still get members from search — we want to create that relationship with our audience,” publisher and editor-in-chief Swati Sharma said. “People know Vox and they have a relationship with Vox. We have a younger audience that’s on all of these different platforms. I think it is definitely a part of building that relationship ourselves.”

That the app makes all Vox content accessible in one place for current and future members also signals the “un-siloing” of the newsroom overall, Sharma said. Vox currently has 78 editorial employees.

When Sharma, who has helmed Vox.com since 2021, first arrived, she found that Vox’s main mediums (text, audio, and video) “weren’t in service of one another.” The “silos” allowed teams to build their own audiences and products. But while teams discussed story ideas with each other and during editorial meetings, “they all [needed] to go into their creative spaces and find what works for their medium and not be in service of just one larger section.”

That’s changed over the past five years, Sharma said. Each team — text, video, and audio — does a little bit of everything now. Text reporters appear on podcasts and in videos, while podcasters and hosts also write their own newsletters.

“In a lot of newsrooms, there’s one dominant form, and at Vox, there wasn’t. They’re all really important,” Sharma said. “[Senior correspondent] Dylan Scott is on our Unexplainable podcast talking about Alzheimer’s. [Host and editorial director] Astead Herndon does a newsletter and a Substack Live every week, and he does a podcast. That’s the future for us, and that’s where our whole newsroom is moving, where those silos are done and you can basically do everything.”

Exclusive: Numeracle Raises $28M To Keep Legitimate Business Calls From Spam Label

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Numeracle has raised $28 million from Edison Partners to help businesses whose calls are incorrectly labeled as spam or blocked by phone carriers, it tells Crunchbase News exclusively.

The McLean, Virginia-based company verifies businesses and links their identities to the phone numbers they use to contact customers. It shares that information with the systems carriers use to screen calls and works to remove incorrect labels.

Rebekah Johnson, founder and CEO of Numericle. (Courtesy photo)

The financing brings Numeracle’s total funding to $34.8 million since its 2018 inception. That total includes a $6.8 million Series A raised in December 2023, founder and CEO Rebekah Johnson told Crunchbase News in an interview. Edison was the sole investor in the new round. Numeracle declined to disclose its valuation.

The company says it has roughly 4,000 business customers and about 85 employees. It became profitable this year, sooner than Johnson had expected.

Numeracle plans to use its new capital to hire and expand internationally. The company also plans to adapt its business verification tools for use beyond phone calls. For example, Johnson said the company is eyeing messaging, chat apps, marketplaces and AI agents.

Its expansion comes as businesses simultaneously face two related problems. For one,  fraudsters are increasingly impersonating them. Meanwhile, call-screening systems can prevent their own calls from reaching customers.

For example, that could mean a patient ignoring a call from a hospital about an upcoming procedure. For a bank, it could mean a customer declining a legitimate call because the phone displays a fraud warning.

Numeracle charges an annual subscription to monitor and address incorrect spam labels. Customers can also pay a per-call fee to display their business name, logo and reason for calling on a recipient’s phone.

From a regulatory warning to a company

Johnson, an engineer who previously worked in regulatory compliance at telecom provider West, said she recognized the problem while participating in the Federal Communications Commission’s Robocall Strike Force in 2016.

She warned carriers that their efforts to identify unwanted calls would also affect legitimate businesses because the screening systems lacked information about who was calling.

About three months later, she said, a carrier’s federal regulatory counsel called her while she was in her parents’ kitchen.

“Everything you said was going to happen is happening. What do we do?” she recalled him asking.

Johnson said that conversation prompted her to start Numeracle.

She was a newly single mother who had lost her job and used her life savings to start the business. Living on a farm in Grand Bay, Alabama, without internet access, she worked from cafes.

“When you need to feed your children and provide a roof over their head, you’re going to count every penny,” Johnson said.

That experience shaped her spending decisions as Numeracle grew, she said. The company operated for several years before raising its Series A.

Johnson said about 90% of Numeracle’s sales inquiries come through unpaid inbound channels. With the new funding, she plans to spend more on marketing to specific industries, including healthcare.

The company’s customers also include businesses in financial services, home services and higher education, as well as government organizations and charities. Johnson declined to identify individual customers.

Verifying increasingly convincing fakes

Numeracle first checks that a business is real and does what it claims. Once it passes those checks, the company links it to the phone numbers it uses and shares that information with the systems carriers use to flag or block calls.

Other companies can also pay Numeracle to run those checks on businesses they’re considering taking on as customers.

A business call from Numeracle, delivered with name, logo, and reason for calling. (Photo courtesy of Numeracle)

Johnson said Numeracle built its own verification tools because existing services weren’t thorough enough. Fraudsters have an incentive to get through: If Numeracle approves them, their calls are less likely to be flagged as spam.

AI has made it more difficult to tell which businesses are real, she said. Numeracle has encountered fake companies with state registrations, employer identification numbers and supposed employees who have active LinkedIn profiles.

“The scary part for me isn’t necessarily the AI voices,” Johnson said. “It’s actually AI-built businesses.”

She said Numeracle uses AI to help identify those fabrications, though she declined to describe the methods in detail.

Numeracle currently operates in the United States. Johnson said interest from Europe has prompted it to move forward with its international expansion plans, with some of the new funding going toward that effort.

The broader question for the company is whether businesses will pay to use its verification services across other forms of communication. For now, its business centers on a more immediate problem — helping customers recognize a legitimate phone call.

Investor POV: ‘Doing more with less’

For Lenard Marcus, a general partner at Edison Partners, Johnson’s telecom and engineering experience was one major reason to invest. In an interview with Crunchbase News, he said her industry knowledge helped her build both the product and relationships with companies that could bring it to customers.

He was also drawn to how much Numeracle had accomplished with relatively little funding, describing Johnson as “someone who was frankly doing more with less.”

Customer interviews helped make the case. Marcus recalled one contact center operator who said Numeracle had increased the number of calls answered from 19,000 to 22,000 out of 40,000 calls a month. For businesses trying to reach prospective customers, that increase in conversations could lead to more sales.

Marcus also believes that Johnson’s years in telecom give Numeracle an advantage over newer competitors. In addition to understanding how the different parts of the industry work together, Johnson has used those relationships to get Numeracle in front of large companies, he said.

“She’s sales. She’s engineering, and she’s just a very, very good leader,” Marcus added.

Looking ahead, the investor also sees demand across healthcare, retail, home services and finance, with AI making impersonation more prevalent.

“This is a communications problem, not one for one industry,” he said.

Studies Show Diet Sodas May Not Be Stalling Weight Loss as Much as Previously Thought

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Artificially sweetened drinks are often demonized based on the theory that they may encourage a greater appetite, or supercharge our sugar cravings, but recent work appears to dispel the bad rap around diet sodas and gaining weight. Now, a two-year clinical trial from the University of Liverpool, England, has provided the greatest insight yet, into the long-term effects of diet drinks on weight loss.

The groundbreaking study, conducted over the course of 104 weeks and published in the British Journal of Nutrition, attempted to build on a similar report in 2023 that also found no proof for the theory that diet sodas can stall weight loss.

Do Diet Drinks Cause Weight Gain or Cravings? What the 2-Year SWITCH Trial Proves

While both studies pitted water against zero and low-calorie drinks that were free of sugar (also known as non-nutritive, or NNS beverages) the continued efforts to find the true value of diet drinks is critical, because sugar sweetened beverages often account for a substantial source of dietary sugar, excess calories, and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes. So, if science can show that NNS drinks do not lead to increased sugar intake or excess appetite, they could instead play a part in a balanced diet. To that end, the Liverpool University study has been described as the longest, most comprehensive trial in this area to date. “This study provides the long‑term clinical evidence that has been missing from international discussions,” explained Professor John Halford, who was lead author on the project.

2-Year Clinical Trial Comparison: Diet Drinks vs. Water

493 overweight or obese adults were entered into a structural weight management program and then split into those who only drank water, and those who consumed a choice of 20 commercially available diet drinks containing aspartame, sucralose, and acesulfame.  Each drink contained less than 2.1 calories per 100ml, with no overall daily calorie cap on drinks. The NNS group were allowed to drink water, but they were asked to drink at least two 330ml servings per day of a diet beverage.

Interestingly, neither group was not blocked from drinking sugary beverages, but they did received coaching to reduce them.

What were the results after 104 weeks?

  •   Weight loss difference between NNS and water groups deemed insignificant
  •   Both groups maintained sustained weight reduction for the full 2 years
  •   Both groups saw reductions in waist size along with improved body composition

The study also found that overall sugar intake decreased slightly greater in those drinking the NNS beverages, seemingly disproving the theory that artificially sweetened drinks encourage sugar cravings, and suggesting the opposite effect may occur.

“These latest findings show that concerns about sweeteners disrupting appetite or causing weight gain are not supported when tested in a rigorous, long-term randomized trial,” said Professor Harrold. “We hope this evidence informs future WHO guidance and public health policy.”

The Invisible Graph: How Companies Grow Revenue And Still Lose Value

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I got off the phone with a potential client whose company is doing great. The client, however, is not even thinking of selling the company as they see future growth over the next couple of years that they wish to materialize in order to get a larger exit.

The conversation prompted me to consider something I believe many entrepreneurs miss, and it’s this: Revenue can rise while enterprise value falls.

When revenue and value move in opposite directions

Founders and boards routinely track revenue and profitability over time. But another graph deserves equal attention: enterprise value. Its trajectory can turn downward while the operating numbers continue improving, with significant implications for M&A timing.

Consider a hypothetical SaaS company generating $15 million in annual recurring revenue, valued at 6x ARR. Its implied enterprise value is $90 million. Two years later, ARR reaches $20 million, but the multiple falls to 3.5x revenue. Enterprise value drops to $70 million. Revenue increased 33%, while value declined 22%.

These are only illustrative assumptions to demonstrate how multiple compression can overshadow operating growth. For a founder postponing a sale until the next revenue milestone, the practical implication is clear: Reaching that milestone may still produce a smaller exit.

Qualitative, absolute and relative factors

The revenue multiplier reflects several dimensions that evolve differently. Qualitative factors include management quality, founder dependence, competitive intensity, product differentiation and strategic relevance to buyers.

Absolute financial measures include revenue, EBITDA and cash flow.

Relative performance measures include growth, margins, net revenue retention and gross revenue retention. Although retention metrics are expressed as percentages, their importance lies in what they reveal about customer durability and expansion. Buyers assess these measures together and compare them with relevant peers. As companies mature, their absolute results can improve while their growth rates weaken.

Meanwhile, stronger profitability, better retention or deeper management capabilities may support a higher multiplier. The valuation outcome depends on the combined effect. Boards should therefore ask which characteristics will improve over the next year, which could deteriorate and how buyers might price those changes.

M&A preparation should begin while you are winning

A sale requires preparation, buyer engagement, negotiation and diligence, during which buyers continually reassess performance and forecasts.

Waiting until a slowdown becomes visible can weaken negotiating leverage and raise questions about management’s projections. Strategic buyer demand also follows its own timetable. A buyer may place substantial value on acquiring a particular technology, customer base or geographic presence today. That interest can diminish after an internal product launch, a competing acquisition or a budget change.

Waiting can still create considerable value when additional scale attracts more buyers, profitability improves or concentration falls. Boards can make that case tangible by reviewing estimated enterprise value alongside the operating plan, using scenarios in which revenue grows while the multiple holds, expands or contracts. They should also consider how debt, cash, dilution, liquidation preferences and transaction terms affect shareholder proceeds. Nobody can reliably identify the valuation peak in advance.

However, every decision to delay a sale embeds a view about future valuation. That view deserves its own graph.

From Monk To Startups: One Founder’s Unusual Path To Building Government Software

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: The following is the latest profile in a series of articles about startup founders from non-technical backgrounds who have launched successful venture-backed companies. Read the previous interviews in the series including with MagicSchool AI founder Adeel Khan here, Trunk Tools founder Sarah Buchner here, Tabs founder Ali Hussain here, Craft founder Ilya Levtov here, GC AI founder Cecilia Ziniti here, Incredible Health co-founder Iman Abuzeid here, and Hilt founder William “Will” Cielen here.

Lilly Chen was 16 and had been repeatedly hospitalized for Graves’ disease when her mother suggested they move to China and become monks. Frustrated with the care her daughter was receiving through Medicaid, she wanted to try something different.

Lilly Chen, co-founder and CEO of FSH Technologies. (Courtesy photo)

Chen left high school and spent about two years as a monk, ordained in the United States before spending several months at a monastery in China. She meditated, reread the same books, and gradually felt well enough to consider returning to school.

She went on to enter Colorado College without a high school diploma, briefly play video games professionally, and become an engineer at Facebook. When she quit that job, she didn’t have a startup idea. She just wanted to find work she believed in.

That mission eventually led her to FSH (pronounced “fish) Technologies,” a Philadelphia-based company that’s building software for some of the government’s least glamorous tasks, including administering school food services and managing public records.

After her brief stint at a large tech company, Chen realized she wanted to tackle the everyday problems that make people’s lives harder, even if solving them wouldn’t generate much excitement in Silicon Valley.

“What does it actually take to solve the really unsexy, boring stuff?” she remembers asking herself.

A different route to college

Before Chen was thinking about government software, she was trying to get well enough to return to school.

At the monastery in China, she meditated, ate food grown on-site or brought by nearby villagers, and lived without internet access. She continued taking thyroid medication and said her health became more manageable during that period.

She also read. A lot. With no nearby place to buy books, she reread the ones she had brought, finding different places around the monastery to sit with them.

Her attachment to those books prompted a conversation with a fellow monk who asked what she enjoyed about reading and where she might find more books. The discussion eventually turned to colleges, and why she wasn’t attending one.

Chen explained her health problems. He observed that she seemed well. She realized she was.

So Chen returned to the U.S., homeschooled herself and enrolled at Colorado College without having earned a high school diploma. She turned 20 during her freshman year and graduated in 2019 with a double major in mathematics and economics.

While she was in college, Chen briefly played Dota 2 professionally. She also played against OpenAI’s game-playing bots, an early encounter with AI technology she would later help support as an engineer.

After an initial job in Boston, she joined Facebook, where she built infrastructure used to train AI models.

Leaving without a startup idea

Then on Jan. 6, 2021, Chen was attending a remote company all-hands meeting while watching coverage of the attack on the U.S. Capitol on another screen. Her concerns about Facebook’s role in enabling the organization of violence made her question whether she could continue working there.

“I realized that I couldn’t work for Big Tech anymore,” she recalls.

She quit shortly afterward.

To figure out her next move, she returned to the sort of questions she had encountered as a monk. What made her happy? What mattered to her? What was she good at?

Programming remained her most concrete skill. She just needed to figure out where to apply it.

An introduction through Envision, a student-run accelerator focused on nontraditional founders, connected her with Contrary, a venture firm that has backed the likes of Ramp and Anduril Industries. The firm wrote Chen her first check in 2021, before she had even settled on the business that would become FSH.

Although she didn’t have a developed company pitch, Chen’s earnest explanation of what motivated her and her passion for solving a meaningful problem won investors over. That investment gave her room to experiment, she said.

FSH took shape into what it is today in 2024. The Contrary investor who first backed her, Jason Chen, has since joined the company as its chief operating officer.

The company has raised $25 million in total funding, including a recent $20 million Series A that Chen said came together in about a week. Participants in that raise included Lachy Groom, General Catalyst, Ramp, Scribble Ventures, Operator Partners, Basis Set Ventures. Contrary also led its seed financing.

Finding the problem beneath the paperwork

Ultimately, Chen landed on government software because in her view, it had the potential to solve many consequential problems that attracted relatively little excitement.

For example, permits, licenses, grant applications and other administrative processes can be slow for reasons that extend beyond an outdated interface, Chen noted. Information may exist in separate departments. Updating a website may require approvals from multiple stakeholders including policymakers, communications staff and an IT team.

Putting a chatbot on top of that system, Chen argues, leaves much of the underlying problem unresolved.

Her approach to solving some of the problems that exist within governments draws on an exchange she had years earlier at the monastery, where one of her chores was feeding koi.

The head monk would periodically ask her if she thought she was the fish or the pond?

Initially, Chen didn’t understand what he was asking. But he kept returning to the question, and she began thinking about it. Eventually, she picked a fish she thought resembled her: one that stayed out of fights, swam with the group and occasionally ventured off on its own.

The head monk’s response was, “You’re the pond, not the fish.”

Chen viewed the exchange as a lesson in looking beyond individual pieces to understand how a whole system works. Looking at government software, she believed that it meant examining why information is scattered or processes are slow before deciding what technology to build.

One early area for FSH has been K-12 food services, where administrators must perform tasks such as coordinating meal planning, nutritional information and reimbursement requirements. Another is records and archive management, including the paper documents that can still require residents to visit a government office to retrieve them.

FSH does use AI for tasks such as optical character recognition, translation and natural-language search. But, Chen said, the company treats it as a tool within its software, rather than relying on a chatbot or an AI agent to handle an entire process.

Government sales have required patience. The company generated less than $100,000 in revenue in its first year, Chen said, reflecting the time it takes to win contracts. At the time of this interview, FSH had roughly eight or nine employees, with additional offers outstanding.

Thinking slowly, moving quickly

Chen’s time as a monk has also influenced how she runs the company.

She admits she is skeptical of startup culture that treats sleeping in the office or working extremely long hours as evidence of commitment or a badge of pride. Rather, Chen views some of that behavior as performative, especially when it comes at the expense of the rest that she believes people need to work effectively.

Her preferred motto to the team is, “Slow is smooth and smooth is fast.”

“We think slowly about a lot of things, and then we do things quickly,” she said.

Chen believes it’s crucial to first understand the people, departments and procedures behind a government service before trying to improve it.

Looking back, Chen also credits her success to finding people willing to back someone whose experience didn’t resemble the usual résumé. That includes the college that admitted her without a high school diploma and an investor who wrote a check before she had settled on a company idea.

“You definitely end up finding your own way if you just stick to being weird,” she said.
