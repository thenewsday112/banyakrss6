Software Engineer - AI Tooling Infrastructure at Supabase

About Supabase

Supabase is the Postgres development platform, built by developers for developers. We provide a complete backend solution including Database, Auth, Storage, Edge Functions, Realtime, and Vector Search. All services are deeply integrated and designed for growth.


About the Role

We’re looking for a Software Engineer, AI Tooling Infrastructure to join our AI Tooling team and help build and run the backend services behind Supabase’s AI products. The Supabase MCP server is one example of what the team runs in production, alongside the services that support agent skills and the dashboard assistant. You’ll work closely with the Platform, SRE, Auth, Billing and Edge Functions teams, and with the engineers on the AI Tooling team who build the product surfaces.

The AI Tooling team runs its own services in production and carries its own on-call. Traffic from AI clients and agents behaves differently from traffic from people: hundreds of calls a minute, retry loops, and no reading of deprecation warnings. This role makes sure the services they use are reliable, correctly authorized and observable. It is a systems and infrastructure role rather than a product AI role, and it is not an SRE role. You will build and operate these systems, not only advise on them.

This role is ideal for someone who thrives in async, fast-paced environments, owns what they ship in production, and is excited about building developer tools that scale to millions.


What You’ll Be Responsible For

In this role, you’ll:

  • Build and operate the production backend services behind Supabase’s AI products, including the MCP server: deployment, scaling, configuration and release.

  • Take ownership of how those services are structured, including splitting or reshaping a service when that makes it safer to run and easier to change.

  • Define, measure and meet reliability targets for the services the team runs. Set SLOs with the SRE team, track error budgets, and use them to decide what to fix first.

  • Participate in the AI Tooling team on-call rotation, and be a first responder for incidents in those services. Investigate, fix, coordinate with other teams during incidents, and write the follow-ups and runbooks.

  • Work the AI Tooling team support queue alongside on-call, and turn recurring issues into fixes rather than repeated manual answers.

  • Own authentication and authorization for AI and agent clients: OAuth apps, scoped tokens, and permissions that make sense for agents as well as humans, including how we change or deprecate a permission without breaking existing integrations.

  • Own observability for the services the team runs: metrics, traces, logs, dashboards and alerts, so we find problems before customers do.

  • Handle capacity and abuse for agent traffic: rate limits, cost and safety controls, and behavior under load or when a dependency degrades.

  • Improve the architecture of the services the team owns as they grow, including splitting or reshaping a service when that makes it safer to run and easier to change.

  • Work with the Platform team on shared code, review ownership and release process, and with the SRE team on infrastructure, alerting and incident practice.

  • Contribute to the code of the AI Tooling team’s tools, such as the MCP server, where it affects reliability and security, in partnership with the engineers who own the product surface.


You Might Be a Good Fit If You

Must have:

  • Have 5+ years of experience building and running backend services in production, and have carried on-call for them.

  • Write strong TypeScript or Go, and are comfortable working in the other.

  • Have designed and operated APIs with real consumers: versioning, error handling, rate limiting, and backwards-compatible change.

  • Know OAuth, OIDC, JWT and API authorization well enough to debug them in production and to explain where implementations commonly deviate from the spec.

  • Have a good understanding of PostgreSQL and relational databases.

  • Are comfortable with CI/CD, Docker and containerized deployments, and with infrastructure as code. Any tool is fine.

  • Can debug across services: follow a slow or failing request through several systems using metrics, traces and logs.

  • Communicate clearly in writing, work with a wide range of stakeholders, and thrive in async, globally distributed teams.

  • Are comfortable navigating ambiguity and moving quickly.

Pick your centre of gravity. We expect you to be deep in at least one of these, and to tell us which:

  • APIs and auth: You have designed permission models, token scopes or OAuth flows for third-party or machine clients, and you have migrated them without breaking users.

  • Operations: You have run production services yourself: carried the pager, written alerts and runbooks, used SLOs or similar to decide what to fix, and made shared systems less risky over time.

  • Gateways and distributed systems: You have built or run proxies or gateways, or debugged hard failures across services (retries, partial failures, upstream outages, fail-open vs fail-closed decisions), and you can reason about load and degradation.

Nice to have:

  • Experience with agent tooling (MCP or similar), or serving traffic from AI agents and LLM clients.

  • Experience moving a service out of a larger codebase while keeping existing clients working.

  • Experience with Grafana, OpenTelemetry, Logflare or a similar observability stack, and real SLO work.

  • Open-source contributions, especially to Node.js, Go, TypeScript or API tooling.

  • ESOP

  • Health Benefits

  • Annual Off-Sites

  • Flexible Work

    We operate asynchronously and trust you to manage your own time. You know what needs to be done and when.

  • Professional Development

    Every team member receives an annual education allowance to spend on learning—courses, books, conferences, or anything that supports your growth.

About the Team

Supabase was born-remote and open-source-first. We believe our globally distributed team is our secret weapon in building tools developers love.

  • ~400 team members

  • 60+ countries

  • 20+ languages spoken

  • Over $1B raised (including our $500M Series F)

Alice Bucknell’s The Alluvials

 

In 2023, artist Alice Bucknell released The Alluvials, a video work filled with wolves, moths, and sediment that looks at a version of Los Angeles in the near future. In this California city, the sand that had once been characteristic of its beaches has taken over downtown, effectively turning the sprawl of highways and skyscrapers into a desert of dunes and dust. Through this speculative lens on an alternate reality, Bucknell presents both the history and consequences of this dried-up world. The work examines the history of the Tongva people of the Greater Los Angeles Basin while also imagining the water desalination corporation Qwench, a perpetually burning landscape, and an eternity in which the sky remains in its golden hour.

 

A year later, the project evolved into The Alluvials video game (2024), a multilevel game that explores the world inside the film. With each embodied exploration of its digital realm, the project presents the uncanny and nearly incomprehensible fallout of climate change. In their exploration of a not-so-far-off Los Angeles, Bucknell poses the questions: Is this how we will want to live? And if the answer is no, what modes of action do we have access to in order to reroute our course in the progression of climate change?

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video work, 2023 | all images courtesy the artist unless stated otherwise Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video work, 2023 | all images courtesy the artist unless stated otherwise

 

 

Embodied perspectives of NPCs

 

The word alluvial means of or relating to alluvium, which Merriam-Webster describes as ‘clay, silt, sand, gravel, or similar detrital material deposited by running water.’ In the game, Bucknell puts players in a kind of alluvial world, one that results from sand and silt accumulating where there should have been running water. Within it, there are four levels in which the player takes on the perspective of what are usually considered non-playable characters (NPCs). In doing so, Bucknell challenges traditional gameplay dynamics with what they refer to as ‘queer and ecological game mechanics.’

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024 Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

Exploring each level of The Alluvials

 

In one such level, Extinction Burst, the game opens with a bright red forest and a mountain lion sulking down a fuchsia path. The scene gives way to a dried-out basin filled with white particles. On closer inspection, these drifting flakes are revealed to be a form of moth. As you approach a desert tree growing near the center of the basin, a question in blue, hazy letters reveals itself in the sky: ‘ARE YOU MOTH OR ARE YOU TREE?’

 

Depending on whether you choose the moth or the tree path, you encounter two different perspectives of the desert landscape. In other levels, you walk through a forest fire, become a wolf in a barren, sand-ridden Los Angeles, or pilot an airboat speeding through the Los Angeles River in the evening. By presenting these alternative points of view, the game not only challenges the agency of the player but also offers an embodied understanding of things humans may take for granted: the knowledge of a moth, a yucca plant, or a body of water.

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

Exhibition history

 

The Alluvials has been exhibited in both film and video game formats in spaces across the US, Europe, and Asia. In each space, Bucknell’s work has come to life in different forms. When the film was shown at LUMINEX in downtown Los Angeles as a four-channel work, scenes of the forest fire lit up the building, creating an uncanny reflection of both the real and fictional city. At HEK Other Intelligences 2025 in Basel, Switzerland, pink camo beanbags were set up on the floor, presenting the game as a two-channel video game that visitors could sit down and play in a space awash in fuchsia.

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

How to live in the world of The Alluvials

 

Within Bucknell’s work, the artist brings together a work of speculative fiction that speaks from an unknown future but carries the threatening tone of a parable lived many times over. True to the worlds they build in game engines, Bucknell makes real the promises, threats, and predictions that the CEOs and climate scientists of our time launch into the news cycle. Yet here, there is an opportunity to progress, level by level, through the very possible consequences of our present-day actions if left unchecked. Here, the posthuman utopia is one in which we leave humanity behind—one that discards the anthropocentric thinking that leads to artificial waters—and instead move forward with a future informed by the logic and processes of the natural world, before it is too late.

“Increased to 85,000 capacity” claim for London Stadium – Report | West Ham News

In a week during which the future of West Ham’s home hit the media again thanks to the owners desperately seeking a way out of their financial cut de sac, some pretty spectacular claims were made about the former Olympic Stadium’s ability to host huge events in the future.

West Ham fans, used to enormous queues and delays just to get inside will find the jaw-dropping suggestion that a further twenty thousand people could be crammed into the arena pretty implausible.

Yes, it coped -somehow- aided by a veritable army of volunteers and impeccably behaved ‘olympic-going’ families, with 80,000 in 2012. But they weren’t all trying to arrive at exactly the same time, were they.

Add the temporary nature of the upper tiers and the constant leaks, queues for everything and the long, post-event shuffle back to Stratford station, unlit and with the hated walking traffic lights halting progress as the rain falls whilst the misery becomes abject, and you have a sporting venue wholly unfit even for its current 62,500 capacity.

London Stadium queues: Try doing it at night, in the rain and the dark. along with another 25,000 fans. No thanks.

Yet 85,000 is the claim. According to sportscape magazine:

“London Stadium Aims for 85,000-People Boxing Spectacles to Generate Income Post-Relegation..aiming to offset £19M losses and drive £15M local economic boost.”

“…By making a formal request to the Newham Council to have boxing bouts categorised as “other leisure events” so as to facilitate the increase in the maximum capacity of the event from 60,000 to 85,000.”

Clutching. At. Straws.

You’d hope the licensing authority will see sense and deny the venue permission. Anyone who’s tried to get to, and from the stadium on a match day will understand why adding another 20,000 – presumably standing on the floor of the arena – but with the same access arrangements, the same shortage of toilets and concessions, will understand the unsuitability of the arena and it’s dreadful surrounds for further expansion.

It needs knocking down and rebuilding, West Ham fans will all tell you that. But with the Hammers’ 99 year lease, that is never going to happen. So further ‘ideas’ to try and turn a profit will surely keep failing thanks to the poor commercial design, the very temporary nature of the upper parts of the structure and the facility- free wasteland that surrounds the ground.

85,000? Well, I won’t be one of them. Not even if you paid me.

Jeff Ekhator has finally rejoined his Juventus teammates on the training pitch. The young striker, signed from Genoa in the summer, took part in a full group session at the Continassa on Friday for the first time since a run of muscle problems that has so far kept him out of every competitive game in black and white.

His return gives Luciano Spalletti one more name to consider in a week when the squad has been kept together at the training centre ahead of Sunday’s visit of Napoli.

A frustrating start in Turin

Juventus announced the permanent deal on 1 July, and Football Italia put the fee at €16m plus add ons. The 19 year old has barely been available since.

According to Gianluca Di Marzio, who credits Sky Sport Italia with the news, his troubles began in the very first friendly of pre-season against Basel. He recovered in time to be named in the squad for the second Serie A round against Parma, where he stayed on the bench, but broke down again a few days later and has spent roughly two further months on the sidelines.

Di Marzio describes the forward as fully recovered, and Friday’s session with the rest of the group was his first since that setback.

Where he fits in Spalletti’s attack

Nobody should expect Ekhator to be rushed into the starting eleven after so long without match practice. Football Italia sees him more as an extra option from the bench, giving Spalletti a third centre forward alongside Nick Woltemade and Randal Kolo Muani.

Adventurer to Climb 100 Peaks on 2,000Km Taiwan Traverse

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Tomorrow, November 1, Japanese adventurer Yoki Tanaka begins a four-month, 2,000km trek north to south down the length of Taiwan. Along the way, he will summit all 100 of Taiwan's celebrated Baiyue peaks.

Taiwan has about 268 peaks exceeding 3,000m, but only 100 of them made this special list, which is based not only on height but beauty, difficulty, prominence, and character. Prominent Taiwanese mountaineers compiled the list in the early 1970s, partly inspired by Japan's own list of 100 revered mountains.

Yoki Tanaka arrives in Taiwan earlier this week. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

The 43-year-old Tanaka has specialized in this unusual combination of long-distance trekking and mountaineering. In 2014, he summited Japan's 100 famous mountains while walking 7,800km over 208 days. (Japan, though far bigger than Taiwan, has only 23 mountains over 3,000m.) A year later, he extended that by climbing 200 mountains and walking 8,000km in 222 days. Finally, he increased that again to 300 mountains. This time, it took him three years and about 20,000km of walking.

Tanaka's projected route across Taiwan.

 

Tanaka will begin on Sunday from the northernmost tip of Taiwan, at the Cape Fugui lighthouse, and finish sometime in March at Eluanbi, the country's southernmost point. The first 150km will be along roads before he reaches his first trailhead. He'll spend the next week trekking. His first five peaks will be Xueshan (3,886m), Nanhudashan (3,742m), Hehuanshan (3,417m), Qilaishan (3,560m), and Nenggaoshan 3,262m).

Precise planning

Although he won't use motorized transport for the entire journey, he will have ample opportunity to resupply along the way. His expedition plan includes a charmingly precise breakdown of how he plans to spend his evenings: town stays, 48 days; mountain huts, 38 days; tent camping, 39 days. He has also budgeted 16 rest days and 20 contingency days.

Tanaka trains in Taiwan earlier this year. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

His most difficult section, scheduled for early January, will take him through the remote interior of the Central Mountain Range for over two weeks. During this time, he'll have to carry his own food, search for trails that may no longer exist, and gamble that he will be able to find enough water.

During a December 2025 reconnaissance trip to Xueshan, Taiwan's second-highest mountain, Tanaka discovered how difficult it can be to obtain water. Two water sources marked on his map were completely dry.

On a previous hike. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

By late January, the Japanese trekker should reach the historic Batongguan Trail, eventually leading to 3,952m Yushan, Taiwan's highest mountain. The Batongguan Trail was originally constructed in 1875 under the Qing dynasty to establish a route across the mountainous interior. Decades later, Japanese colonial authorities constructed a separate crossing through the region. Sections of the route survive as remote hiking trails and provide access to some of Taiwan's highest mountains.

Tanaka first visited Taiwan in the spring of 2025 and has made several reconnaissance visits since, including an east-west crossing of the island earlier this year. He had intended to begin his Taiwan 100 Peaks Traverse project this past spring, but local mountaineers advised him to avoid the rainy season and typhoon risks. Their advice prompted him to shift his starting date to November.

Yoki Tanaka on a training hike in Taiwan. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

Interestingly, of Taiwan's 100 Baiyue peaks, only 99 are now recognized as surpassing 3,000m. The exception is Lushan at 2,981m. It was originally measured at 3,043m but subsequent measurements reduced its elevation. Nevertheless, it remains on the list.

Taiwan's representative in Japan told Japanese media that the fastest prior completion of the Baiyue by a foreign climber took three years and eight months, spread over multiple trips.

Once Tanaka begins, his expedition website will provide live tracking of his progress across Taiwan.

Why cheap health insurance plans can backfire for Americans seeking lower healthcare costs

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Healthcare costs are a major concern for Americans, and health insurance premiums are a primary fuel for that anxiety.

Shrinking federal subsidies for Affordable Care Act plans led to large premium increases for millions of Americans in 2025, causing many people to drop coverage. With premiums expected to rise further and Medicaid work requirements set to begin in January 2027, many Americans are left scrambling for affordable options.

We are health economists who study public policies aimed at expanding health insurance coverage. When it comes to our own health insurance, we are fortunate: We are among the roughly 160 million Americans who receive coverage through their employers.

But if you don’t work for a large employer that provides health benefits, finding coverage for your family can be confusing and, increasingly, expensive.

People looking for low-cost alternatives have no doubt encountered several options. They may look appealing and, in some cases, may be appropriate, but they can also carry real risks.

Navigating trade-offs

Historically, people who didn’t have access to employer-sponsored coverage had limited health insurance options. Before the Affordable Care Act went into effect in 2014, insurers could charge you a higher premium based on your health status, exclude coverage for preexisting medical conditions, or deny you coverage altogether.

The ACA eliminated this kind of discrimination, established a minimum standard of 10 essential benefits that health insurance plans had to cover, and required plans to fully cover the cost of preventive care. It also introduced income-based subsidies that could be used to purchase coverage in newly established online marketplaces.

Low-cost plans that aren’t available through ACA marketplaces are essentially vestiges of the pre-ACA health insurance landscape.

Regardless of where you get coverage, choosing health insurance involves trade-offs. The more you pay in premiums, the more comprehensive your coverage will be in terms of how much you must pay when you need care and the breadth of the network of covered providers.

Some trade-offs are clear when you choose a plan, but some lower-cost options may have limitations that are not immediately obvious.

Less costly short-term plans

As their name suggests, short-term, limited-duration plans are designed for people with temporary gaps in coverage, such as those who are between jobs or months away from qualifying for Medicare. These plans, which can be purchased for periods of up to six months, tend to be cheaper than marketplace coverage for two reasons.

First, because they are not subject to ACA rules, they may not provide the full range of essential health benefits. Short-term, limited-duration plans often do not cover maternity care and commonly don’t have an annual limit on out-of-pocket costs. In fact, these plans may cap how much they will pay in a year, leaving policyholders stuck with large, unexpected expenses.

Second, unlike plans offered through the ACA marketplaces, short-term, limited-duration plans are allowed to deny coverage based on your health status. Screening out higher-cost consumers reduces premiums, but if you are not in perfect health, you may not be able to find an insurer that will sell you one of these more limited plans.

Because of a concern that they destabilize the market by siphoning off healthy consumers, short-term, limited-duration plans are banned in five states.

Alternatives that look like insurance

Another seemingly affordable option is healthcare sharing ministries, arrangements through which consumers with common religious beliefs agree to share medical expenses. Their websites resemble those of health insurance companies—for example, they often present different plan options offering different levels of benefits—but they avoid using standard insurance terms like “premium” or “deductible” because these offerings are not insurance.

Because they are not regulated as insurance companies, healthcare sharing ministries are not subject to consumer protections or other state or federal requirements. There is also no guarantee that the ministry will cover your medical expenses when you submit a claim.

Like short-term, limited-duration plans, healthcare sharing ministries may deny coverage based on your health history, exclude coverage for preexisting conditions, and put a limit on the dollar amount they will pay in benefits.

Another lower-cost option available in 14 states is health coverage offered by a farm bureau. These plans are similar to healthcare sharing ministries in that they are limited to a certain group of people—to enroll, you must be a member of the state’s farm bureau, though that does not mean you necessarily have to work in agriculture.

These plans are also not subject to federal or state insurance regulations. This means that, like short-term, limited-duration plans, they can offer lower prices by not offering certain benefits required by the ACA and by screening out high-cost consumers. Unlike short-term, limited-duration plans, there is no limit on how long you can hold farm bureau coverage.

Choosing a health insurance plan often requires fighting through a thicket of jargon.

What should you do?

Although the cost of marketplace coverage has increased dramatically since 2025, for many consumers who need coverage for more than six months, it remains the best option.

If you are single with an annual income below $64,000, a couple with an annual income below $87,000, or a family of four with an annual income less than $132,000, you should still be eligible for a premium subsidy. And you will know that the plan you are purchasing is real insurance that covers a standard set of benefits and is subject to robust consumer protections.

If you are considering an alternative to marketplace coverage, there are a few things you should do to protect yourself:

Make sure you ask for a summary of benefits and coverage. This is a plain-language summary of a health plan’s benefits and coverage with examples of how much the plan would cover for two specific conditions—diabetes and childbirth. Insurers and employers who offer coverage are required to provide this document to anyone applying for coverage. If they cannot provide one, what they are selling may not actually be insurance.

If they do provide a summary of benefits and coverage, read it carefully. As we noted earlier, one reason some options might be less expensive than a marketplace plan is that they provide much less coverage. Don’t assume that they cover all the benefits you would expect from a health insurance plan, or that they will cover the full cost of the care you receive.

Finally, never enter your phone number into a “do you need insurance” website. This area is rife with fraud, including multiple cases in which telemarketers misled consumers into paying hundreds of dollars per month to purchase sham health insurance.

Health insurance is expensive because healthcare is expensive—which means that consumers have to make difficult trade-offs. There is no easy answer, but beware of options that look too good to be true. They very well might be.
