New Research Strengthens the Case for Emotional Dysregulation in ADHD Criteria

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

When the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) was updated in 2022, officials believed that research was lacking to expand ADHD’s definition to include the common and extremely impairing symptom of emotional dysregulation. Consequently, the overwhelming and uncontrollable emotions reported by many patients remain largely unrecognized as ADHD manifestations.

Now research appears to be catching up to patient reports.

A new landmark study has revealed three ADHD subtypes that reflect neurobiological differences: predominantly inattentive; predominantly hyperactive/impulsive; and the new severe-combined, which is marked by emotional dysregulation.1

Using neuroimaging and computational modeling to map the pathways of ADHD, researchers found that the severe-combined type had 45 areas of brain abnormality tied to emotion and control compared with 26 each for the two other types. The study, which analyzed 1,154 brain scans of children and adolescents with ADHD, was published recently in JAMA Psychiatry.

New Presentation, New Treatment?

The findings are “an example of recent insights emerging from neuroimaging that may impact clinical care, which has been an elusive goal,” says F. Xavier Castellanos, M.D., professor emeritus of child and adolescent psychiatry at New York University’s Grossman School of Medicine. He was part of the working group that contributed to the DSM-5.

“Still, it’s too early to assume that their intriguing results are real in the sense of replicating repeatedly in independent datasets, particularly by investigators who are not allied or collaborating with the original research team,” says Castellanos. He, like the study authors, calls for prospective studies to validate their biotypes and determine whether they may influence treatment.

Findings that reveal a new severe-combined subtype may come as no surprise to the thousands of parents of children with ADHD, and adults with the condition, who have named emotional dysregulation as the most impairing feature of ADHD in ADDitude surveys.

But, as David Anderson, Ph.D., senior psychologist at the ADHD and Behavior Disorders Center at the Child Mind Institute, points out, parents’ anecdotal feedback precedes scientific validation.

“Finding evidence of a subtype in the brain leaves open questions about the balance of nature versus nurture. Is there a biological basis for subtypes and is it reliable across the span of development?” Anderson asks. “Emotion dysregulation can be biological for kids who are more prone to emotional behavior from an early age, while some are frustrated with the world due to negative feedback they’ve gotten since kindergarten, so it’s environmental. There are multiple pathways to come to this.”

If the study’s findings are replicated, Castellanos says, it would become a new presentation in a future version of the DSM. “I served on the DSM-5 committee, and we were explicitly reminded that the DSM follows the field; it does not lead it. It’s only when the scientific evidence is strongly supportive that DSM changes are allowed. Otherwise, it’s too disruptive.”

Stephen V. Faraone, Ph.D., distinguished professor at SUNY Upstate Medical University, said he believes that emotional dysregulation was not included in the DSM-5 because it might have made differential diagnosis with anxiety and mood disorders more difficult, especially in primary care. He also said he was skeptical that ADHD can be divided into biological subtypes, adding that underlying ADHD brain mechanisms exist along a spectrum and do not fall neatly into separate categories.

The study’s researchers said the findings may eventually create a path toward developing personalized therapeutic strategies.

ADHD and the Social Processing Gap

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

“Knowing social expectations versus implementing them in real time is a very big challenge for people with ADHD.” — Sharon Saline, Psy.D.

This tension is at the heart of many slip-ups — from oversharing and interrupting to difficulties reading the room — that stump adults with ADHD across various scenarios. Dr. Saline, a clinical psychologist who specializes in ADHD, described this as the “social processing gap” during her October ADDitude webinar, “Reading Body Language & Social Cues: A Primer for People with ADHD.”

Here, learn more about the gap and how to fill it, along with quick tips for navigating sticky social situations with ease and confidence.

The Social Processing Gap

ADHD symptoms and traits — like working memory challenges, distractibility, impulsivity, hyperfocus and more — directly impact communication and social skills. But there’s another factor that tends to influence social interactions: shame.

A history of negative and uncomfortable social interactions means that many adults with ADHD approach social situations assuming they’ll misread a facial expression, take up too much of the conversation, or commit a faux pas. As a result, many are unable to fully participate in conversations without extreme vigilance and/or becoming overwhelmed, anxious, self-conscious, or exhausted.

“ADHD affects our ability to interpret social information when it’s occurring in real time, because of anxiety and rejection sensitivity that complicate those interpretations and how people respond,” Saline said.

The Social Interpretation Reset

Navigating misunderstandings in relationships, friendships, family, and work comes down to three words: Notice. Check. Clarify.

Noticing, or building social awareness, doesn’t mean scanning for signs of disapproval. It is being attuned to other people and adapting to connection, without taking things too personally or making assumptions. “The challenge is to notice these cues without automatically assuming that they’re bored, annoyed, or rejecting us,” Saline said.

That’s where clarification comes in. Uncertainty is a potent trigger for rejection sensitivity, so Saline encourages respectful questioning to address assumptions. For example, if your partner says they’re not upset but their tone sounds irritated, ask, “I’m hearing something different in your tone. Is there something you’d like to talk about?”

Strategies for Healthier Social Interactions

Try these tips for navigating common ADHD social challenges:

  • To listen without losing the thread, focus primarily on meaning, not on every detail of the person’s story.
  • Can you talk all night about a topic? Start by giving people permission to interrupt you if you get carried away. Say, “Please reel me in!”
  • Are they mad at you or is it in your head? Ask — and if they say no, take them at their word until they tell you otherwise.
  • Made a slip-up? Repair without shame. Say, “I’m sorry, I just interrupted you. I got so excited. I want to hear what you have to say.”

Pliers Have Built-In Wire Cutters for Small Repairs

Some pliers have built-in wire cutters near the hinge, just behind the gripping jaws. These can trim soft wire without needing a separate pair of cutters. Not every pair has this feature, though. It’s also worth checking whether the tool has wire strippers, which remove insulation without cutting through the wire itself.

Wire Strippers Can Also Cut Screws to Length

Some wire strippers have small, threaded holes near the handles that allow users to shorten machine screws. The screw goes into the appropriately sized hole, and squeezing the handles cuts it to length. This feature can come in handy when installing electrical fixtures or other hardware that requires shorter screws.

Manual Can Openers May Have Built-In Bottle Openers

Some manual can openers have a bottle opener built into the handle or near the cutting mechanism. Depending on the design, it may look like a small notch or hook in the metal. Other models have magnets that help lift cut lids from cans without touching the sharp edges.

Nail Clippers Often Have Built-In Nail Files

Many nail clippers have a small metal file that folds out from beneath the lever or sits along the body. It helps smooth rough edges after trimming. Some models also have a pointed tip for cleaning beneath nails, although the design and placement of these extra features vary.

AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities is now generally available

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Today, we’re announcing the general availability of AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities. AWS Continuum is a security service that finds and validates code vulnerabilities, then delivers fixes as patches you can automate or as pull requests in your repositories. AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities confirms which findings are exploitable in your environment, ranks the resulting issues by business impact, and proposes a fix for each one. Every remediation starts in a mode where Continuum proposes the fix and a person approves it before it ships. In the future, you will be able to decide which actions Continuum can take on its own, within the guardrails you define.

Using frontier artificial intelligence (AI) models, threat actors can turn a vulnerability disclosure into working exploit code in minutes, and the same tools that speed up development also increase the volume of findings your teams must process. If your organization runs a large application estate, your scanners likely produce thousands of findings a day, and many of them need no action. A finding can be unreachable, unexploitable, or already mitigated by a runtime control. Generic severity scores don’t tell you which resources a threat actor can actually reach, so a critical score on a dev-only utility looks the same as a medium score on a service one step away from a payments database. Previously, confirming whether a finding was exploitable took hours of manual investigation, and shipping a fix with confidence took days of coordination across security, infrastructure, and application teams, because the blast radius of a change is hard to assess without business context. To close that gap, Continuum proves which findings are exploitable and bounds the risk of each fix it proposes.

Continuum builds a context graph from two primary sources: your code repositories and your AWS environment. For AWS, you set up a collector in the account and Region where your code runs. The collector gathers resource details such as AWS Config resources and network configuration so Continuum can judge whether a vulnerability is reachable over the network and what a threat actor could reach from it. From this graph, Continuum learns which systems are business-critical, who owns them, how services depend on one another, and which data each one touches. It uses that picture to identify your highest-value systems, such as customer data stores, payment pipelines, and authentication services, and then ranks each resulting issue by how close its attack path runs to those systems.

Continuum analyzes each finding against your code and its environment context and assigns an evidence level, such as a verdict proven by static analysis of the vulnerable code path. For findings it can reproduce, Continuum runs the exploit in an isolated sandbox and attaches proof-of-concept code with the reproduction steps. It produces the attack path, the supporting source analysis, and a blast radius assessment instead of a risk score alone. It also evaluates whether your existing defenses would detect and stop the validated path.

For each confirmed issue, Continuum generates a fix (a code patch, a dependency upgrade, or a configuration change) and opens it as a pull request in your connected repository, ready to review. Every fix requires human approval: you review and merge the pull request, so the final decision stays with your team. In the future, you will be able to build confidence in specific remediation classes and let Continuum apply those fixes automatically once its confidence gates pass, such as test coverage, scope bounds, and historical success rate.

AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities in action
To show how this works, I open AWS Continuum in the console and go to Code vulnerabilities. Before I can review issues, I set up an environment. I connect a code source, either a GitHub integration or an Amazon S3 bucket, and then create an environment that scans it on a schedule I choose. An Amazon S3 source has a couple of prerequisites: the bucket sits in the same account and AWS Region where I turn on AWS Continuum, and I grant access through an AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM) role that AWS Continuum assumes. I can optionally add AWS infrastructure context by setting up an AWS Continuum collector in the account and Region where my code runs and authorizing my environment to use it. For the full requirements and steps, see the AWS Continuum User Guide.

The Overview page brings these pieces together in one place, showing my Code integrations, the Environments built on them, and the status of any AWS collector that supplies AWS resource context.

I open one of my environments. The Issues tab lists validated, prioritized issues rather than raw scanner output, each with a severity, a status, and the time it was created. I choose one of the issues.

The Overview rates it High severity, with the status Completed and the remediation already Delivered to GitHub. The Risk assessment explains why. Continuum rates its Exploitability as Exploitable, with an evidence level of Static proven and a validation run status of Completed. Likelihood is High, since the service is reachable from an attacker, requires no authentication, and needs no user interaction. Impact is Moderate, which means an attacker gains the ability to modify business data, with a blast radius reaching other users in the same tenant. A Proof section includes the source analysis behind the verdict.

The Remediation section shows a fix already delivered as a pull request in GitHub, changing four files, with 50 lines added and 24 removed across the cart service. Continuum opens the fix as a pull request titled Continuum fix, and I choose View pull request to open it in GitHub. The console is explicit that a generated patch does not mean the issue is fixed. I still review and merge it, which keeps the final decision with my team. A Contributing findings section lists the specific scanner finding this issue was built from.

The pull request description carries the same risk assessment, remediation summary, and proof from the console, so whoever reviews it in GitHub sees the evidence without leaving their workflow.

The Findings tab lists the raw scanner findings across the environment, the evidence that issues are built from.

The Scans tab shows the history of each scan with its status, origin, and request and completion times, and I use Configure scan frequency to change the cadence or Queue scan to run one on demand. The Configuration tab is where I manage the environment: the code and context sources it reads from, its code integrations, environment settings such as whether Continuum creates remediation patches, and the AWS collectors it uses for AWS context.

In a single workflow, I work through the validated issues in priority order with each scan, reviewing the evidence and scope behind each one before I approve and merge the fixes I trust. Every fix waits for my approval, so the backlog keeps shrinking while my team keeps control of what ships.
