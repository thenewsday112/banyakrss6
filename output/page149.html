Is sharenting safe? 7 ways to mindfully post about your kids online

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

“Sharenting” is more common than ever. Discover why parents share posts about their kids online, the risks involved, and 7 tips for safer, more mindful posting.

Your kid loses their first tooth or says something hilarious at dinner, and your thumb is already halfway to the share button. For a lot of parents and caregivers, posting these moments feels like second nature.

Believe it or not, there's actually a name for this habit: sharenting. And while it’s often a simple way to stay connected, it also puts your child’s story online before they can choose to share it. If you’ve started to wonder about the risks, it’s a good time to take stock of your posting habits.

Here's a look at why parents share and what’s worth knowing about the risks, plus seven small shifts to help you post with confidence.

 

What is sharenting?

Sharenting is when parents or caregivers post about their kids on social media. It could be a single birthday photo, or years of posts documenting a whole childhood. Think videos, funny quotes, school updates, potty-training wins, and even ultrasound pictures from before the baby arrives.  

It's also widespread. Research reviews estimate that about three in four parents share photos, stories, or videos of their kids online, and most use their children's real names.

Parents have always shared news about their kids through holiday letters, birth announcements, and photos mailed to relatives. Social media just made the audience much bigger, the posts more public, and the record harder to remove.

Why do parents share their kids’ lives online?

Most sharenting comes from a good place. You're proud of your kid and you want the people in your life to know. Studies on parents who post about their kids point to the same reasons:

  • Staying close: Photos and videos let faraway family and friends watch your child grow. 

  • Celebrating and remembering: Posts mark your child’s milestones and give you something to scroll back through later.

  • Finding community: Other parents and caregivers can offer advice and a “me too” at 2am, when parenting feels isolating.

  • Feeling seen: A kind comment on a rough day reminds you that you’re doing okay.

What are the risks of sharenting on social media?

Sharing your kids online isn’t necessarily harmful. Still, a few risks are worth knowing about:

A lasting digital footprint. Screenshots, reshares, and archives mean a photo can outlast the original post. Many kids have years of photos online before they're old enough to understand who can see them. 

Privacy and consent. Young kids can't weigh in on what you post about them. Their public image takes shape long before they can agree to it, and that can be hard to undo later.

Personal information. Someone could piece together a full name, birth date, hometown, and school from a handful of posts. Over time, details like these may be misused for identity theft.

Location and background details. Location tags and details like a house number, school logo, or regular park in the background show where your child spends their time. On a public account, that can make their routine visible to strangers.

Facial recognition and AI. Photos on public accounts may be saved, scanned, or collected by AI tools and facial recognition software. Some of those tools turn ordinary photos into fake images.

How to practice mindful sharenting: 7 tips to balance safety and connection

You don't have to log off to keep your kid safe. A few small shifts protect their privacy and still let you share the moments you love. Start with one or two of these, whatever feels most doable, then add more as they become habit.

1. Take a mindful pause before you post

Before you hit share, take a breath and think about whether you want to post this moment. If you do, scan the photo and caption for anything that gives away too much, like a full name or a street sign.

For some structure, a short breathing exercise or meditation can help.

2. Verify your privacy settings 

A couple of settings are worth checking before your next post. If your account is public, anyone can find and save your posts. Photos taken on your phone may also include the location where you took them.

Try: 

  • Switch to a private account and remove followers you don’t know.

  • Review your tagging settings.

  • Turn off geotagging in your camera and app settings.

Platforms can change their defaults without warning, so it’s worth redoing this check every couple of months.

 

3. Leave out identifying details

You can share the moment without giving away your child’s identity. Skip birth dates, school names, and daily routines. Use a nickname or first initial instead of your child’s full name.

Posting a day or two later makes it harder for anyone to know where your child is in real time.

4. Ask your child for their opinion

Once your child is old enough to weigh in, show them the post and ask if they're okay with you sharing it. This shows them that their opinion matters. If your child is too young to decide, the next tip can help you decide for them.

5. Picture your child as an adult

Imagine your child at 25, seeing what you’re about to post. Does it give away something private about them, such as a health issue or a hard time at school? What seems relatable now may feel too personal to them later.

If you're unsure, keep the more personal moments in a private family group or an offline album.

6. Review your past posts

Your past posts felt right at the time, and it’s okay if some show more than you’d share today. Scroll back through your posts for details like full names, school names, or locations.

Delete the post, archive it, or move it to a private album if it feels too revealing. 

7. Talk with family about boundaries

You're not the only one with a camera. Grandparents, co-parents, babysitters, and friends may post photos of your child too, usually out of love and pride. A conversation up front about family boundaries keeps everyone on the same page.

You might ask family to:

  • leave full names and birth dates out of captions

  • skip location tags and recognizable landmarks in the background

  • use private groups or shared albums instead of public posts

  • check with you before posting anything

Sharenting FAQs

What is the meaning of sharenting?

Sharenting means posting about your child on social media, and the word blends "sharing" and "parenting." It covers anything from baby photos to regular updates as they grow up.

What are some common sharenting examples?

Common sharenting examples include ultrasound pictures, birthday posts, funny quotes, and health or milestone updates. Even a photo that seems harmless can reveal more than you intended, like a club sign or a team jersey in the background.

Why do parents share posts about their kids online so often?

Many parents share to stay connected and celebrate their kids. Sharenting keeps faraway family members in the loop and connects you with other parents who get it. During hard stretches, it can help you feel less alone.

What are the biggest risks of sharenting on social media?

The main risks of sharenting include a lasting digital footprint and exposed personal details. Photos can be saved, reshared, or collected by AI tools, which is why child safety groups encourage private accounts. 

That said, many posts don’t lead to harm, and small habits like turning off location tags help lower the risks further.

How does sharenting affect children’s privacy and safety?

Sharenting shapes your child’s public image before they’re old enough to agree to it. Names, schools, and tagged locations can also reveal their routines to strangers, and a handful of details may be used for identity theft. 

If you keep personal moments offline now, your child has more room to shape their own image as they grow.

At what age should kids have a say in social media posts?

There's no set age for when kids should have a say in what you post. Young kids may not fully understand what sharing means, so the decision starts with you. You can start asking as soon as they’re able to weigh in, and give their yes or no more weight as they grow. 

How can I practice responsible sharenting on social media?

You can practice responsible sharenting with a mindful pause before you post. From there, check your privacy settings, and leave out personal details including names, schools, and locations. Ask for your child’s input once they’re old enough, and let family members know your boundaries.

Justices to decide whether failure to plead an affirmative defense prevents moving for summary judgment on that same defense

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Younge v. Fulton Judicial Circuit District Attorney’s Office, Georgia, to be argued on Nov. 2, involves an issue that will quicken the pulse of civil procedure aficionados. No less than four different rules of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure are in play: affirmative defenses under Rule 8, amending a pleading under Rule 15, pretrial management under Rule 16, and summary judgment under Rule 56.

The case raising this cornucopia of pretrial procedural rules comes out of a relatively typical civil rights suit in which the plaintiff asserts that she was improperly dismissed from her job and is now suing for damages. Jasmine Younge held a high-level position in the Fulton County District Attorney’s Office for about two months until she was fired in July 2019 by District Attorney Paul Howard. Younge says she was dismissed from her job about two weeks after telling Howard that she was pregnant. Later that year, Younge sued the Fulton County District Attorney’s Office for a number of civil rights violations, including the claim that termination because of her pregnancy violated Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

The DA’s office answered the complaint, and pretrial proceedings ensued. Save for a few disputed matters, discovery closed in January 2022. About two months later, at a pretrial hearing on a discovery issue, the DA’s office informed the court and Younge that it intended to move for summary judgment, including on the basis of the personal-staff exemption defense, which it had not asserted in its answer. (Under Title VII, certain officials are exempted from the statutory definition of employee, including (1) members of an elected official’s “personal staff” and (2) “policymaking appointees.”) The DA’s office explained that it did not learn of the defense until its lawyer began drafting the summary judgment motion.

Soon after the March 2022 hearing, the DA’s office moved for summary judgment, citing its defenses based upon the statutory exemptions from Title VII in support of the motion. The DA never sought to amend its answer to add these defenses.

Younge objected to the motion, arguing as a procedural matter that it was too late for the DA’s office to assert new affirmative defenses. Her waiver argument regarding the personal-staff exemption was rejected several times in the lower courts, first by the federal magistrate judge who permitted the personal-staff defense to be asserted and recommended summary judgment in favor of the DA’s office predicated on that defense. The district court then agreed with this recommendation and entered judgment in favor of the DA. Next, the case was appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the 11th Circuit.

The 11th Circuit (as had the lower courts) applied its reasoning in the 1988 case of Hassan v. U.S. Postal Service, in which it had employed a prejudice analysis to determine whether to allow the defendant to move for summary judgment based on an unpled affirmative defense. Here, the district court had determined that there was no prejudice to allowing the personal-staff defense. Significantly, in addition to finding that the DA’s office should prevail on the defense, the lower courts noted that Younge could not identify any additional discovery that she would have pursued had the defense been asserted earlier.

As the 11th Circuit summarized, “since Hassan, [i]t had repeatedly reaffirmed that a district court may receive evidence of an unpleaded affirmative defense if the plaintiff was not ‘prejudiced’ by the defendant’s failure to plead the defense in its answer.” Furthermore, the circuit court explained that “the record contains copious discovery evidence concerning each of the six factors” relevant to “the personal staff exemption.” Accordingly, the 11th Circuit affirmed the district court, finding that “Younge had not suffered prejudice from the DA’s Office’s late assertion of the personal staff exemption” and that summary judgment had properly been entered in favor of the DA.

The justices agreed to hear the case in March to determine whether an affirmative defense may be asserted as a basis for summary judgment if not presented earlier in a pleading.

The parties’ positions

In seeking reversal of the judgment below, Younge argues that the court should take a textualist approach to reading the applicable rules. “Rule 8(c)(1) emphasizes” without exception “that a defendant must plead ‘any’ affirmative defenses,” she states. Since the DA did not do so, it should not have been allowed to seek summary judgment on the basis of such an unpled defense.

Younge further argues that allowing a summary judgment motion based upon an unpled affirmative defense permits a defendant to circumvent the requirements of Rule 15(a)(2) for amending a pleading and Rule 16(b)(4) for modifying a scheduling order. The former requires a court to analyze a number of factors, including “undue delay” on the part of the party seeking to amend, and is stricter than a mere prejudice standard. The latter requires “good cause,” considers due diligence, and, again, is more demanding than just showing prejudice. Younge would then have the court set aside the 11th Circuit’s prejudice standard and apply the “well-established standards and procedures of Rules 15(a)(2) and 16(b)(4).”

The DA, on the other hand, stands by the prejudice standard set out in Hassan and applied by the 11th Circuit. It asserts that this standard is correct given the interrelated purposes of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure: to resolve cases on the merits and to avoid their resolution on the basis of technical or formalistic application of the rules themselves.

According to the DA, the text, structure, and historical development of the FRCP support this purposive reading of the rules. Regarding the text, the DA invokes Rule 61, the harmless error rule, which provides that “[a]t every stage of the proceeding, the court must disregard all errors and defects that do not affect any party’s substantial rights.” “Structurally,” the DA expresses that “the Rules impose limits on amending pleadings to import new factual issues … but not on raising legal arguments like the one the DA’s Office raised here.” And as for the history, the DA points out that “Rule 12 used to provide that a defendant waives all unpleaded defenses but no longer does.”

Overall, the purposive approach to interpreting the FRCP may prove especially appealing to the court: As the DA argues, the FRCP drew on and were influenced by the rules in the courts of equity, which aimed to simplify pleading rules and adopted a flexible approach to promote the resolution of cases on the merits. In pretrial matters such as pleadings and discovery, context – the underlying facts, the claims and defenses asserted, and the parties’ conduct, among other things – matters. Given this, although the court may not write broadly, I think it’s unlikely to find that the 11th Circuit’s prejudice standard was out of bounds.

Keep in mind that weather events can greatly influence the intensity of action you see. Warm weather can throw a wet blanket on things for sure, but a few chilly mornings, or better yet, a full blown cold front can trigger a seeming explosion in daylight buck activity.

Mature bucks that have largely sat on the sidelines watching their younger counterparts awkwardly make passes at the local does often decide to enter the game in this late October time frame. What’s more, bucks are still largely sticking to routines in their core areas right now, allowing you to set up on the patterns you’ve been observing all fall. And the cherry on top ? Our pick falls on Saturday, a day most hunters don’t have to answer to the bossman and can carefully plan a precise hunt, both morning and evening, that could pay off big. October 31 Morning Hunt Plan: Stake Out a Scrape Line

Mississippi State University collared-buck research reveals buck activity trends later into daylight in the morning hours, allowing savvy hunters opportunity that evenings do not offer. Once in security cover and bedding areas, bucks do not immediately bed down, especially now. Falling temperatures and rising testosterone extends this activity window as bucks continue their nighttime pre-rut activities of scrape-making, rubbing, and checking local does well into daylight.

Your play on this late October morning is to use your scouting intel to set up on a hot scrape or scrape line in transition cover near a known bedding area. And by “hot” I mean a location where a buck has proven he is willing to visit scrapes repeatedly at or near daylight. If you do not have this information, there’s still time before the weekend for some midday speed-scouting, as long as you’re confident that you can scout specific transition areas without alerting bedding bucks. An alternate setup in hilly terrain that’s a favorite of mine is a ridge-top lookout. This time of year is when hunters occasionally get those early afternoon trail camera photos which leave them sputtering, “If I’d only been there 15 minutes earlier.”

If you want to change it up for your evening sit, try slipping into an evening food source you’ve been saving for such a time as this. Difficult to hunt in the morning due to the potential for spooking deer, these doe hangouts now become more attractive to bucks than that house on the block giving out full-sized candy bars is to trick-or-treaters. New scrapes have probably shown up along these plot edges, and if a target buck has only been hitting them in night hours, this could be the day his script flips. Take extra time to make a slow, silent approach, and if you think there’ll be deer lingering after dark, arrange for someone in a vehicle to pick you up and clear the field before you leave. This will keep the plot fresh, allow you to check or put up a camera on any new active scrapes for monitoring, and set you up nicely for our next day of the rut just days away.

Bonus Tip: Read a Buck Like a Book

So how aggressively should you be using decoys, rattling, or calling during your pre-rut hunts? It’s a good question, and from my experience it depends on deer in your area, the hunting pressure there, and specific buck personalities. The goal is to incite a reaction, not intimidate. In areas with less pressure and a more mature age class of deer, more aggressive approaches can be used successfully. By far the best way to fire up a buck is to play off his personality. If he sees a buck pushing others around, he knows he can likely antagonize him by mirroring this with aggressive calling, rattling, or even a buck decoy. You can do the same. Rely on what trail cameras tell you, historic data, and visual sightings in the field to tailor your exact tactics to lure in a true monster—the ultimate Halloween treat.

Two cheers for “insecure originalism”

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In his new book, Justice Samuel Alito takes aim at what he calls judicial “insecurity,” or a judge’s self-conscious worry that he might be advancing his own policy preferences under the guise of impartial adjudication. “Twenty-first century originalists,” he argues, “should shed this insecure mindset.”

Alito’s suggestion is at odds with decades of conservative legal thought, including remarks by Justice Antonin Scalia. More fundamentally, what Alito labels insecurity is better understood as a form of humility and self-awareness. And that humility is laudable for all judges, not just originalists. There is much more to fear from the self-assured or overconfident judge than the insecure one.

Was Scalia “insecure”?

In some of the book’s most discussed passages (and in an op-ed in The Wall Street Journal), Alito impugns “insecure originalism,” in part because “insecurity” may lead originalists “to worry that they are subconsciously engaging in ‘results-oriented’ decision-making and ‘judicial activism’ in the service of their own policy preferences.” Alito urges originalists to shrug off this worry: “Originalist judges should . . . be on guard against subconsciously setting a higher bar for legal arguments that yield outcomes they find desirable as a policy matter.”

The insecurity that Alito bemoans has a rich history that his discussion omits. In recent decades, the jurist most associated with this kind of thinking was longtime conservative hero Scalia. In his oft-repeated view, “The judge who always likes the results he reaches is a bad judge.” This maxim was admonitory. It not so subtly suggested that other judges, perhaps of the non-originalist variety, always liked where their theorizing led them. But not the principled originalist, whose impartial theory necessarily brought about disappointment – the price of integrity.

Scalia often fleshed out this line of thinking by pointing to specific issues that originalism resolved in ways he claimed to find regrettable. The First Amendment, in Scalia’s view, encompassed a right to burn the American flag, something that Scalia personally found distasteful. And the confrontation clause effectively demanded that many serious offenders go unpunished. Scalia’s critics sometimes chafed when he drew attention to these decisions, which seemed to give him permission to issue other rulings that did align with his own policy preferences.

Scalia’s disciples have long echoed his view of the “bad judge.” In published writings, including a judicial opinion, Justice Neil Gorsuch has favorably adapted Scalia’s “bad judge” admonition. And Justice Amy Coney Barrett made Scalia’s aphorism a prominent part of her recent memoir and related book tour. Barrett has also offered her own variations on this theme, remarking for instance: “You should sometimes be reaching results that you really dislike.” In a similar spirit, a majority opinion by Justice Brett Kavanaugh defended its own impartiality by adducing examples of standing decisions that cut in different ideological directions.

Insecurity, or anti-partisanship?

What Alito labels “insecurity” is really a judicial virtue and better characterized as a form of self-consciousness or rigor. When a judge realizes that he is consistently ruling for or against a particular kind of party, he has good reason to reflect on the possibility that he is the victim of his own biases. That point has special salience in today’s United States, which is marked by partisan polarization.

When a judge confronts a case with partisan valence, he would do well to pause over his own potential bias. In the context of contemporary federal judging, such a bias often correlates with the judge’s appointing president. A judge appointed by a Republican or Democratic president is likely to have personal views aligned with Republicans or Democrats, respectively. And the judge is likely to have a social set that is ideologically skewed. As Alito noted in an interview, such a skew may not be the judge’s doing, so much as the result of ideological backlash against the judge’s rulings. But even if unfair or unfortunate, the backlash’s effects are real.

In this context, a judge will be tempted to please his own ideological tribe while being insensitive to its rival. A Democratic-appointed judge may play to Bluesky, writing zingers in trendy language that please imagined audiences. Or a Republican-appointed judge may similarly look forward to standing ovations at Federalist Society galas. These incentives and trains of thought are not so hypothetical. And once this kind of behavior takes hold, the judges in question become more like partisan actors than principled defenders of either legal rules or the judiciary.

Against that backdrop, an anti-partisanship principle offers a means of reaching better, more accurate results – not a means of sacrificing accuracy for illicit ends. A judge who sensibly factors in his own partisan affiliation, background, or associations is more likely to identify and apply the law by his own lights. Or so one might hope. Whether anti-partisanship can work largely depends on how it is implemented.

Implementing anti-partisanship

There are many ways of implementing anti-partisanship, and different approaches may befit different scenarios or appeal to different jurists. Surely no judge should factor in party identity in a way that squarely contradicts the law’s dictates. And because many judges have sincere legal views with an ideological valence, complete anti-partisanship is both infeasible and undesirable. Yet judging often involves subtle choices. And those subtleties create room for the anti-partisanship principle to have meaningful effect.

At the Supreme Court, certiorari, that is, deciding what case to hear, affords perhaps the most immediate opportunity for discretionary judgments. Is a particular case or issue ripe for prompt consideration, or sufficiently worthy of the court’s attention? These questions yield judgment calls, and a judge may reasonably consider whether he is consistently making these decisions in favor of one party or ideology. Cert might be warranted only when a relatively objective test is satisfied, such as a deep circuit split. Or the justice might have a practice of avoiding expedited review in such cases.

Moreover, all appellate courts confront myriad opportunities to craft the scope and depth of their holdings. A judge who supports one broad holding after another in favor of his appointing president or party is probably ruling irresponsibly. That judge is not merely transgressing principles of minimalism (deciding only what needs to be decided) or gradualism (deciding incrementally) but doing so in an especially objectionable way by ruling grandly with partisan effect. By contrast, a humbler judge will be sure to wait beyond the first or second chance to resolve a major issue when that issue has a partisan valence.

Judges of all kinds also face an array of different grounds on which to rule. An anti-partisan judge might look with care at jurisdictional requirements or opportunities for abstention, adapting Alexander Bickel’s “passive virtues” (that is, pursuing an impassive strategy, rather than ruling either swiftly or grandly). Or he might shelve a holding in the alternative where a single, narrower holding would suffice.

Precedent affords additional opportunities for anti-partisanship. When a party seeks to overrule case law, a judge might ask whether that request has a partisan valence – and then look to see whether the request to overrule has broad, cross-ideological support.

Finally, judges may express themselves differently depending on the presence of partisan tensions. Barrett made this point in Trump v. Anderson, which (as to result) unanimously held that Colorado could not exclude then-candidate Trump from the presidential ballot based on section 3 of the 14th Amendment. She wrote separately to note that the court “has settled a politically charged issue in the volatile season of a Presidential election” and that, “[p]articularly in this circumstance, writings on the Court should turn the national temperature down, not up.” Thus, Anderson was “not the time to amplify disagreement with stridency.” In other contexts, judges may follow this general advice by muting their rhetoric or even suppressing dissents.

Too anti-partisan?

In our current polarized environment, it may be hard to imagine that a judge would be too anti-partisan. Yet Alito quite properly raises just that concern.

As Alito insightfully puts it: “insecure originalists may actually welcome occasions when their originalist analysis leads to results they dislike. When that occurs, they experience a gratifying frisson, a brief catharsis in which their anxiety about having unconsciously succumbed to the temptation of results-oriented decision-making is released.” The image here is of a judge who becomes so excited about proving his own impartiality that he becomes utterly partial.

Once again, Alito’s point is hardly limited to originalists, and other commentators have raised similar concerns about judging in general. For example, Judge Richard Posner once noted that “[i]t is a source of satisfaction to a judge to vote for the litigant who irritates him . . . for it is by doing such things that you know that you are playing the judge role.”

This kind of concern shows that the anti-partisanship principle must itself be deployed reflectively and cautiously. There is no one simple trick for impartial or accurate judging, and any maxim of juridical wisdom can be twisted or taken too much to heart.

Alito’s worry also indicates that the anti-partisanship principle has only contingent appeal. In a less polarized polity, partisanship might play a smaller or even a trivial role in judicial decision-making. In that environment, the anti-partisanship principle would not help – or might even worsen – judicial bias. But that is not our world today.

Alito elaborates his point in a way that further underscores the appeal of anti-partisanship. To wit, he notes that “an originalist judge should not cavalierly or happily embrace results that defy common sense.” Attention to common sense is indeed justifiable, but that is because (or if) it is truly “common.” Consensus views may well provide a helpful indication that a particular ruling is senseless, catastrophic, or absurd.

But “common sense” is not at all the same as Alito’s other and more salient subject – namely, “outcomes [judges] find desirable as a policy matter.” In placing these two ideas in successive paragraphs and casting them as comparable, Alito inadvertently reveals how easy it is for a judge to draw an equivalence between, or fail to differentiate, his own highly contestable views and genuine consensus.

Anti-partisanship may not be for everyone. The legal system may even benefit from having some judges in each court who align with one or the other of the nation’s two political parties. That arrangement would help partisans identify with the judiciary and might foster a kind of adversarial system within the third branch. But that picture seems appealing, if at all, only if the partisan judges remain the exception.

“The Spirit of Liberty”

In a later chapter, Alito praises Judge Learned Hand’s famous speech on “the Spirit of Liberty.” Alito notes for instance: “I am convinced that the spirit of the Constitution—of limited but effective government, individual rights, and equal justice under law—is alive and well in the hearts of the American people.”

Alas, Alito’s discussion does not quote or acknowledge what Hand believed the spirit of liberty to be. In the speech’s most memorable and oft-repeated line, Hand posited: “The spirit of liberty is the spirit which is not too sure that it is right.”

One might say that the spirit of liberty is a bit insecure. The spirit of the Constitution is, too.

The promise of lower operating costs and cleaner flight has pushed electric aircraft from a fringe experiment to one of the aviation industry’s most closely watched frontiers. After years of renderings, prototypes, and overly optimistic timetables, companies are now flying full-size aircraft, submitting hardware to regulators for certification, building factories, and testing routes with prospective customers.

Among the top three U.S. developers of electric planes, Archer Aviation and Joby Aviation are concentrating on piloted vertical-takeoff-and-landing air taxis, otherwise known as eVTOLs. Beta Technologies is taking a broader—and in some ways more innovative—path into the market. Rather than betting first on air taxis, Beta is looking to enter electric aviation through short-haul freight planes that use conventional runways, fed by an open charging network that’s designed to make electric aircraft—both Beta’s and its competitors’—part of a working transportation system.

To that end, the company is developing two variants of its Alia airframe: the runway-based CX300 I’m flying in and the A250, which adds four lift propellers for vertical takeoff and landing. Beta says its test pilots have flown the Alia family more than 300,000 kilometers, including doing multiple U.S. crossings and overseas demonstrations. The company claims an order and option-to-buy backlog of 991 aircraft.

“We’re taking a pragmatic stepwise approach for both certification and market entry,” says Beta’s CEO and founder Kyle Clark. “We’re ready to go today with medical and cargo aircraft that fly from airport to airport.” Next up, he says, will be passenger aircraft with conventional takeoff and landing, followed by cargo eVTOLs, and finally passenger eVTOLs.

Reducing the Risk of FAA Certification

Beta’s 17,500-square-meter production facility sits beside the Burlington airport and looks more like a giant sunlit workshop than a conventional airplane factory. Geothermal heat pumps help heat and cool the structure. The roof will eventually hold a megawatt of solar panels. On the floor, technicians make composite structures and join aircraft sections beneath American and Pride flags.


3 Competing Paths to Electric Aviation

The top three U.S. electric-aircraft makers are taking different approaches to the same challenge: how to make short-hop flight cleaner, quieter, and commercially useful.

Joby Aviation’s piloted, four-passenger S4 aircraft has six tilting propellers to provide both vertical lift and cruise propulsion. It’s optimized for fast, quiet air-taxi service. The company is also working on hydrogen fuel cells for longer-range transport. Joby says the S4 is in the final stage of the Federal Aviation Administration’s certification process and has flown trials in Dubai, New York, and Texas.

Archer Aviation’s piloted, four-passenger Midnight has 12 propellers—six tilting rotors and six stationary rotors. The aircraft, which Archer has been testing in both vertical and conventional takeoffs and landings, is aimed at air-taxi service in urban areas. The company says Midnight is in Phase 4 of the FAA certification process and has completed trial flights.

Beta Technologies’ piloted Alia aircraft comes in two flavors: The A250 eVTOL has separate vertical-lift rotors and a rear cruise propeller, while the CX300 is a conventional-takeoff fixed-wing plane. The two models use the same pusher motor, battery pack, flight control computer, and fixed-pitch propeller. The company is initially focusing on cargo and medical transport, regional logistics, and airport-to-airport operations, with passenger service later. Beta expects FAA certification for the CX300 in 2027 and for the A250 a year later.


Kyle Clark, a native of Vermont, founded Beta in 2017 to realize his long-held dream of building a hybrid-electric aircraft. (For his bachelor’s thesis at Harvard University, he designed a flight-control system for such an aircraft.) Martine Rothblatt, CEO of the biotech company United Therapeutics and cofounder of SiriusXM satellite radio, invested $48 million in Beta to fund its first prototype, with the eventual goal of using the aircraft to transport organs for transplantation.

The company has kept much of its design and manufacturing in-house, partly because the pieces are closely coupled and partly because suitable aviation components didn’t exist when development began. Vertical integration gives Beta’s engineers control over the interfaces and any failure data. But it also means the company must be competent at several businesses simultaneously: airframes, electric motors, inverters, batteries, software, chargers, and aircraft production.

The production line can build both the fixed-wing CX300 and the vertical-lift A250, which share the same fuselage, wings, pusher motor (for horizontal propulsion), batteries, avionics, and much of the tooling. This results in some one-size-fits-all decisions: The CX300’s twin booms, which connect the wing to the tail surfaces, look superfluous until you see the A250’s four lift propellers mounted on similar structures.

On the CX300, those booms add weight and drag even though they serve no lift function. But the trade-off—a simpler overall design—is part of Beta’s central strategy: Rather than applying for FAA certification for the more novel and complex eVTOL A250, with its multiple lift systems and transitions between hover and wing-borne flight, the company is bringing the runway version forward first. It’s certifying the motor, batteries, fly-by-wire controls, and much of the airframe in the simpler aircraft; it will then reuse evidence and manufacturing experience when it certifies the A250.

The other big reason to start with the CX300 is logistical: The United States has more than 5,000 public-use airports, so the CX300 can operate from infrastructure that already exists. The cargo and medical flights that the aircraft will initially tackle offer tightly defined missions in which lower energy and maintenance costs matter more than cabin amenities. Hence the company’s decision to leave cabin air-conditioning off the CX300, at least for now. “Things that don’t exist on an airplane don’t weigh anything,” Clark says. “They can’t break. You don’t have to service them…. You don’t have to certify them.”

“This strategy, at the beginning, was seen as a failure of their VTOL,” says analyst Sergio Cecutta, a managing partner at SMG Consulting. “But I think a lot of people were very myopic. It was a way for [Beta] to break up the certification in two pieces, and so they have retired a lot of the risk. [A majority] of the [CX300’s] parts are shared with the VTOL, and that means that a lot of the documentation for certification can be easily reused on the VTOL side when that certification starts.”

How Redundancy Enhances Safety

A decade and a half after electric cars began making significant inroads into the automotive market, electric airplane technology is advancing rapidly. In some ways, an electric car has more complicated power-engineering requirements than an electric aircraft. An electric car’s power demands change constantly, while an electric aircraft operates in a narrower set of power regimes: takeoff, level flight, and descent.

But if your car’s drivetrain fails, you can just pull over to the side of the road. That’s not an option for a pilot 1,000 meters up. The hurdle isn’t that electric motors are inherently fragile; it’s that aviation safety standards are extraordinarily demanding. Catastrophic failure conditions generally must be held to roughly one chance in a billion flight hours or less.



Because individual electrical components cannot meet that standard on their own, electric aircraft rely on layered redundancy: duplicate power paths, independent inverters, fault isolation, monitoring, and backup capacity. Each layer helps close the safety gap, but each also adds weight and complexity.

To close the reliability gap and satisfy regulators who have little experience with electric aircraft, Beta engineers have built redundancy into the motor itself. When I visit the company’s motor shop, technicians are assembling the H500A pusher motor, which splits up critical components like the inverters and stator sections.

“You could lose an entire half of the system, and it would still function the same,” says Adam Lowe, Beta’s flight operations lead.

The H500A’s broad diameter gives the magnetic forces greater leverage, producing high torque at low rotational speed, a useful trait when turning large propellers. The H500A weighs about 75 kilograms and produces about 640 kilowatts of power. The motor achieves 97.5 percent efficiency, based on internal testing; typical conventional turboprops have an efficiency range of 25 to 35 percent.

Beta had aimed to have its motor certified in the first half of 2026, a deadline that passed without a public announcement. Although the company’s certification strategy is aimed at streamlining the process, getting clearance to fly from the Federal Aviation Administration is probably still its biggest obstacle. For Beta, and any company trying to bring an electric aircraft into service, the FAA must sign off first on the propeller, then the motor, then the aircraft. After that, the manufacturer must pursue a production certificate from the FAA, proving that the company can build the same approved aircraft again and again, with the right tools, records, inspections, quality controls, supplier oversight, training, calibration, and parts tracking.



Beta has found a narrow on-ramp to get the A250 flying before full certification. Under the FAA’s eVTOL Integration Pilot Program, companies and public agencies can run FAA-supervised, real-world trials to test how advanced air-mobility aircraft might operate in the national airspace. For Beta, that included July flights of the CX300 across a Virginia-to-Maryland corridor carrying, among other things, a manufactured transplantable organ in a medical containment system. Beta has also been chosen for six other FAA-backed trials.

For the fixed-wing Alia CX300, customer trials are moving in parallel. In March, Beta’s CX300 successfully completed 23 flights over 10 days on Loganair routes linking six Scottish cities. The airline has since signed a term sheet for five Beta aircraft, with options for five more and service expected to start in 2029.

Innovation in eVTOLs

Beta is pushing so hard for the A250 because the case for electric flight gets even more interesting with vertical-lift aircraft. Helicopters and other VTOLs have a less orderly life than fixed-wing planes do. They must hover, sidestep, turn in place, settle, climb, and hold themselves in the air by main force. But conventional helicopters are built around a rotor speed that varies only a few percent because the engine, transmission, and rotor system are all designed around one narrow operating point.

“One of the dreams of vertical flight has been to vary the rotor rpm,” says Anubhav Datta, an aerospace engineering professor at the University of Maryland, College Park, who studies electric propulsion and vertical flight. Electric motors, he argues, have advanced enough to make that practical. A rotor that can slow down or speed up as conditions change can waste less energy, make less noise, and better match the odd assortment of tasks that vertical flight demands.

Electric propulsion also lets designers break up the work with multiple motors. The weight of an electric motor is tied closely to its torque. If you spread that torque among several motors, each of the motors can shrink, meaning that their combined weight is less than would be needed for one big engine. This is one reason why so many electric aircraft designs appear to have sprouted propellers wherever an engineer found a place to put one.

There is a downside to using multiple smaller motors: If one of the motors quits, the rotor it’s driving has little stored energy compared to the inertia in a conventional helicopter’s one large rotor. This inertia can come in handy if the engine dies, because the rotor will continue to spin and allow the pilot to descend under control, in an emergency technique known as autorotation.

Beta Technologies, Made in Vermont


According to Beta Technologies founder and CEO Kyle Clark, his native Vermont—known more for its maple syrup and outdoorsy lifestyle than its technological achievements—is a big part of the secret to Beta’s success at attracting talent. “The recruiting strategy is this: You find anybody who has vacationed in Vermont or went to school here. And you say, ‘You can actually live here and have a high-paying job in a mission-driven business, doing something that you care about with great people.’ ” The company currently employs about 1,500 people, most of them in South Burlington, Vt., and is actively hiring for its engineering, manufacturing, software, flight operations, and commercial teams.

As befits an aviation company, Beta offers flying lessons for all employees—a perk that is intended to give engineers, software developers, pilots, and mechanics a common language. The company also operates a health clinic for employees and their families and is building a day care center. Those programs have an idealistic Vermont flavor that is shaped by Clark and his wife, Katie Clark, who is Beta’s head of brand, creative, culture and spaces. But they also serve a practical purpose: attracting and retaining the best people in a small labor market. —T.C.

Electric-aircraft makers compensate with redundant motors and electrical channels, but most leading designs also have wings to allow gliding. In Beta’s A250, that means that if all else fails, the pilot still has a shot at reaching a suitable landing spot.

That helps explain the convergence in today’s eVTOL designs. Joby Aviation’s S4 uses six propellers that tilt between lift and cruise. Archer Aviation’s Midnight has six tilting propellers and six dedicated lift props. Beta’s A250 keeps its four lift propellers horizontal and uses a separate pusher motor for forward flight.

Beta’s Approach to Batteries

In Beta’s home state of Vermont, the electricity produced in the state comes almost entirely from renewable sources. Of course, electric aviation’s climate advantage depends not only on how clean the power grid is, but on how efficiently the aircraft uses energy. The turbines in conventional jet engines are marvelous machines, but they throw away a lot of energy as heat. Plus they need to carry the hardware to manage that heat—inlets, ducts, radiators, oil coolers—and the drag that comes with pulling air through all of it.

Kyle Clark [in cockpit], founder and CEO of Beta Technologies, pilots the company’s Alia CX300, an electric plane that Beta expects to receive FAA certification in 2027. Clark began designing a hybrid electric plane while still in college.Beta Technologies

By contrast, an electric design, with no combustion, gearbox, or oil circuits, avoids much of that energy burden. The result is an aircraft that is more than twice as “slippery” in terms of its lift-to-drag ratio. Batteries will never come close to the fuel density of petroleum fuels. But because electric drivetrains are much more efficient than combustion engines, a battery doesn’t necessarily need to match the energy density of petroleum fuel to be useful.

While the motors on an electric aircraft provide the drama at takeoff and landing, the batteries set nearly every boundary: the range, payload, charge time, cooling load, reserve energy, and, ultimately, useful life. For now, the hardest limit on electric aviation is not imagination, software, or even motors. It is the stubborn weight of stored energy.

“One thing you notice about [electric] airplanes is that they are all small,” says Cecutta, the aviation analyst. “They are not small because someone has done a market study and decided that is the optimal size. They are small because you cannot make them big. This limitation is from the batteries.”

In a conventional aircraft, fuel accounts for a significant percentage of the takeoff weight. But that weight gradually disappears during flight. In an electric plane, the battery packs also account for a lot of the weight; in Beta’s CX300, they’re close to half the aircraft’s mass. But a drained battery at landing weighs the same as a fully charged one at takeoff. That means the rest of the aircraft must be light enough to lift a power source that never gets lighter, yet strong enough to endure vibration, turbulence, hard landings, and years of fatigue. The more engineers push to save weight, Datta notes, the more flexible the airframe becomes, making the wings and other structures more prone to bending, twisting, and vibration. In that sense, the battery problem becomes a structural problem, too.

Beta’s CX300 carries five liquid-cooled battery packs of about 45 kilowatt-hours each, giving the aircraft roughly 225 kWh of rated capacity, though not all of that may be usable in flight. Each pack weighs 255 kg and contains 2,772 cylindrical cells. Altogether, about 14,000 cells sit beneath the occupied cabin, each one a tiny storehouse of energy, heat, and potential trouble.

The burden of the battery explains much of what I see elsewhere in Beta’s aircraft: the 15-meter wingspan, the composite structure, and the almost doctrinal effort to leave out every piece of hardware and creature comfort that’s not essential to the mission. The CX300 even does without flaps, which most aircraft use to generate extra lift at low speeds, so it must take off and land unusually fast for an aircraft of its size.

On Beta’s battery line, commercially supplied standard 21-millimeter-diameter, 70-mm-long cells are tested and assembled. Cells become modules, modules become subpacks, and eleven subpacks become a 45-kWh pack. Cooling plates manage heat during normal operation, while passive barriers and electrical isolation are designed to contain the ugly edge cases that Beta’s testing process intentionally triggers.

The modular design of the packs will give Beta’s planes a path to better batteries as the cells and battery chemistry improve. Caputo tells me that the company is already evaluating higher-density cells at its battery testing facility in nearby St. Albans, Vt. More watt-hours per kilogram could eventually mean more range, more payload, or both.

Beta Technologies’ Electric Ecosystem

What sets Beta Technologies apart from its competitors is the breadth of its ambitions: It’s not just building electric aircraft. It’s also cultivating a broader ecosystem to nurture the growth of electric aviation.

For instance, Beta intends its motor business to extend beyond its own aircraft. The company has delivered lift motors for Eve Air Mobility’s proof-of-concept aircraft and has a contract to supply up to $1 billion in pusher motors as Eve’s program develops. In addition, Beta is selling flight-control computers and software to Horizon Aircraft. It is also producing propulsion systems for the military, as well as the MV250, a hybrid-electric autonomous aircraft for defense missions.

Beta’s fast-charging stations [left] have been installed at 138 locations. The thermal-management station [right] keeps the battery from overheating during charging. The company uses the open-standard Combined Charging System (CCS) so that other aircraft can use its chargers. Beta Technologies

Further evidence of Beta’s far-ranging plans is visible near the battery line in the South Burlington facility, where workers are readying a few of the company’s 1.5-meter-tall Charge Cubes for shipping. Intended for installation at airports, each cube takes 480-volt AC from the grid and can deliver up to 320 kW at as much as 1,000 volts DC. According to Beta, charging either model of its Alia aircraft takes about 35 minutes, from the minimum charge required under visual flight rules to full state. A separate thermal-management cube circulates water-glycol coolant through the aircraft to keep the batteries from overheating during charging.

The charge cubes use the Combined Charging System (CCS), an open standard increasingly shared across the electric transportation industry. Beta’s strategy resembles Tesla’s decision to treat charging as part of the vehicle business, with one important difference: Beta is courting other aircraft makers, airports, and operators to use its technology. Archer, Atlantic Aviation, Signature Aviation, and government agencies have purchased or installed Beta’s charging equipment. As of October, Beta has 138 fast-charge locations, with additional sites in permitting or construction.

For charging beyond the fixed network, Beta offers a wheeled 40-kW Mini Cube and a 65-kW Mini Cube+ that can ride inside an Alia and plug into more modest electrical service. The Mini Cube can also charge cars, buses, and trucks—any vehicle that uses the CCS standard.

Back above Lake Champlain, the CX300 levels off, and the motor recedes to a faint hum. Then Caputo switches it off, and there’s nothing but the rush of wind around the canopy. The aircraft glides on its long wings while the coasting propeller turns the motor into a generator, feeding about 9 kW back to the battery.

Then we turn to the east and slip into the landing queue behind a National Guard C-130 Hercules, and Caputo powers and slides the throttle forward. The motor springs to life again, pushing the airplane, and perhaps aviation itself, into the future.

Julius Thomas Speaks His Mind When It Comes to Peak Performance

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

When Julius Thomas hung up his NFL cleats in 2018, he didn’t know he was about to face the hardest, and longest, transition of his life. The tight end who caught touchdowns from Peyton Manning walked into graduate school at 30 with no job, no playbook, and none of the daily wins football used to hand him.

“When you have to go from a master to a rookie, you go from winning a lot, knowing what you’re doing and very comfortable with all of it, to then having to rebuild that,” he recalled. “That is extremely challenging, because in the beginning of any journey, it’s losing, doing it wrong, working really hard, struggling through it.”

The lessons he learned over the years that followed changed the way he looked at performance. He went back to the classroom, completed a clinical residency and earned his doctorate in clinical psychology in June 2025.

In pro sports, the mentality is often to push through and keep going. While Thomas lived that for years, today, as a performance coach and consultant with his company Optimal Performance, he sees how much of peak performance relies on stepping back and checking in with ourselves.

“One of the things I say is the biggest unaddressed area of performance in all of sport is emotional regulation,” Thomas told Muscle & Fitness.

Thomas’ Take on NFL Injuries This Season

The 2026 NFL season has already tested the push-through mindset from the opening weeks. Six of the 32 quarterbacks initially named starters were hurt early in the season, including Jayden Daniels, Caleb Williams and Jaxson Dart. Now Philadelphia Eagles quarterback Jalen Hurts is now expected to miss the rest of the season after doctors found deep vein thrombosis and bilateral pulmonary emboli, with his case traced back to a calf injury from an opponent’s cleat.

Thomas resisted pinning the run on any single cause. He also didn’t think any of this was unusual. “Football has a 100% injury rate” he says. “Every single person that plays football is going to get injured, so injuries are just high in that sport in general.”

Contact injuries come with the job, he said, while training loads, surfaces, off-season habits, mobility, and recovery—or the lack thereof—shape the non-contact ones. Research has also shown that the mind does have a seat at the table.

A 2017 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine linked stress responses to higher injury risk and found psychological interventions reduced injury rates. Hence, Thomas makes it a point to take our foot off the gas a lot more frequently than we do.

“If you just only floor it, only floor it, only floor it. How long do you think that engine is going to do well?” Thomas explains. “Not very long.”

The brakes represent the parasympathetic nervous system, the rest-and-digest branch that counters the stress response. “If we don’t do enough to create more parasympathetic tone in our nervous system, if we don’t sleep enough hours, all we’re doing is degrading the major systems in our body,” he explained, noting the cost of the grind will come sooner or later.

Julius Thomas

How Every High Performer Pays the Tab

This pattern follows high performers into the boardroom as well. Thomas has coached executives who asked him for nootropics to sharpen their focus—then admitted that he himself was only getting four hours of sleep a night for 20 years. So he’s built a framework called “Striving for 100” to bring clients back to five daily habits, including exercise, sleep, nutrition, recovery and healthy substance use, with recovery anchoring the rest. One-on-one, he layers in radical acceptance, cognitive restructuring and reframing drawn from several evidence-based methods such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT).

“You’re either gonna pay the cost slowly and surely by recovering in your 30s and 40s and 50s,” he says, “or you’re gonna pay it all of a sudden when you’re sick.”

He holds himself to the same standard. Though when a friend had warned him a full transition would take about a decade, he refused to believe it.

“I am eight years in transition. And I’m almost to where I want to be in my next profession,” he admitted, but pointed out the difficulty he’s experienced with identity and how much our society gets it wrong.

Thomas bristles at the advice athletes often hear on the way out, that they simply need to separate who they are from the sport they played.

“It really bothers me when people say that about athletes, because they’re trying to minimize the challenge of the experience, so that when the athlete finds himself in the psychological challenge, they feel like they shouldn’t be, and this should be easy,” he explained, adding that identity runs deeper than a label, and it’s built by what you do every day and reinforced by how others see you.

Years after his last game, people still want to talk about NFL playing days, in which the two-time Pro Bowl tight end caught 12 touchdown passes in both 2012 and 2013. “It’s hard to get people to see you in your new career,” he admitted.

Now he prepares athletes for that shift and asks them whether they’re ready for one of the hardest things they’ll ever do, and whether they’ve built the tools to get through it.

“Do you have the psychological skills to reframe, recontextualize, find meaning, find purpose?” he’d ask.

What he doesn’t want any player to forget is that the struggle says nothing about them. Every major life change strains us. “Transitions are always hard,” he said. But with the right tools and support, it becomes easier to navigate over time.

Julius Thomas

When the Pain Doesn’t Show on an X-Ray

Most of us were never taught to name what we feel, let alone work through it when stress drags on for months. Thomas wasn’t either, and he’s seen the toll it could take on the body.

“Emotions trigger our nervous system,” he says, referring to his studies in psychoneuroimmunology. “So the better you get at managing emotions and reducing negative emotions, the less sustained stress you’d have on your nervous system.”

And it matters because our nervous system, immune system, and psychology are connected, he says, and when stress becomes chronic, various aspects of our life take a hit. Sleep and diet slip first when emotional strain builds, he noted, and muscle tension climbs as anxiety lingers.

However, simply putting a feeling into words can help turn the volume down. A 2007 UCLA brain imaging study in Psychological Science found that labeling emotions dampened the amygdala’s response to negative images.

Though few of us get support doing that work. He cited CDC data that reported 10.9% of U.S. men receive counseling or therapy from a mental health professional, compared with 16.9% of women. And, unfortunately, Thomas sees where we choose to spend time and money instead.

“People are not interested in paying for mental health services, but they’re really interested in new pants and new shirts and nice bags,” he said, adding how much easier it is for many of us to ask for help with physical pain or discomfort. As a player, he never hesitated to walk into the training room with a sore ankle.

“We are constantly asking for help in so many areas of our life,” he says, “but we somehow, as a culture, believe that we should handle what’s in our heart and in our mind by ourselves.”

“If you want sustainable high performance, you have to build well-being and quality of life into what you do,” he said.
