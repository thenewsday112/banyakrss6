Semi-automatic shotguns shoot more reliably than ever before. They aren’t finicky about ammunition, and they will shoot for a long time in between cleanings. The fact that semi-autos have become the primary gun among waterfowlers tells you all you need to know about the semi-auto’s reliability in tough conditions. That doesn’t mean semi-autos are made only for duck hunting. They also fit right into upland hunting, target shooting, and turkey hunting. You have plenty of choices, and a 12-gauge semi-automatic shotgun can handle almost any shooting chore.

There are also two main types of semi-automatic shotguns to choose between: gas-operated guns, which have the advantage of reduced recoil at the cost of a little more maintenance, and inertia guns, which stay cleaner longer.  But what semi-automatic shotgun is right for you? What to Look for When Choosing a Semi-Automatic Shotgun

A semi-auto shotgun is absolutely the most versatile of all shotguns, but you should still choose your new gun with its main use in mind. Aside from the choices you make based on weight, barrel length, and so on, when you buy any shotgun, there are a couple of additional factors to consider when choosing asemi-auto:

  • Gas guns have noticeably less felt recoil
  • Inertia guns work well in bad weather
  • Inertia guns stay cleaner longer than gas guns
  • Typically, gas guns are more complicated to take apart and clean

Some 3 ½-inch 12-gauge semi-autos won’t cycle very light loads well. Many 3-inch guns will cycle light loads reliably. If you want to shoot targets and hunt with a gun, a 3-inch semi-auto may be a better choice.

FAQs

Q: Is a semi-auto shotgun worth it?

There is no more versatile shotgun made than a 12- or 20-gauge semi-automatic. It can shoot any load and acquit itself well in any type of shooting situation. They are light enough to carry, long and steady enough to shoot at targets, and gas guns especially reduce felt recoil. If you’re only going to own one shotgun and want to use it for a variety of purposes, it should be a semi-automatic. Even a top-of-the-line gun is worth it if you can use it for everything.

Q: How reliable are semi-auto shotguns?

Semi-auto reliability has improved a great deal. Most good semi-automatic shotguns can run for many hundreds of rounds without cleaning and without malfunction. Inertia guns are especially known for working well in wet, nasty conditions of waterfowl hunting. Semi-autos are also much less picky about ammunition than they used to be. You have to do your part to maintain the gun, and if you do, it will serve you very reliably.

Q: Do semi-auto shotguns jam?

Semi-autos used to have the nickname “jam-a-matics” in the 60s and 70s. The development of the inertia action, first sold by Italian makers Benelli and Breda in the 1960s, and improvements in gas-gun design have made semi-automatic shotguns extremely reliable. They can still jam, especially if they aren’t maintained by their owners. But many hunters and target shooters go through entire seasons without a problem with their semi-auto.

Q: What are the advantages of a semi-automatic shotgun?

A semi-auto offers more firepower than a break-action double without the need to manually cycle the action, as you do with a pump gun. Semi-autos can be made very light, with the use of alloy and plastic parts that don’t compromise performance. Gas-operated semi-autos have the huge advantage of reducing felt recoil. The moving parts of the gun essentially store, then release, recoil energy over a longer period, changing the feel of recoil from a kick to a shove.

Final Thoughts

Semi-automatic shotguns have achieved a level of versatility and reliability unknown to previous generations of shooters. There’s a gun for any purpose, plus plenty of all-purpose guns, as the list we’ve highlighted here shows. The Browning Maxus II, our best overall gun, is a good example of what a semi-auto can be: light, soft-recoiling, versatile, and adjustable to fit a range of shooters. On the target gun side, the Beretta A400 Xcel can go toe-to-toe with $10,000 O/Us in sporting clays competition, while a field gun like the Benelli SBE3 will shoot in any weather. There has never been a better time to buy a semi-automatic shotgun.

From garage-hammered pitons to swami-belt harnesses, climbers have been making their own equipment since the sport was in its infancy. Gear has come a long way since then. It’s also gotten a heck of a lot more expensive. While we can’t in good faith recommend you save money by making your own chocks and cams, there are still a number of things you can DIY for cheap.

Earlier this month, I went on a DIY kick and decided to hand-make a few pieces myself. Some were items I’d seen friends carry around at the crag. Other ideas came from online forums (and this magazine). With a single trip to the hardware store and a heap of old gear that’s been rotting in the trunk of my car, I was able to make five things—and saved $183 total. Here’s what I came up with, how much each item cost, and what I would do differently next time.

1. Ice tool leash

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $39.95 for an Edelrid Spinner Leash on Backcountry

DIY cost: $5
Savings: $35

Materials:

  • Double-length 1-inch tubular webbing
  • Tent pole shock cord (from an old tent, or $5 on Amazon)
  • 2 carabiners
  • Wire coat hanger
  • Lighter

Dropped tools are no joke. At Colorado’s ice parks, losing one will typically earn you a swim in a very frigid creek. On multi-pitch ice, you could end up stranded. The trick is to clip your tools to your harness via a bungee system that’s elastic enough to preserve freedom of motion, but not so springy as to send your dropped tool boomeranging pick-first into your thigh

Purpose-made leashes are pricey. A DIY version is free if you have old tent poles lying around your house and only $5 if you need to purchase new shock-cord. I started by clipping the bar tack off an old piece of double-length tubular webbing. I then tied one end of the shock cord to a piece of wire, and shoved it through the tube (this took forever). Once I could access the shock cord from either end, I tied an overhand knot in the center to create a girth-hitchable loop for my harness. I then scrunched the webbing down as much as possible over the two legs of the shock cord, tied overhangs for both carabiners, and melted the edges to finish them.

(Note: For another $10, you can purchase a swivel from most hardware stores, putting the result on par with $80 swiveling leashes.)

2. Nut tool tether

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $17 for a Personal Tool Tether on Gear Keeper

DIY cost: $5

Savings: $12

Materials:

  • Keychain spring coil or telephone cord ($3 at Goodwill)
  • Wire crimps ($2 at the hardware store)
  • Crimping tool or needle-nose pliers
  • Scissors

Some nut tools come with integrated leashes. These don’t add a ton of cost to the tool, but they are boring as all get out. Aftermarket versions from Gear Keeper, Amazon, and beyond aren’t much better. The answer: Make your own out of an old spring-coil keychain bracelet.

First, snip the bracelet in half and feed an electrical crimp onto either end. Next, fold one end of the coil around the inside edge of a nut tool, tuck the end into the nearest crimp, and squeeze the crimp shut with pliers or a crimper tool. Then do the same thing around a small accessory carabiner to complete the tether.

3. Stick clip

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $94.95 for a Metolius Basic Stick Clip at REI

DIY cost: $25

Savings: $70

Materials:

  • Painter’s pole ($20 at the hardware store)
  • Small spring clamp ($1 at the hardware store)
  • Two stainless steel hose clamps ($4 at the hardware store)

The cost of stick clips has always baffled me, especially since you can make one yourself with a tree branch, a small stick, and a bit of tape. However, I’ll concede that not everyone loves tramping around the crag looking for an appropriately sturdy stick. For those folks, try this trick.

You can buy a painter’s pole from just about any hardware store for relatively cheap. The ones at my local were $20 for 8 feet or $40 for 23. Once you’ve got that, use a couple of stainless steel hose clamps (basically big metal zip ties) to attach a small spring-loaded clamp. Voila!

4. Stiffy draw

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $54.95 for a Panic Quickdraw on Kong

DIY cost: $15

Savings: $40

Materials:

  • Mad Rock Trigger Wire Carabiner ($11.95 at Rock and Resole)
  • 12 inches heavy-duty insulated housing wire ($3 at the hardware store)
  • Single-length 1-inch tubular webbing
  • Bent-gate carabiner
  • Duct tape
  • Scissors

The internet said I could make one of these by stuffing a piece of webbing into a section of transparent vinyl tubing and tying carabiners to the ends. I tried this and found the result upsettingly floppy. Vinyl tubing is also much more expensive than heavy-duty housing wire, which you can purchase by the foot at Home Depot. Tubing has the added benefit of being foldable.

So, after my first attempt failed, I went back to an old Matt Samet recipe and shoved a section of wire into the hollow core of a length of tubular webbing. I then tied the ends with a water knot to make a continuous loop, and duct-taped a Mad Rock Trigger Wire Carabiner and a bent-gate carabiner to either end. It’s not quite as long as other purpose-made stiffy draws, but it does the trick at a fraction of the price.

5. Rope tarp

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $24.95 for a Trango Cord Trapper on Backcountry

DIY cost: $4

Savings: $21

Materials:

  • Heavy-duty 1 mm plastic drop cloth ($4 at a hardware store)
  • Transparent shipping tape
  • Colored tape of your choice
  • Scissors

I first saw a DIY rope tarp like this at the notoriously dusty Vail Amphitheatre. It’s incredibly lightweight and astonishingly cheap. All you have to do is cut a four-by-four section from a painter’s plastic drop sheet and affix loops to opposite corners. (Note: I used 1 mm-weight plastic, but it felt a little flimsy; 2 mm would be better and only costs $1 more. Tyvek House Wrap would also be superior, but it tends to be more expensive if you don’t happen to have a housing contractor in your life.)

I found that heavy duty plastic shipping tape (especially the kind designed for long-term storage) sticks better than just about anything else. Tape a loop to either corner, and add colored tape to distinguish the belay end from the climber’s end when the rope is stacked.

So, you want to be a school administrator?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As an educational leadership professor, I have coached many students aspiring to transition from classroom teaching to assistant principal and principal positions. Through this work, I have reviewed countless résumés, and one thing continues to surprise me: how many look almost identical to those used to apply for teaching positions, with little more than an administrative certification or graduate degree added.

Earning an administrative license establishes eligibility for school leadership positions, but it does not automatically make someone a competitive candidate. A résumé should reflect the transition from classroom teacher to aspiring school leader by demonstrating the ability to lead others, influence school practices, and make a meaningful impact.

The “So What?” Test

I recently came across a LinkedIn post by executive coach Melissa Schmidiger that made an important point about résumés: every bullet should pass the “so what?” test. Rather than simply describing responsibilities, applicants should demonstrate what they accomplished and what changed because of their work. This advice is particularly relevant to aspiring school leaders.

I frequently encounter statements such as:

  • “Responsible for facilitating professional development.”
  • “Participated in instructional leadership team meetings.”
  • “Served as grade-level chairperson.”
  • “Collaborated with administrators on schoolwide initiatives.”

There is nothing inherently wrong with these statements, but they tell me very little about the candidate’s actual leadership contributions. You facilitated professional development. So what? What did teachers learn? Did instructional practices change? How do you know?

These are precisely the types of questions aspiring administrators should expect when interviewing for leadership positions. A résumé that lists responsibilities tells me where you have been. A résumé that demonstrates impact helps me understand what you accomplished while you were there.

Sometimes It’s the Résumé. Sometimes It’s the Experience.

Through coaching aspiring school leaders, I have noticed two groups of candidates. The first has actually done quite a bit of leadership work. They have mentored colleagues, facilitated professional learning, helped develop schoolwide initiatives, led collaborative teams, and addressed meaningful problems of practice. Yet their résumés fail to capture the depth of these experiences. They describe their work in terms of responsibilities rather than contributions and results.

For these candidates, the solution is relatively straightforward. Instead of writing “Responsible for mentoring new teachers,” a candidate might explain how they developed a mentoring structure, supported colleagues with instructional planning, and used classroom observations or feedback to assess improvements. The experience is already there; the résumé simply needs to communicate it more effectively.

The second group presents a more complicated challenge. These individuals have earned their administrative credentials and completed the required internship, but they have limited experience leading beyond their own classrooms. Their résumés may be filled with descriptions of successful teaching, committee memberships, and graduate coursework. What is often missing is evidence that they have taken responsibility for leading adults, improving systems, or addressing challenges beyond their individual classrooms.

This is not necessarily a résumé-writing problem. It is an experience problem, and no amount of creative wording will compensate for leadership experiences that have not yet occurred.

Leadership Experience Doesn’t Begin With a Title

Being an excellent teacher is a tremendous asset when pursuing school leadership. Successful teachers understand instruction, students, families, and the daily realities of classrooms. However, the work of an assistant principal or principal requires additional competencies, including supporting adult learning, navigating difficult conversations, analyzing competing priorities, allocating resources, building trust, and making decisions that affect an entire school community.

A teacher may have tremendous success with students but still need opportunities to develop these broader leadership capacities. Fortunately, aspiring administrators do not need to wait for an official title to begin developing them.

Leadership opportunities can take many forms: facilitating professional learning communities, coordinating interventions, mentoring colleagues, leading curriculum development, examining attendance trends, or helping a school address an instructional challenge. What matters is not simply joining another committee or adding another responsibility. Candidates should be able to explain the problem they addressed, their specific contribution, and the results of their efforts.

Leadership experience is not necessarily about how many activities appear on a résumé. It is about the substance of those experiences and what they reveal about a candidate’s ability to work with and through others.

Your Internship Should Help Tell That Story

Educational leadership preparation programs also have a responsibility to help candidates develop these experiences. An administrative internship should be more than a collection of completed hours or a checklist of activities. While observing administrative work and participating in school operations are valuable, aspiring leaders also need opportunities to exercise judgment, solve problems, work with colleagues, and reflect on the consequences of their decisions.

If your internship involved attending meetings, observing administrators, and assisting with school operations, those may have been worthwhile experiences. But what did you learn about leadership? What opportunities did you have to influence decisions or contribute to improvements?

Consider an intern who identifies a pattern of chronic absenteeism, works with school staff to examine underlying causes, helps develop an intervention, and monitors its implementation. That experience tells a much richer leadership story than simply writing “Assisted with attendance initiatives.”

Even when an initiative does not produce the intended results, candidates should be able to explain the leadership process, the evidence considered, the challenges encountered, and what they learned. Impact is not always a percentage increase or a dramatic turnaround. Sometimes it is a stronger process, improved collaboration, a better-informed decision, or a meaningful change in practice.

Before You Submit That Application

I encourage aspiring administrators to review their résumés through a different lens. Ask yourself:

  1. Does my résumé describe primarily what I did as a teacher, or does it demonstrate my development as a leader?
  2. Can I identify specific examples of how I influenced the work of other adults?
  3. Have I helped address a problem that extended beyond my own classroom?
  4. What changed because of my contributions, and what evidence supports that conclusion?
  5. If I cannot answer these questions, what leadership experiences do I need to pursue next?

These questions should not be reserved for the weeks leading up to an interview. They should inform how aspiring administrators approach their graduate coursework, internships, professional responsibilities, and opportunities for growth. Waiting until application season to think about leadership experience may mean discovering too late that there are significant gaps in your preparation.

A License Is a Starting Point, Not the Finish Line

My intention is not to discourage teachers from pursuing school leadership. Schools need thoughtful, capable educators who are willing to take on the challenges of leading others. But preparation for that transition should be intentional, and candidates need to understand that demonstrating effectiveness in the classroom is not the same as demonstrating readiness to lead a school.

For some aspiring administrators, the next step is to revise their résumés so that their leadership accomplishments are more visible. For others, the next step is to seek out the experiences that will eventually make those accomplishments possible.

Your administrative license tells me you have met the requirements to apply. Your résumé should tell me what you have done to prepare yourself to lead.

And if you cannot yet tell that story, perhaps the next step is not another revision of your résumé, but another opportunity to lead.

Perimenopause Begins Earlier and Hits Harder for Women with ADHD

Perimenopause is destabilizing, and even debilitating for many women with ADHD. In an ADDitude survey of nearly 5,000 women, 63% of respondents aged 45 and older said ADHD had the greatest impact on their lives during the menopausal transition.

Fluctuating estrogen levels affect attention, memory, executive function, and emotional regulation. Meanwhile, hot flashes, poor sleep, anxiety, and brain fog place added stress on an already strained nervous system.

Emerging research suggests that women with ADHD may experience perimenopause and its symptoms earlier than their neurotypical peers. In a study published in European Psychiatry, women with ADHD reported their most severe perimenopausal symptoms from ages 35 to 39 — almost a decade younger than women without ADHD. Their symptoms were also relatively more severe, which may help explain the uptick in ADHD diagnoses at this time.1

We cannot yet definitively say that hormonal changes begin earlier in women with ADHD. However, research suggests that significant symptoms of perimenopause may emerge earlier than clinicians expect.

Perimenopause Is Under-Recognized

If your periods, sleep, mood, or ADHD symptoms have changed, do not dismiss them because a doctor says you’re “too young” for menopause. These changes deserve thoughtful medical evaluation.

In a large study published in the journal Menopause, one in three women aged 35 and older said they were unsure whether they were in perimenopause. Many did not know whether their symptoms reflected hormonal changes, especially if their periods remained regular and/or they had other health conditions. About 16% attributed their uncertainty to barriers such as dismissive healthcare encounters, including being told they were “too young.” 2

Adding to the problem, some women experience perimenopause symptoms while their menstrual cycles remain regular. They may not develop obvious menstrual changes until later in the transition or until they’ve entered menopause.

“It is a universal transition with ambiguity and often lack of validation,” the study’s authors wrote. Better symptom recognition and communication are critical first steps toward improving care.

Hormone Testing for Perimenopause

Hormone tests are typically used when symptoms begin earlier than expected, the diagnosis is unclear, or another condition is suspected. If testing is warranted, your doctor should look at follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and estrogen levels. FSH signals the ovaries to develop eggs and produce estradiol. As ovarian function becomes less predictable during perimenopause, FSH generally rises to compensate while estradiol fluctuates. Multiple tests may be needed to establish a clear picture.

If you are younger than 40 and have irregular periods along with perimenopause symptoms, ask whether you should be evaluated for primary ovarian insufficiency (POI), a condition that requires careful treatment.

Finding Stability in Perimenopause

I use the term “hormone stabilization therapy,” or HST, to describe hormonal treatment during perimenopause, when the goal is to smooth disruptive hormonal swings rather than replace hormones after menstruation has ended.

Certainty that you are in perimenopause is not required before discussing relief. Treatment should focus on the symptoms causing the greatest problems even while your doctor continues to evaluate their source.

Medication Considerations in Perimenopause

Estrogen therapy relieves symptoms of perimenopause and may improve the effectiveness of ADHD stimulant medication. Prior use of estrogen therapy may render it less effective. If you have a uterus and use systemic estrogen, you will need a progestogen to protect the uterine lining. The type and schedule should be individualized. Vaginal estrogen may be an option for urinary or vaginal symptoms, including for some women who cannot use systemic treatment.

ADHD medication may need to change — whether it’s the dose, timing, or type of medication — during this stage for continued symptom control.

Birth control is often recommended for its role in smoothing hormonal fluctuations. In my clinical experience, progestin-only birth control may worsen mood and symptoms of ADHD in some women Talk to your provider about your symptoms and whether other birth control options may be right for you.

Antidepressants and other non-hormonal medications can help manage hot flashes, mood swings, anxiety, and depression.

Online casino revenue in the United States is still climbing, but at the slowest pace analysts have tracked. New figures from Citizens put third-quarter iGaming growth at 12% over the same three months of 2025, the lowest rate on record and a step down from the 16% seen in the second quarter, NEXT.io reported.

Analysts quoted in the coverage said the slowdown was broadly expected. It also came with a slight late pickup: Gambling News noted that September growth reached 12%, after 11% in August.

FanDuel still leads as shares shift

None of the big names changed places in the iGaming rankings, but most of them lost ground. FanDuel kept top spot with 25.1% of the market, compared with 26.6% a year earlier. DraftKings fell to 22.2% from 24.3% on an annual basis, although that figure was better than its 20.9% in the second quarter. According to Citizens, adjustments DraftKings made to its business model in the spring probably explain the rebound, and NEXT.io described it as the operator’s first quarter-on-quarter gain in two years.

BetMGM slipped to 18.6% from 20.7%, which marks six quarters of decline in a row. Caesars dropped to 6.4% from 7.1%.

Fanatics was the clear exception. Its share rose to 5.2% from 3.9%, the biggest improvement of any operator, though still a touch below the 5.4% it held in the second quarter. Rush Street Interactive moved up to 7.4% from 6.8%, and bet365 reached 1.7% from 0.4% after going live in Michigan and West Virginia. Citizens analysts said the boost from those launches, and from Hard Rock’s entry into the same two states, was starting to wear off.

Sports betting holds up despite prediction markets

The sportsbook side of the report looked steadier. Handle grew 7% in the quarter even with one fewer week of NFL games in the period, and gross gaming margin was flat year on year at 9.6%. Same-store handle in September rose 1%, its best monthly showing of 2026 outside the World Cup months of June and July.

DraftKings held 37.6% of handle, compared with 37.3% a year ago, to stay the largest sportsbook, with FanDuel at 32.8%, up from 31.4%. FanDuel’s share of gross gaming revenue fell to 35.4% from 37.7%, yet its 10.4% margin beat the 9.5% DraftKings achieved. Fanatics took 9.9% of handle, down from 11.2%, while lifting its revenue share to 8.8% from 7.3%. BetMGM’s margin improved to 10.2%, and Caesars’ fell to 6.9%.

Those numbers fit a view Citizens analyst Jordan Bender has put forward before: that prediction markets are not a major threat to licensed sportsbooks, and that their heavy advertising may be bringing new customers into sports wagering rather than taking existing ones away. That debate has been running all autumn, with NFL contracts driving most prediction-market volume and state regulators pushing back on sports event contracts.

The iGaming slowdown also lands as legal online casino markets keep posting large totals in absolute terms. In New Jersey, online casinos outearned Atlantic City’s floors in September. Analysts expect sportsbook handle to rise in the fourth quarter as the NFL season continues.

Renovation Diaries: The House Envelope and Other Lessons in Efficiency

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: This is the latest in a series of posts by Sally Kohn— journalist and CNN political commentator, TED talk giver, and design aficionado—chronicling her adventures in remodeling.

Above: The exterior as it is now.

Let’s level set. I live in a 1780s barn that was turned into a house in the 1950s, and as far as I can tell the windows and the siding haven’t been changed since. The fact is, many parts of our money pit farmhouse need updating. Some, like the 1950s asphalt roof built directly on top of a much older shake shingle roof, we replaced last year. Other things we’re still waiting on, like the vintage 1950s bathroom tile with vintage crumbling grout. But since we can’t close many of our windows without pushing on them from the outside, and we have an inexplicable leak behind our very ancient siding, we put off the inevitable long enough. It is time for some grand-scale renovations.  

Stay tuned for some of the other things we’ll be changing, including a kitchen refresh. But first up, if we’re going to go to all the trouble—and, ahem, expense—of replacing all of our siding and windows, can we make our old home more environmentally responsible in terms of both materials and energy efficiency? Let’s find out.

My guide on this journey is Shannon Pendleton, principal of Sanderson Sustainable Design and co-host of Passive House Accelerator. In addition to being a friend and neighbor, Shannon is a leading expert on how to make homes more sustainable and energy efficient. And while those concepts might immediately make you think of solar panels and the like, Shannon explains that in energy efficiency, an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. In other words, using green energy to power your home is great. But designing your home to need less energy in the first place is even better.  

But with apologies to Shannon, when she explained the science of the “building envelope,” all I could think about is that envelope feels like the wrong word. Maybe it should be “building envelop”—as in, how enveloped is your building. Because your “building envelop” is what stops the outside environment from getting in and the inside environment from getting out. That means everything from bugs and dust to wind to heat and cold.  

Above: A tighter envelope = warmer interiors.

So I’m making two big changes to my “building envelop,” both of which create an opportunity for more energy efficiency. Let’s start with the siding. We have very old, very stained, very falling-apart cedar. I could just replace it with cedar, maybe even sustainably harvested cedar. But the fact is, our old cedar is cupping and splitting. Yes, more maintenance would help, but that’s hard to do with second-story wood. Also I’m lazy. Alternatively, I could have gone with some plastic product or chemically treated wood but, like I said, we care about the environment and also want to preserve the look and feel of our historic home. So we’re going with Thermory siding.  

Thermory takes sustainably forested wood—in our case, we’re using pine—and puts it through an intense heat-treating process that makes the wood more stable and rot-resistant. It’s an updated twist on the Japanese shou sugi ban process, where wood is charred to make it water and rot resistant. Thermory is that, without the blackening (though they actually do have a product that mirrors the shou sugi ban look, but with the added benefits of Thermory’s advanced thermal modification). In other words, Thermory gives us everything we like about natural wood plus all these added benefits.  

Using a more stable rot-resistant siding definitely envelops our house better (take that, flying squirrels), but the energy efficiency upgrades come from what will be behind the siding. Our contractor—the wonderful, lovable, fantastic-to-work-with John Gemmi and his company, Gemmi Construction—were already planning to weather-seal behind the siding, and of course put new insulation behind that where we find any lacking. But Shannon suggested installing “a rain screen, a continuous air barrier, and continuous exterior insulation” to give us the most enveloped envelope possible. Ultimately, we settled on Zip R sheathing, which does all three of those things in one system. So that’s our plan.

Above: Me, my wife Sarah, and the current siding and windows. Stay tuned for the final reveal.

As for the windows, well…. The most energy-efficient option is triple-pane glass, but in our case, since we’re replacing a gajillion windows, we just couldn’t make it work in our budget. We have two walls of windows in our great room, most of which are currently fixed panes that we’re excited to replace with operable awning windows. We looked into doing triple-pane for just those windows, but that would have made the windows too heavy to open. So yes, we’re choosing a little less efficiency there but counting on the fact that having new windows that don’t require a screwdriver and grunts to close will nevertheless up our efficiency. Plus, thanks to Ambiance Design, we’re adding retractable Duette Honeycomb shades over those large window walls, which will give us some extra energy efficiency on super hot days.  

Niece Lumber helped us pick out Lincoln Windows, a family run U.S.-manufactured brand with two advantages. First, we could get retractable screens which we’re very excited about. And second, they offer custom colors on their exteriors. You’ll have to stay tuned to figure out what color choice we’ve made. It’s gonna be…dramatic.

Work on the siding and window part of the renovation is starting soon. I’ll be posting updates on my Instagram (@sallykohn), plus more updates along the way here as we get into our kitchen renovations, including the most sustainable and cool countertop and our very locally made and custom color stove. Watch this space!

Match report – Player ratings – Arteta reaction – Video

Arsenal crashed out of the Carabao Cup last night, going down 5-3 on penalties to Fleetwood Town after a 1-1 draw in normal time.

Mikel Arteta made changes, as you’d expect, with starts for young players Marli Salmon, Max Dowman and Ceadach O’Neill, while thus far peripheral senior men like Martin Zubimendi, Cristhian Mosquera and Piero Hincapie also came in for what ought to have been valuable playing time. He also decided, somewhat curiously in my opinion, to start both of his strikers, with Viktor Gyokeres up front and Kai Havertz behind him in midfield.

There was a surreal start to the game when the referee spotted a drone hovering above Highbury Stadium and stopped play. I’m unsure what the big issue was however. Was he worried about player safety, TV rights? Whatever it was, we were stalled for 8 minutes until it was sorted out, not ideal for both sets of players on a night when conditions weren’t exactly clement.

When play began again Arsenal dominated the ball but struggled to test the home side. Hincapie headed a Dowman cross over, before the 16 year old had a clear sight of goal but scuffed his right-footed shot wide. In the middle, Gyokeres looked unhappy as if the ball should have been squared but the shot was the right decision, the execution of it was the issue.

Then, Fleetwood scored. We weren’t competitive enough around the second ball after a long kick from the goalkeeper, they played it forward, and Aaron Loupalo-Bi got the wrong side of Salmon before firing home through the legs of Illan Meslier. It’s not great defending from the 17 year old, but it’s one of those moments as a young player that are inevitable and that ultimately you learn from. He was superb against Ipswich in the last round, and given how badly we fared further forward with more experienced players, I don’t think we should give this much more than a passing mention.

Decent balls in from Dowman gave Havertz and Timber headed chances which they spurned before the break, and that was the extent of our attacking play in that first period. So, it was no surprise when the manager made changes at the break with Ben White and Martin Odegaard replacing Salmon and Timber. It meant a bit of a reshuffle, and early in the second half we found an equaliser.

O’Neill, who looked lively on the left, played a decent ball in from that side, Fleetwood’s defenders didn’t deal with it, Gyokeres – as he does too often – fell over, but on his way down touched it beyond the keeper for 1-1. And that should have been the point at which the game changed in our favour, but what we produced subsequent to the goal was just dismal. Odegaard made us a little bit better, but around him few others stepped up.

Havertz was abysmal missing a great chance after Odegaard’s clever pass put him through, and Gyokeres completing 5 of 9 passes all night against League Two opposition illustrates the limitations of his game in stark fashion. When we need a focal point up front, he just cannot provide it. Eberechi Eze came on and made little difference, and while perhaps there’s some mitigation for Martin Zubimendi playing with a broken nose, he completely failed to impose himself on the game in any meaningful way for a player of his calibre. And then everything was compounded by the red card.

After a bit of a scramble near the touchline there was a strong tackle on Dowman, one which took ball but also slid through on the man. He took exception to it, there was a very slight retaliatory movement with his leg which sparked a bit of a melee (Ben White was right on the scene to protect his teammate), and the referee adjudged it sufficient to send Dowman off. I think it’s very, very harsh.

I don’t think the challenge from the Fleetwood player is quite as bad as some make out, but I can understand why Max reacted. He shouldn’t have done that, but you can’t send a player off for that. It wasn’t a kick, it wasn’t violent conduct, and I think an experienced referee should have been able to sort that out with a couple of yellow cards. Whether Arsenal will appeal the severity of a three match ban remains to be seen, but I think they have a good case to do so if they choose.

What I really didn’t like, however, was Arteta’s reaction as Dowman went off. He clearly gave him an earful, and said afterwards, “Something that we discussed already before the match.”

A little earlier there had been a confrontation between Odegaard and a Fleetwood player, and I think we can all understand the manager warning his players not to rise to any kind of bait. Nevertheless, Max Dowman is 16, and the manager’s job is to protect him. It doesn’t mean he has to be mollycoddled, it doesn’t mean he shouldn’t face the internal consequences of being shown a red card, but that should have played out in the dressing room, not on the touchline and on live TV. Arteta has done incredible work at this football club, but he’s not infallible, and in my opinion he got this one really wrong – regardless of how frustrated he might be with the reaction from Dowman.

There was a late flurry from Arsenal as we looked to avoid penalties. The keeper made a very good save from Ben White, Odegaard had a shot which required another stop, and just before the end there was a really presentable chance for Havertz from an Eze cross which he headed tamely wide with no conviction. A moment which summed his own performance, and that of the team. Feeble.

So then penalties. Fleetwood made no mistake with five solid spot kicks, we made one through Bruno Guimaraes, and that was that. A massive win for the home side, and fair play to them for it. It’s a night that will live long in the history of that club, and this is what cup football can throw up. It’s not the first time we’ve been the giant killed by a lower league side, but I really don’t think we can have any complaints because of how poorly we played overall. Congrats to them.

Afterwards, Arteta said:

First of all, congratulations to Fleetwood for going through the next round. The manner that they have done it, and for us a very sad day. There is nothing to criticise the players for their attitude, their application. The determination – you could see that was there from the start.

The team keeps trying and trying with the ball with chances, shots and especially shots that we had not to score many more goals. Then you don’t capitalise, then you cannot go through to the next round. Unfortunately, we are out.

I will say I think that’s a very generous assessment of our performance. Protective almost, in a way the comments about Dowman were not. There’s a big difference between having a lot of the ball and making that possession count, and while nights like this can happen, upsets are part of football, too many players out there last night let themselves down. Ahead of a big game at Anfield at the weekend, it’s not ideal, because some of them are going to have to play, and if they’re anything like they were last night, we’ll be in trouble.
