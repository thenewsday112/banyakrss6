Inside the New D.C. Restaurant That Only Sells Five Curry Steak Frites Every Night

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The hottest new spot in Washington, D.C. focuses on the traditional heat-packed cooking methods of northern Thailand, while still using local ingredients from around the DMV. Alfie’s, from chefs Justin Ahn and Alex McCoy, started as a pop-up in Petworth in 2017 before traveling around the world, serving diners in New York and London. This year, the restaurant put down permanent roots in a Georgetown alleyway. The co-owners led us through a day in the new kitchen, preparing everything from hand-cut green papaya in som tam salad to the limited-quantity Thai steak frites with a massaman curry peppercorn sauce.

In the video, the kitchen team breaks down rib-eye steaks for their take on a steak au poivre, which they only make five of each night. The steaks are carefully seared for each order, with the au poivre massaman curry sauce is finished in the pan with the leftover beef fat. Classic “bar fries” are served up with the massive 26-ounce rib-eye.

Elsewhere in the kitchen, another chef carves green papayas. The irregular slices of papaya get tossed in a funky, spicy dressing for each order. Ko calls it the “most labor intensive dish on the line;” each order is carefully mixed with fresh tomatoes, peanuts, and a palm syrup and tamarind-heavy dressing. A rich pork belly curry is simmering in the background. Gaeng hung lay (a traditional northern Thai curry) cooks down large pieces of pork belly to get that fatty layer, with pickled garlic and pineapple balancing out the rich flavors of curry paste, tamarind, and fish sauce.

Right before service, the chefs begin prepping herbs and sticky rice that has been soaking overnight and is quickly steamed for that malleable, glutinous texture. McCoy talks about how being a chef is more of a lifestyle than a job, but there’s immense payoff when you get to see diners enjoy your hard work every night. He hopes that Alfie’s will become a place where “you can just forget where you are, have a good time, and just lose yourself in the experience and the food.”

Everyone loves a vacation. But the cleanup and tidying as the trip is finishing up? Not so much.

It’s not always clear what guests are responsible for cleaning. Dishes, sheets, towels, and taking out the trash are sometimes part of checkout instructions at Airbnb rentals. With checkout typically at 11 a.m., the last morning of a trip can turn into a mad dash through an unreasonably long chore list, even when a cleaning fee has been tacked onto the bill.

That frustration, along with a growing number of travelers returning to hotels for more consistent quality and fees, is behind Airbnb’s new checkout rules. Learn which tasks guests may still face, and how hosts are responding.

What Airbnb’s New Checkout Rules Mean for Guests and Cleaning Requirements

Starting with reservations booked on or after November 20, 2026, Airbnb will require hosts to clearly explain their house rules and checkout requests before guests book. Hosts won’t be allowed to add new requirements through messages or signs inside the rental after a reservation is confirmed.

“House Rules are now displayed more prominently on your listing, where guests can easily see them before they book,” according to Airbnb’s announcement. Guests can also find these instructions in their Trips tab and reservation reminders.

Under the new policy, hosts generally can’t require guests to strip beds, start laundry, wash and put away dishes, or take trash to an off-site disposal facility. However, hosts can still request simpler tasks, such as placing dirty dishes in the sink, gathering used towels, or putting trash and recycling in designated bins. Guests may also be asked to turn off lights and return keys before leaving.

Airbnb’s policy distinguishes between house rules and requests. “Guests must follow hosts’ house rules, and are encouraged to comply with hosts’ requests,” according to the company. Some exceptions may apply. For example, rentals in areas with bears may require guests to use animal-proof trash containers, while drought restrictions could affect water use.

Guests are still responsible for leaving the property reasonably tidy. Airbnb’s ground rules for guests prohibit excessive messes and require guests to take responsibility for damage beyond normal wear and tear. Hosts can still charge standard cleaning fees and request reimbursement for unexpected cleaning costs or damage.

What Happens When Airbnb Hosts Violate the New Checkout Rules?

Hosts who don’t follow Airbnb’s new checkout rules could receive warnings or requests to update their listings. Repeated violations may lead to notices on their listings or lower placement in search results. Hosts can also appeal flagged requests.

The policy also prevents hosts from charging their own fines for missed chores or restricting access to amenities already included in a reservation.

However, not every host supports the changes. Some believe washing dishes after cooking is a reasonable responsibility for guests, while others worry the new rules could increase cleaning costs. A Change.org petition has called on Airbnb to reverse its restrictions on requiring guests to wash dishes.

Guests who encounter unexpected chores or requests that violate the new policy can contact Airbnb for help.

Geoscientists and engineers rely on reservoir models to understand the subsurface and guide drilling decisions, often under intense time pressure. Yet the current modeling infrastructure that many companies rely on for these critical decisions was designed for smaller datasets, simpler workflows, and siloed operations. As subsurface data grows in scale and complexity, teams need a faster, more connected way to transform uncertainty into actionable decisions.

Now generally available, agile reservoir modeling (ARM) from SLB, integrated with Azure Data Manager for Energy (ADME), is co-engineered for cloud scale to close that gap. The combined solution connects ensemble-centric reservoir modeling and simulation with a governed data foundation, elastic Azure high-performance computing, and scalable storage designed for the throughput and concurrency of large ensemble studies.

Scale ensemble modeling with ARM on Azure

A single reservoir model represents one plausible interpretation of the subsurface. Ensemble modeling evaluates hundreds of model realizations together, giving asset teams a broader view of potential outcomes and a stronger basis for understanding uncertainty and risk.

The industry has long recognized the value of ensemble modeling for risk-aware decision-making, but adoption has been constrained by infrastructure and software designed for smaller, sequential studies. Moving from a limited model set to hundreds of realizations multiplies the compute, storage, data movement, and results that must be managed. ARM addresses this barrier by distributing realization generation and simulation across elastic Azure high-performance computing, while scalable storage provides the capacity, throughput, and parallel access required by ensembles of different sizes.

Connect reservoir workflows to a governed data foundation

Building and updating reservoir models draws on many systems: seismic, wellbore, interpretation, and simulation data, plus the applications and compute that process them. When these pieces are fragmented, customers must connect and orchestrate them on their own, and large-scale simulation becomes a disconnected, one-off exercise.

SLB and Microsoft combined their respective technologies, ARM engineered by SLB and ADME engineered by Microsoft, to remove that burden. Rather than a set of loosely connected tools, customers get one integrated workflow in which each component has a clear role:

  • Azure Data Manager for Energy is the governed data foundation. OSDU® Technical Standard-aligned services and OSDU Domain Data Management Services (DDMS) manage seismic, wellbore, interpretation, and simulation data.
  • Petrel™ subsurface software is where engineers define interpretation and uncertainty workflows, connected directly to data in ADME.
  • Agile reservoir modeling is the ensemble workflow engine. It orchestrates the generation of reservoir realizations and simulation runs and supports discovery and analysis at enterprise scale.

As a result, teams build ensembles from trusted corporate data and publish results back for discovery and reuse, which reduces data movement and improves collaboration across asset teams.

Put ensemble modeling into practice with a connected workflow

Beyond integration and scale, ARM distinguishes itself by orchestrating all stages of an ensemble study across modeling, simulation, data management, and analysis. The workflow tightly connects the tools reservoir teams already use with Azure-scale infrastructure and the governed data services provided by Azure Data Manager for Energy.

I went looking for the source of one little statistic. That was easier said than done.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apparently, 75% of your emotions are generated by smell.

Or that’s what the internet says, anyway. The statistic appears everywhere from marketing books and articles to academic papers about the power of scent. As a perfumer (someone who creates fragrances) and journalist who writes about these things, I came across it several times, and when I finally wanted to cite it for an article about the impact of scent on mood and memory, I went looking for the original research.

But I couldn’t find it.

This wasn’t a totally new experience for me. One time, while working on a workplace story as the editor of a hospitality trade magazine, I couldn’t find the source of an employee retention statistic, so I wrote the people who included it in a study. They wrote back to say they had no idea what I was talking about.

In this case, there was no such dead end. I followed link after link, figuring surely I would get to the bottom of it, but every source pointed to another source, which pointed to another, and somehow none of them led to the original research I was looking for.

I asked ChatGPT, Google Gemini and Claude for help. They gave me links to wellness blogs, aromatherapy companies, and authoritative-looking PDF reports. But as I ruled each one out as the original source, the answers became more circular, and the trail ate itself.

Me: Where did the statistic that 75% of emotions are generated by smell come from?

Google Gemini The Sense of Smell Institute.

Me: Great. Which study?

Gemini: There isn’t a specific study. The statistic was popularized through scent marketing research.

Me: So where did the number come from?

Gemini: Martin Lindstrom’s 2005 book Brand Sense.

Me: Does the book contain the original research?

Gemini: Lindstrom commissioned extensive research into sensory branding through the research firm Millward Brown.

Me: Does that research establish that 75 percent of emotions are generated by smell?

Gemini: Would you like to know how lavender and citrus can influence your mood?

A breakthrough! Or so I thought

Lindstrom did look like a real lead. Several writers and businesses traced the number to the Danish author’s 2005 book Brand Sense: Sensory Secrets Behind the Stuff We Buy. Simon & Schuster’s page for the book says it outright: “Research shows that a full 75% of our emotions are in fact generated by what we smell.” Aha!

I bought the book and read it. While there is talk of emotions and the senses, the number isn’t in there.

Next, I wrote to Lindstrom’s company asking them to point me toward the original research. They sent me a link to a copy of Brand Sense and told me I would find the research in the book.

I tried another route. Brand Sense was based in large part on an international study by market research company Millward Brown. The 75% figure has been attributed directly to that research. So I went looking for the Millward Brown study. But I couldn’t find it anywhere.

Does 75% even make sense?

There were other questions: Does the stat even make scientific sense? How would you measure the percentage of emotions generated by smell? Do people with no sense of smell experience 75% fewer emotions? And what about emotions that seem to have nothing to do with the senses at all, like grief over someone’s death, a breakup, or anxiety over losing your job?

I asked Kristen Lindquist, a social and affective neuroscientist and psychology professor at Ohio State University who studies emotion. She had never heard of the statistic.

“I imagine if somebody had done that study I would be aware of it,” she told me.

There is a relationship between smell and emotion, and it may be possible to measure something like this, she said. Researchers could, for example, ask people throughout the day how they’re feeling and whether what they’re feeling is related to something they’re seeing, hearing, touching or smelling. But that still wouldn’t make emotion the product of one sense. “Emotions really arise from all of our sensations,” Lindquist said, including signals from within our bodies. Our brains process all of that information together to figure out what is happening and what it means for us.

So, what about that breakup?

“If you get dumped and you smell some nice cookies, are you going to feel 75% better?” Lindquist said. “Probably not.”

When repetition becomes evidence

There’s a name for the particular way an unsupported claim can acquire the appearance of fact through citation: citogenesis. Coined by Randall Munroe in an XKCD comic, the term describes the process by which an unsupported claim gets repeatedly cited until the citations themselves make it look like truth.

I called Jodi Schneider, an associate professor at the University of Wisconsin-Madison Information School whose research includes scientific evidence and citation integrity, to talk about it. I sent her the 75% statistic, and she went looking for the source herself.

“It was not a casual ‘Let me look for 20 minutes, and there it is,'” she told me. “I would find a thing, [it] would go to something else, and that would be a dead end.”

Her conclusion: “It’s hard to source.”

The problem, Schneider said, is that repeating a claim is much easier than investigating it. “Somebody says something, it sounds like a thing you’d like to know or repeat, and you don’t trace it back to where it came from. You just pass it on.” Then repetition becomes the “evidence,” and “if you hear the same thing from multiple places, it’s harder to question.”

This psychological phenomenon is known as the illusory truth effect: We’re more likely to believe information we’ve encountered before. The effect was first demonstrated in the 1970s and has since been replicated many times. More unsettlingly, research has found that repetition can make a false claim seem more believable even when it contradicts something we know to be true.

In other words, seeing the same statistic everywhere doesn’t just make it look well sourced; it makes it feel true.

It’s hard to fully check every claim. Writers build arguments from dozens of pieces of information, and tracing each one all the way back to its origin can take an absurd amount of time.

Newsrooms have spent years cutting staff and budgets, including the fact-checkers and copy editors whose job was to catch this kind of thing. At the same time, the volume of information journalists have to sort through has exploded. Finding a source for a claim can take seconds. Establishing where it actually came from can take hours (or an eternity).

Meanwhile, the consequences of not checking facts compound. Once a bad claim gets published, it becomes another source for the next journalist, another search result for the next reader and another apparent confirmation that the claim must be true.

Then came AI

AI is making that problem worse. It repeats the same unsupported claims while stripping away some of the clues that might otherwise make us suspicious of the source.

“The polish that generative AI can put on just about anything is already a challenge,” Schneider said. More importantly, “Generative AI is really, really good at being persuasive, even if the claims inside the persuasion have no basis in reality.”

Most people are not going to track a statistic through six articles, buy the book everyone cites, email the author, and start calling academics. They don’t have the time or energy for that, and they shouldn’t have to do it anyway. One of journalism’s roles is to provide information that’s more reliable than TikTok or whatever nonsense Google Gemini spits out.

When we get it wrong, we don’t just add another bad source to the pile. We give the claim credibility and put it in front of more people. And once they’ve seen it, the illusory truth effect suggests they’re more likely to believe it the next time. (Research has also found that repeated misinformation is more likely to be repeated than new misinformation, and can continue to influence people even after it has been corrected.)

Nobody is going to die because they believe smell generates 75 percent of their emotions. But replace that claim with one about a vaccine, a war, an election or a medical treatment and the same failure becomes considerably less benign.

That’s why the difference between finding a source and finding the source matters. I found hundreds of sources for the 75% statistic. I still haven’t found the source. That doesn’t mean it’s not out there.

I just can’t find it.

Elizabeth Bromstein is a writer, editor, and perfumer.

Can You Learn to Feel More Loved?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Imagine you have a colleague who remembers that you had a difficult appointment and checks in on you. Or a friend who is just there to listen without telling you what you should have done differently. We don’t tend to think of these experiences as grand declarations of love, but they leave us with a certain lingering warmth: the feeling of being loved.

In our research, we coined the term felt love to focus on the receiver’s side of love. This emphasis shifts attention away from the expressions of love (i.e., loving signals). While expressions are actions we choose to take, felt love is an internal, subjective sensation that washes over us. However, how strongly it does so also depends on us: The same act of care may pass unnoticed or land as love, based on the receiver’s cognitive beliefs or state of mind. Many experiences may elicit felt love, and they occur not only in romantic settings, but when we share time with friends, receive supportive gestures from family, or even get unexpected help from strangers. We are only just beginning to uncover how people come to feel loved and whether that experience can be changed—if it can be learned, like a skill.

In our research on shared beliefs about love, we asked 495 people representative of the U.S. population to judge situations that would make most people feel loved. What they ranked most love-eliciting was being shown compassion in difficult times—an act that, in principle, any person could (and should) offer. Pets feeling happy to see them ranked highly for eliciting the feeling of being loved. Clearly, love, as people live it day to day, reaches past courtship and partnership.

Work like this partly drives how we measure who feels loved and when. It is common for studies to measure the experience of love as if it were a stable trait—for example, romantic attachment as a demographic indicator—but this practice obscures fundamental aspects of the lived human story. Relationship status may describe part of someone’s circumstances, but it does not tell us whether that person feels loved in their daily experience. For example, many people experience love while unattached, and many do not experience love despite being in a long-term relationship. In fact, in our studies, strong experiences of felt love are reported by participants who identify as unattached. To study felt love, we also have to ask about the experience directly.

Tracking felt love in real time in real life

Subjective states like felt love are inherently volatile, so studying it requires many careful observations, ideally while people are going about their daily lives. We often rely on complex research methods that focus not only on how people feel at a given time, but on the patterns of change over time and how those are shaped by our environment and affected by others.
 
Our basic approach is simple in principle: Ask people how loved they feel at different times while they go about their lives. In two studies published in 2020, in which over 200 participants reported their felt love states a handful of times per day for several weeks, we found that people with higher levels of felt love also tended to report greater flourishing and emotional well-being. 

Frequent, repeated observations also allow us to disentangle a person’s typical experience from day-to-day fluctuations. This can also reveal individual differences in how persistent daily felt love states are—a feeling can be strong and brief, or weaker but sustained. Persistence is not automatically desirable: A below-usual feeling of being loved can persist, too. Whether particular patterns directly affect well-being remains an important open question.

To study the role of persistence, we developed custom mathematical models that include love inertia—that is, how long that warm feeling stays with us. In our recently published study, 52 participants reported how loved they felt as well as how much love they had expressed over a period of 28 days. We found that people whose felt love states were more persistent also enjoyed better mental health and indicators of well-being. Even more interestingly, the more people expressed love to others in their daily lives, the more they themselves tended to feel loved in return. Expressing love appears to be a gift that keeps on giving: Showing love is associated with a lasting sense of warmth and better well-being, and it also spreads love around you.

This finding suggests an actionable insight: Expressing love could be one route to feeling loved. If you are feeling a lack of connection, proactively expressing selfless care for someone else might help jumpstart your own feelings of being loved. We don’t yet know what accounts for this association. Expressing love might invite a warm response, change how you interpret a relationship, or be part of an interaction that lifts both people up.

Can we learn to feel more loved?

Can an interaction actually change how loved someone feels? A 2024 study with 218 parent–child pairs argues just that. The authors of that study randomly assigned parents and their children, aged eight to 13, to one of two nine-minute conversations. In one condition, they took turns answering questions that encouraged personal disclosure (for example, instead of a surface-level question like “How was school?,” they might ask, “What is something you worry about?”). In the other condition, they made small talk. Children in the disclosure condition reported feeling more loved during the conversation.

This result cannot readily be explained by children in warmer families simply choosing more personal conversations—assignment of children to conditions was random. The researchers changed the conversation and observed a direct difference in the children’s experience.

Such findings need to be independently replicated for reliability and generalizability, but this is an important and promising step: A straightforward intervention changed an experience of feeling loved. The next question we can work on is whether changes of this kind can become part of daily life.

Our focus on the receiver side of love brings a slightly more complex perspective: that an act does not produce a loving feeling by itself, but that effect is moderated by interpretation and expectation—thoughts that come to us in the moment, cognitive beliefs that support those thoughts, and the justifications we have for those beliefs. A colleague’s quiet help can be experienced as love, or dismissed as routine professional courtesy.

An area that is not yet well-explored is how the reception of expressions of love can be taught. If feelings follow perceptions, and perceptions follow beliefs, then felt love is a cognitive phenomenon in addition to an emotional one, so that cognitive skills contribute to how we experience the social world. It may be possible for people to learn to notice when they reflexively dismiss acts of care, to check their interpretation against what actually happened, and to re-evaluate in order to let the sender’s kindness count. It is also possible that a deficit in this cognitive skill prohibits many people from benefiting from the ambient love that is already present in everyday life. Even so, biased perceptions can have positive effects in this context. People with a stronger general tendency to interpret daily life experiences as expressions of love feel more loved in general, with all the well-being benefits that brings.

Where the science goes from here

We now have ways to measure felt love in daily life, evidence linking it to well-being, and at least one experiment that changed it in the moment. A sensible next step is to connect those findings.

Can an intervention change daily feelings of love for weeks or months? Does that change lead to better well-being? Does it work for people who start out feeling relatively unloved, or mainly for those already doing well? Those are different questions, all embedded in the vastly complex network of our daily environments and experiences—part of what is sometimes called the exposome—and they require different, carefully drawn comparisons.

Future trials could focus on the cognitive component of felt love—whether an underlying ability to appreciate acts of love extends to other facets of life, and whether interventions should target the individual’s reasoning before focusing on their social context. Repeated measurements in daily-life settings could be used to show whether any differences generated by the intervention survive after the study session. Such trials would need to be designed with a great deal of care to establish the mechanisms of causality—a standard challenge of daily-life social psychology studies, but an especially critical one here. Establishing how to make experiences of felt love more available is an expansive but worthwhile scientific endeavor.

Finally, an emerging focus on love also recognizes how it is culturally and contextually bound. While certain loving gestures are found all over the world (such as helping someone in need, or empathic attention), to foster intercultural understanding, empathy, and inclusivity in diverse societies, it is vital to recognize love’s cultural variations in everyday life. We are currently conducting global cross-cultural research to identify specific ways love is shown in everyday life across different types of relationships, beyond just the romantic.

Do try this at home

While we and other researchers continue to conduct larger studies to unpack these exact mechanisms, we don’t have to wait for the data to start paying closer attention to the quiet moments in our own lives. What happens when you feel genuinely cared for? What happens when you proactively express love to someone else? Being mindful of how they respond can reveal something that checking a box to count affectionate gestures misses. Fostering these experiences doesn’t require a laboratory—it is a daily practice we can all begin right now. Showing love is a low-cost experiment we can all conduct in our own daily lives.

AWS Weekly Roundup: Amazon Polly Generative engine, TorchNeuron for Trainium, and more (October 26, 2026)

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A few weeks ago I presented to a small group of commercial and technology leaders from Brazilian agribusiness at the AWS office in São Paulo. My talk ran through where generative AI fits across the different stages of an agribusiness operation, and the point I kept returning to is that the markets we call traditional are exactly the ones where keeping up with this technology decides who ends up ahead of the curve. Nobody there was arguing about whether AI belongs in agriculture. The questions were about delivery: who receives the output, in which language, on which device, often while driving between farms. That is why a launch I might otherwise have filed under routine leads my list this week. The Amazon Polly Generative engine upgrade added 14 new voices, two of them in Brazilian Portuguese. When your user has both hands on a steering wheel, the interface is a voice.

Presenting “GenAI no Agronegócio Brasileiro” at the AWS office in São Paulo.

The rest of the week had a sharper edge. The biggest launch is a security service built on the premise that the gap between a vulnerability being disclosed and a vulnerability being exploited has effectively closed, and AWS published the honeypot evidence for exactly that in the same week. Now, let’s get into this week’s AWS news…

AWS Continuum for code vulnerabilities is now generally available
AWS Continuum reframes vulnerability management as an end-to-end job rather than a scanner that hands you a list. It builds a context graph from your repositories and your AWS environment, assigns each finding an evidence level, and reproduces exploitable findings in an isolated sandbox with proof-of-concept code so you see the issue rather than a severity label. It ranks issues by how close the attack path runs to business-critical systems, then opens the fix as a pull request. The control boundary matters: every remediation currently requires human approval, so you review and merge the pull request, with autonomous classes of fix gated behind confidence thresholds for the future.

The “why now” came from AWS’s own honeypots in the same week. Amazon Threat Intelligence found a PHP2Shell exploitation attempt in MadPot telemetry dated October 17, four days before CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 were publicly disclosed, and on disclosure day proof-of-concept exploits were circulating around 02:00 UTC, roughly twelve hours before the embargo was due to lift at 14:00 UTC. The practical reading is direct: a patch window measured in days assumes a delay that no longer exists, and an investigation should start from the earliest known exploitation rather than from the disclosure date. AWS also published the AWS WAF mitigations for the same CVEs. Continuum is available in US East (N. Virginia) and US West (Oregon).

Introducing Amazon ECS Gateway
Amazon ECS Gateway splits the work of exposing a service between the people who own the network and the people who own the application. Platform teams define one gateway with the organization’s subnets, security groups, TLS certificates, listener settings, and AWS WAF web ACLs. Application teams opt in by adding a gateway connector to the service definition, and ECS gives them an ACM certificate, a unique DNS hostname, listener rules, a target group, and a security group that admits traffic only from the Application Load Balancer.

The numbers make the economics clear: up to 25 services share a single ALB, ECS provisions the first ALB on first connect and removes ALBs when they are no longer needed, and a gateway can associate with up to 10,000 clusters, with five gateways per cluster. You can adopt it incrementally with UpdateService while your existing load balancer stays in place. Amazon ECS Gateway is available in all commercial Regions where Amazon ECS is available, at no additional charge.

Last week’s launches
Here are some launches that caught my attention this week.

Amazon Polly delivers a major upgrade to its Generative engine – The Generative engine now runs a larger, more expressive catalog: 57 generative voices across 11 languages and 25 locales, with 14 new voices across 9 locales and Hindi and Finnish generally available for the first time. Two of the new voices are Brazilian Portuguese, Leo and Carolina. It is a drop-in upgrade, so the voice names, the same SynthesizeSpeech call, and the price all stay the same while the output gets more natural.

AWS announces TorchNeuron: native PyTorch support for AWS Trainium (public beta) – TorchNeuron is an open source PyTorch backend that lets you run your model on Trainium by switching to .to(‘neuron’), a configuration change instead of a rewrite, with eager execution and torch.compile intact. It integrates with the Neuron Kernel Interface for custom operators, and it works on Trainium2 and Trainium3 instances. If you want the bigger picture on why hardware choice should not lock in your software, the AWS Insights piece on that theme is a good companion read, and the release lands alongside AWS Neuron 2.33.

New ways to customize models with Amazon SageMaker AI, without managing GPUs – Training sessions are now generally available, giving you control of the post-training loop, including custom loss functions and reward signals, while SageMaker AI handles GPU allocation, distributed execution, fault recovery, and checkpoint storage. You pay for the tokens SageMaker AI processes, and the meter stops while your reward function scores a batch. There is a full technical walkthrough, the training session APIs, and new support for Muse Glimmer, Qwen 3.8, and Ministral 3.

Structured outputs for Claude models available on Amazon Bedrock – You can now get schema-compliant JSON back from any Anthropic Claude model on Amazon Bedrock, across both the bedrock-runtime and bedrock-mantle endpoints, without custom parsing, retry logic, or post-processing. That makes jobs like pulling every required field from a financial report, parsing clinical notes, or routing support tickets by intent far more reliable to automate.

Amazon ElastiCache Serverless for Valkey now supports online migration with zero downtime – You can migrate self-managed Valkey or Redis OSS on Amazon EC2 into ElastiCache Serverless with zero downtime, meaning zero errors returned to the client during the move, measured the way the ElastiCache SLA measures availability. You prepare the migration, start the data sync, then choose a manual or automatic cutover, with no third-party replication tools. It is available in all commercial Regions, the China Regions, and the AWS GovCloud (US) Regions.

Other AWS news
Here are some additional projects and posts that you might find interesting.

Discover tools at runtime with AWS Agent Registry – AWS Agent Registry is a managed catalog for MCP servers, agents, and agent skills, so an orchestrator can search approved capabilities at runtime instead of compiling every tool in at build time. This walkthrough shows the discover, instantiate, and execute loop in a working sample, with search results that return connection metadata but never invocation credentials.

Tokenomics for coding agents: benchmark cost and quality on your own code – Coding assistants replay a growing transcript on every turn, so their cost math is nothing like ordinary inference. This post gives you open source tooling to measure model, harness, and hosting choices on your own repositories and build a cost-and-quality frontier you own, with sample results where routing came out 44.7 percent cheaper for 4.8 percent lower mean quality.

How U.S. Bank migrated 119 TB from Oracle Exadata to Amazon RDS for Oracle – U.S. Bank moved four Oracle Exadata databases totaling about 119 TB to Amazon RDS for Oracle using a config-driven pipeline built on Oracle XTTS, AWS DataSync, and Amazon EFS. The initial baseline copy finished in about 7 days with zero data loss and cutovers averaging roughly four hours per database, over a bandwidth-capped Direct Connect link.

Where is the best place to meditate? Here’s how to find your spot

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Psst: there's no “best” place to meditate. Some spots just make it feel more natural. Here's how to find the right location for your next meditation session, indoors or out.

You have a free morning and want to meditate, but you’re not sure where. The living room isn’t private or quiet enough. The lighting in your home office is harsh. In your bedroom, the only place to sit is your bed, and you’re worried you might fall asleep. So instead of practicing mindfulness, you end up weighing the risks of distraction or discomfort.

The good news is, you don't need a Pinterest-worthy space, just a spot that works for you. People practice on train platforms, in parked cars, and on office floors during their lunch break, and they still get the benefits.

Whether you're just getting started or you’ve been meditating for years, this guide will help you find and level up that spot. 

 

What makes a good meditation space?

A good meditation space is one where you feel safe, comfortable, and undisturbed. These four qualities tend to make the biggest difference:

  • Comfort: A supportive cushion, a chair with a good backrest, or even a spot against the wall reduces strain on your body. 

  • Quiet: Minimal noise, foot traffic, or notifications helps you avoid competition for your attention.

  • Calming sensory cues: Soft lamplight, a candle, a diffuser, or a warm blanket can signal it’s time to slow down.

  • Familiarity: The same corner or cushion becomes a cue over time, so your brain starts to wind down as soon as you arrive.

Where is the best place to meditate?

The best place to meditate depends on what you want from your session, and many people rotate between a few spots. Indoors is more predictable, since weather and temperature are rarely a factor. Outdoors, on the other hand, can feel more grounding and restorative.

Indoor meditation spots

When you meditate indoors, you have more control over the temperature, lighting, and setup of the space, which can help you build a consistent habit. 

Your bedroom corner. It's private and may already feel like a place to recharge, so it works well when you want to slow down. 

Your living room. This spot works best when it’s empty, like early morning before anyone's up or once the house has gone quiet for the night.

Your unused nook or closet. With just a cushion and a curtain, almost any small, overlooked area can become your own little sanctuary.

Your bed. Meditating here is a gentle way to start your day or transition into sleep, though the same comfort that makes it appealing can also make you doze off before you're done.

Outdoor meditation spots

The outdoors offers something special: fresh air, the open sky, and nature sounds that help calm your nervous system. Time in green space is linked to lower cortisol and reduced stress. One explanation is the attention restoration theory — the idea that natural settings give your focus a chance to recover.

A backyard or garden. If you have one, some greenery and a comfortable seat can turn it into a peaceful retreat.

A beach or park. Either works especially well near water, where the sound of waves or a stream can ease your mind. 

A balcony or patch of grass. Even a small outdoor space gives you a change of scenery, which can help you relax.

If sitting feels hard, try walking meditation. Focus on the rhythm of your steps to give your mind something to settle on.

How to optimize your meditation space: 5 tips 

A few small, deliberate changes can turn your meditation space into one that genuinely supports your practice.

1. Set up a regular spot 

Store your cushion, blanket, and anything else you use to meditate in your dedicated place. When you return to the same spot consistently, ideally around the same time, settling in can start to feel automatic. 

2. Create a little privacy

Face away from clutter, toward a blank wall or window. In a shared space, a folding screen or blanket draped over a chair can mark off your corner. Even with your eyes closed, feeling tucked away can help you settle.

3. Choose the right sound 

Try playing steady background noise—like soothing nature sounds or music—to cover unpredictable and intrusive sounds like traffic or a neighbor's TV. If you prefer some structure in your practice, you could follow a guided meditation. 

To slow down, experiment with tracks around 60–80 beats per minute, which researchers have linked to a slower heart rate. For daytime focus, many people find that ambient or instrumental sound works best.

4. Minimize distractions 

Put your phone on Do Not Disturb, and tell the people you live with that you'll be unavailable for the length of your session. You could also pick a time when they’re less likely to need you.

5. Use props for good posture

If you need to take the strain off your lower back, sit on a cushion or folded blanket to raise your hips slightly above your knees. You can also meditate near a wall, or place a chair nearby to support your spine if sitting upright gets tiring. 

A meditation cushion can help you hold your position more comfortably. Try keeping the room on the cool side too, since an overly warm setup can nudge you toward sleep.

Where is the best place to meditate? FAQs

Does where I meditate really matter?

Where you meditate shapes how the practice feels once you're there, but consistency is what matters most. A pleasant space can motivate you to start, though it's not required.

What if I don't have a quiet place to meditate?

You can still meditate in a noisy home or busy neighborhood, and plenty of people do. Headphones with a guided session or ambient sound can add a bit of calm almost anywhere, even a parked car or a bathroom. 

You can even let the noise become part of the practice by resting your attention on the sounds themselves, instead of trying to push them away. Over time, that flexibility often makes your practice stronger.

How do I set up a meditation corner in a small space?

You don’t need much to set up a meditation corner. A cushion, a clear patch of floor, and some calming objects are enough. Pick an empty corner and mark it off with a plant, a small rug, or a curtain. 

Can I meditate in bed?

Meditating in bed is fine, and it's a gentle way to wind down at night or start your morning. If you’re worried about dozing off, sit up against the headboard instead of lying down, or try it earlier in the day when you’re more alert.

Is it better to meditate indoors or outdoors?

The better choice depends on what you need that day. Indoors gives you privacy and control, which suits a daily habit, while outdoors adds fresh air and a grounding connection to nature. Plenty of people mix the two.

How do I reduce distractions when meditating?

To reduce distractions when you meditate, start with what you can control: silence your phone and pick a time when interruptions are less likely.

For noises that still come up, treat your breath as your anchor and return to it whenever your mind wanders.

What should a meditation space look like?

Your meditation space doesn't need to look a certain way. It just needs to work for you. Soft light, minimal clutter, and something comfortable to sit on are usually enough. 

Can music or sounds help me meditate?

Music and calming sounds can ease you into your meditation. Soft instrumental tracks, nature sounds, or steady background noise all help mask distracting noises and signal to your body that it’s time to relax. 

As your practice develops, you might find you prefer silence, so it's worth experimenting to see what suits you.
