Shoveling snow can be a dangerous winter chore. Icy walkways can cause serious falls, and prolonged exposure to the cold can lead to hypothermia or frostbite. Another safety concern is something called “heart attack snow.”

The name sounds dramatic, but it points to a real concern for people clearing sidewalks and driveways in winter. For those in snowy climates, understanding the risks of heart attack snow is important before picking up a shovel.

Understanding the risks can help prevent a routine chore from becoming a medical emergency.

Why Is Heavy, Wet Heart Attack Snow Dangerous to Shovel?

Heart attack snow is a nickname for heavy, wet snow that’s harder to shovel than dry, powdery snow. The name comes from the strain that lifting this type of snow can place on the heart. According to the National Weather Service, snow with a higher water content weighs more, making each shovelful harder to lift.

The added effort can quickly raise heart rate and blood pressure. Cold temperatures also cause blood vessels to narrow, making the heart work harder. The American Heart Association warns that this combination can trigger a heart attack or sudden cardiac arrest.

The association says the risks are especially concerning for “people who already have cardiovascular risks like a sedentary lifestyle or obesity,” including those with a history of smoking, diabetes, high cholesterol, or high blood pressure. People who have previously suffered a heart attack or stroke also face greater risks.

Even lighter, powdery snow can be dangerous, especially when large amounts require repeated lifting or hours of clearing.

How to Shovel Snow Safely and Reduce the Risk of a Heart Attack

Before heading outside, people should warm up indoors and stretch gently. Dressing in layers helps protect against the cold, while staying hydrated is important even when someone doesn’t feel thirsty.

The American Heart Association recommends pushing snow rather than lifting it whenever possible. When lifting is necessary, smaller loads and frequent breaks can help limit physical strain. A snow blower can also reduce the amount of lifting, although operating one still requires effort.

Slippery surfaces are another concern. “Walking on ice is extremely dangerous,” according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The agency recommends using rock salt or another de-icing product to keep walkways clear of ice. Sand can also help prevent slipping.

People with heart disease or significant cardiovascular risk factors should have someone else handle snow removal, according to the American Heart Association.

Recognizing the warning signs of a heart attack is equally important. Chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath, dizziness, nausea, cold sweats, or discomfort in the arms, back, or jaw can all signal a serious problem. Symptoms aren’t always severe and can vary from person to person.

Anyone experiencing possible heart attack symptoms should stop shoveling immediately and call 911. The American Heart Association advises against waiting to see whether symptoms go away.

Wolf Creek Ski Area Will Become Third Open Colorado Resort on Halloween

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

A southern Colorado ski resort is hot on the heels of the Summit County ski areas that scrambled to be Colorado’s first to open earlier this week.

Wolf Creek Ski Area in Colorado’s San Juan Mountain range will open for the season on Saturday, October 31, 2026. 

Treasure Stoke, Bonanza, and Nova lifts will operate from 9 am until 4 pm on Halloween and through Tuesday, November 3. Wolf Creek will close again Wednesday and Thursday, November 4 & 5. The resort plans to operate Fridays through Sundays until there’s significantly more snow.

With 22″ of snow to date, and 20″ falling over the last week alone, including 12″ of fairly wet snow that has made for an excellent base, Wolf Creek plans to open a mix of groomed runs and off-piste terrain.

A full list of runs that will be open can be found on Wolf Creek’s website. In total, roughly 30% of the mountain’s terrain will be open for skiing—an impressive stat given the time of year. Skiers should be prepared for variable conditions, ski with extra caution, and be prepared for unmarked obstacles and possibly open water.

View this post on Instagram

Wolf Creek’s Ski & Snowboard school, rentals, and Treasure Sports shop will all open this weekend, as will food and beverage services across the mountain. The Main Day Lodge and Upper Lodge will serve a homemade menu including burgers, fries, chili, soups, and a salad bar. Pathfinder Bar will have a full drink menu available too.

To kick off the season, Wolf Creek will have Local Appreciation lift ticket rates. Lift tickets will cost $73 for adults, $55 for seniors, and $36 for children.

Super El Niño predictions have indicated that ski areas in the Southwestern US, like Wolf Creek, could be in for a good season, and a Halloween opening day certainly bodes well for that. Recent updates from the Climate Prediction Center also say that El Niño is forecasted to peak between October and December, but could last until early spring of 2027.

Italian Police Used AI to Identify $200 M. Worth of Stolen Art and Antiquities

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In the fall of 2024, Italian police used AI to analyze photographs taken at a popular antique fair, identifying a particular oil painting as having been one stolen from a church near L’Aquila 20 years prior.

The Carabinieri Command for the Protection of Cultural Heritage, Italy’s art crime squad, has been using an AI algorithm to identify stolen artworks and artifacts since 2023. The platform they use is called SWOADS—or Stolen Works of Art Detection System—and was developed by Salvatore Rapicavoli, an IT engineer who joined the art crime team in 2023.

A BBC article detailed the process, which began with Italian police officers visiting Mercanteinfiera, a 40-year-old “antiques village” that attracts exhibitors from all over Europe, in October 2024. When the officers uploaded their photographs into the art crime database, Swoads flagged a particular image of a young woman holding a scroll. The oil painting was a close—though, to the human eye, not an exact—match to a 17th-century painting of Saint Agnese that had been taken from Virgo Potens, a small church in Sulmona, in the Abruzzo region. At some point over the past two decades, the lamb Sant Agnese was cradling in the original had been painted over with a scroll inscribed with Latin text. Swoads, however, was able to identify “hidden visual fingerprints in the works—pairing similarities between shades of colour and rough silhouettes.”

Rapicavoli’s goal of using technology to solve art crime problems has been a success. Swoads has recovered more than 300 large paintings and statues, and more than 165,000 small objects like coins, letters, and medals, according to the BBC. All told, these objects are worth over $200 million. As for the portrait of Saint Agnese, it was returned to the church from where it was stolen. The thieves have yet to be apprehended.
