An authentic wheel of Grana Padano carries a signature brand burned into its rind—so you know that if you see a diamond pattern and the cheese’s name, it must have been produced in the Valle Padana (Po River Valley) of Northern Italy according to strict rules with roots in a 12th-century Cistercian monastery. At the time, the region surrounding the city of Milan was primarily soggy marshland. The monks who built the monastery—Chiaravalle Abbey—were followers of the Rule of Saint Benedict, which, along with devotion to prayer, mandated that they practice agriculture with a focus on responsible, sustainable use of land. They first figured out how to turn the marshy landscape into arable fields so they could grow high-quality feed for dairy cattle. Since milk (and fresh cheese) didn’t last long, the next challenge for the monks was how to preserve this nutritious, valuable food source to nourish themselves and their community.

The method they developed—heating the milk in copper cauldrons, adding rennet (a coagulating enzyme), cutting the curds into tiny pieces, and molding, salting, and aging the cheese—remains largely unchanged today. By producing the first iteration of what is now known around the world as Grana Padano, the monks of Chiaravalle Abbey earned the distinction of operating the world’s first cheese dairy.

Easy to like, portable, and a good source of protein, the cheese became an emblematic food, enjoyed by Italians at all levels of society through the Renaissance and into the modern era. Its iconic name, meanwhile, developed over time. The monks called it “caseus vetus”—Latin for “old cheese”—but since the peasants didn’t know Latin, they referred to it as “grana” for its distinctive grainy texture. As production spread well beyond the monastery, each province had its own grana: Cheese made in Milan was “grana Milanese,” and “grana Mantovano” was from Mantua.

It wasn’t until 1954 that the Italian government codified the name Grana Padano to unite the different varieties of grana cheese under a single wider geographical indication and that the Grana Padano Protection Consortium was established. In 1996, Grana Padano was awarded the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) designation. Today the consortium incorporates 134 member dairies, who together produced over 6 million wheels of cheese in 2025. In addition to safeguarding the name, the organization is also responsible for the global promotion of Grana Padano PDO and governs every detail of production to ensure that each wheel meets the strict standards established under the PDO. 

The specifications start with the cows’ diet: All fresh forage and at least 75 percent of dry fodder (such as hay) must be grown within the cheese production area. Raw milk must be collected within 24 hours of milking and be partially skimmed, allowing for natural separation of the cream. After a natural whey starter is introduced to the copper (or copper-lined) milk vat, calves’ rennet is added for coagulation. The cheese is then broken into rice-size curds using a spino, which resembles a large whisk, and packed into round molds, where it sits for at least 36 hours. The wheels are immersed in brine for anywhere from 14 days to a month and aged for at least nine months and up to 24 months or longer. Wheels aged over 20 months can be labeled Riserva.

Bite into a chunk of Grana Padano PDO, and you taste the sweetness of fresh milk coupled with a savory hint of toasted nuts. Riservas may be flakier and more crystalline in texture than younger versions of the cheese but never dry or crumbly, and they must still have a delicate flavor. Grana Padano PDO is an exceptionally versatile cheese, equally delicious on a cheese board, wrapped with a slice of Prosciutto di Parma PDO and drizzled with aged balsamic for a sophisticated appetizer, as a filling for ravioli, or grated over soup or pasta.

It’s good for you, too. Lactose- and gluten-free, Grana Padano PDO is low in fat and an excellent source of protein, calcium, and vitamin B12. A 30-gram piece (just over one ounce) has as much nutrition as a pint of milk, making it a beneficial as well as tasty solution to the “afternoon slump” for both kids and adults. Whoever you share Grana Padano PDO with, be sure to tell them what they have in common with Italians from more than 1,000 years ago—that they’re savoring a taste of European culinary history.

Nate Bargatze Is (Sort Of) Leading By Example

It’s a bit hard to believe that Nate Bargatze, one of the biggest comedians today, met his wife while working at Applebee’s in Tennessee. Or that on his first job overseeing the dog kennel in an amusement park (apparently a thing), he let the dog out (“it was a whole thing … and that was kind of the end of the dog kennel”). Or even the basic fact that at the height of his career he decided to move back to Tennessee so that his teen daughter might have a more normal life. Bargatze’s life, like his comedic work, reads like a folksy tall tale. It draws you in with the details and lulls you with its sense of warmhearted authenticity — then lands a punchline.

But authenticity matters to him, in his storytelling and what he’s learned over the years. He recently brought some of his experience to Ford’s Accelerate conference, where he is a spokesperson for what they’re calling the “essential economy” — of skilled technicians and tradespersons, a group that, according to economists, is in short supply and needs to grow. Bargatze is an interesting choice for this forum, a comedian with a high school degree and no formal trade skills besides those he learned onstage. He went to community college for a year and worked a variety of jobs, from waiting at Applebee’s to reading water meters. But he gets the value and opportunity in trade school. “I relate to it very much, because I’m someone that should’ve went to these trade schools. I did not go to college ... They’re going to really learn something and have a craft and know how to do something that is going to be, I believe, important for just the future.”

Nate Bargatze poses with students at the Ford Accelerate conference, which he mc-ed. | Ford

He also is thinking about this because his 14-year-old is not too far from entering the workforce. “Right now it’s like, yeah, I don’t know if she’s wrapping her head around it. But then, as she continues to get older, she will wrap her head around it. She doesn’t have a sense of urgency of cleaning her room, so you work on that and you just keep saying it and you need to [instill that urgency] as a parent. And if you don’t, your kids watch you. So if you don’t have any kind of sense of urgency, they’re going to pick that up from you.” Which all boils down to a most authentic parenting philosophy: “Be a good example, basically.”

Tell me about what you’re doing right now at this Ford Accelerate event.

What Ford is doing with this event that we’re at right now, where they’re showing kids going into trade schools ... letting kids know that this is kind of the future, that they’re going to have jobs. And it’s like we don’t know where jobs are going with AI and all this stuff, but the trade schools… We need that. I relate to it very much, because I’m someone that should’ve went to these trade schools.

I worked the water meters. I did not go to college. They’re going to get more college than I did. But they’re going to really learn something and have a craft and know how to do something that is going to be, I believe, important for just the future. I think they’re putting themselves in such a great position, probably better than they realize now, but they’re going to, I think, look back and just be like, “Man, that was such a good call.”

I’m wondering, since we’re talking about work, what’s the most important thing you’ve learned about work in your life?

I always go back to when I waited tables at one point and our manager there always talked about a sense of urgency. And that’s always stuck with me for some reason. It was just going to a table and just have a sense of urgency. It goes a long way when you get to that table and they really feel like they’re being taken care of. I think I’ve just kept that even as I’ve gone forward. If you respond to emails or my team is talking to you, I don’t want to be like, “I’ll get to them next week.” You kind of want to just be like, “No, let’s get to them and let’s make sure that we stay on top of it.”

Chelsea’s day-to-day sporting leadership will now be shared between Winstanley, Sam Jewell and Joe Shields, each with defined responsibilities.

Winstanley will continue overseeing transfer negotiations, player contracts and several departments at Cobham, including medical and performance operations. Shields’ work centres on the academy and player pathways, while Jewell has wider responsibilities across the BlueCo operation, including Strasbourg.

According to Jacobs, discussions about restructuring began in April and accelerated following the appointment of Xabi Alonso as head coach.

Stewart had overseen investment in Chelsea’s data analysis operation, although Jewell and Shields have increasingly taken responsibility for using that information in recruitment and talent identification.

Dave Fallows will remain involved in an advisory capacity, rather than taking on a fixed day-to-day portfolio.

The changes are being described as a natural evolution of Chelsea’s sporting model. There is no suggestion that Stewart’s departure followed a breakdown in relations, and the club are not currently planning to recruit a replacement.

#756: How Losing $500K in ‘08 Led One Trader to a Different Kind of Portfolio, with Jared Dillian

📅 Oct 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On the morning of the Lehman Brothers collapse, Jared Dillian, who ran the firm’s ETF trading desk, walked past TV trucks and a crowd of reporters to get to the trading floor. He looked around at the chaos, took off his tie, and figured he wouldn’t be needing it anymore.

That week, about half a million dollars of his restricted Lehman stock went to zero. Some of his colleagues, who had loaded up on discounted shares through the company’s stock purchase plan, lost roughly 80% of their net worth.

Jared came through the Lehman Brothers collapse in a different position. He had lived far below his means, with a cheap house, a small mortgage, and no vacations, so while colleagues grabbed whatever job they could find, he had the cash to walk away and start his own newsletter.

In this episode, he explains the lessons from the Lehman Brothers collapse that still shape how he invests. He covers why he treats cash as an option rather than a drag on returns and how a simple five-way split across stocks, bonds, cash, gold, and real estate has historically cut volatility roughly in half.

He also shares a Las Vegas test for finding your risk tolerance, explains why he thinks buy-and-hold investors haven’t faced a real bear market since 2008, and gives his outlook on bond yields, inflation, and Social Security.

Key Takeaways

The biggest ideas from the conversation

+
  • Cash gives you the freedom to bet on yourself: Jared’s coworkers earned the same Wall Street paychecks, but many spent them on pricey apartments and weekend golf. Because he lived cheaply, Jared came out of the Lehman Brothers collapse with enough liquidity to skip the job he was offered and start his own newsletter instead.
  • A smoother ride beats the highest return: Jared focuses on risk-adjusted returns rather than raw returns, because the S&P 500 fell 57% from 2007 to 2009 and investors who panic-sell near the bottom lock in that loss. A portfolio built to reduce volatility makes it easier to stay invested when markets drop.
  • Split your portfolio five equal ways: Jared’s Awesome Portfolio holds 20% each in stocks, bonds, cash, gold, and real estate, rebalanced once a year. In backtests going back to 1971, it has returned about 9% annually, a point or two below the S&P 500, with roughly half the volatility and a worst year of −11.8%.
  • Test your risk tolerance at the craps table: Jared’s gut check is to go to Vegas and notice how much you’re willing to lose before the pain makes you walk away. Despite a career trading on Wall Street, his limit is a few hundred dollars, while some friends will put $10,000 on the table.
