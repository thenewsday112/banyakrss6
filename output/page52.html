AI boom hits a wall on Wall Street as OpenAI cuts fundraising target while Meta gets a credit warning over its cash flow, spending and monetization

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The seemingly endless gusher of capital that’s been showered on Silicon Valley during the AI boom may be coming to an end soon as Wall Street pushes back on massive spending plans.

Following a brutal selloff in tech stocks over the past week, OpenAI slashed the target on its latest fundraising round to $20 billion from $30 billion, sources told the Financial Times. That also cut its expected valuation to $1.25 trillion from $1.4 trillion.

The funding round was also upstaged by Anthropic, according to the report, as OpenAI’s arch-rival prepares to go public next month. Ahead of its IPO, Anthropic filed an S-1 on Monday that showed revenue has soared and its cash flow turned positive in the first half of this year.

OpenAI didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.

The timing couldn’t have been worse. Quarterly earnings reports from hyperscalers this week spooked investors. Alphabet saw its cash flow sink deeper into negative territory in the third quarter, with the Google parent also joining Meta and Amazon in charging ahead with massive spending plans. On Thursday, Alphabet fell about 12%, its worst day on record.

To be sure, there were other positive signs as well, in addition to Anthropic’s bullish S-1. Microsoft reported strong cloud growth and stayed cash flow positive, though it said its capital spending will accelerate, while AI chip maker SK Hynix blew away earnings forecasts.

But the narrative had already turned bearish, especially after AI debt fears sent shares and bonds of “neocloud” companies tumbling earlier this month.

At the same time, OpenAI was seeking more capital just as other AI giants tapped financial markets heavily. This past week alone, Elon Musk said SpaceX closed $40 billion in debt financing, Meta announced plans to raise $95 billion in equity, and Microsoft raised $25 billion in a bond sale.

The prospect of hyperscalers continuing to hemorrhage funds was too much for S&P Global, which cut its outlook on Meta to “negative” from “stable” late Friday, citing negative cash flow and uncertainty around capital expenditure ​plans.

The credit ratings agency maintained Meta at AA- but warned ⁠the social media leader’s leverage could top 1.0x as soon as 2027, which is the downgrade threshold for the current rating. That’s up from about 0.1x in 2025 and primarily reflects its strategy of leasing data center capacity rather than from its debt issuance.

Meanwhile, S&P sees Meta’s capex reaching $140 billion this year, topping $225 billion in 2027, hitting $252 billion in 2028, and then flattening out from there. As a result, it expects free operating cash flow to remain negative: -$22 billion this year, -$71 billion in 2027, and -$39 billion in 2028.

Of course, other hyperscalers are spending heavily and running cash deficits, but S&P also said Meta’s ability to monetize its AI investments is more limited than that of its peers.

“While we believe the company intends to monetize these investments via improved advertising tools that support faster ad revenue growth, as well as from the development of new revenue streams stemming from its introduction of agentic apps such as Muse, it is unclear when these revenue streams will generate material contributions that exceed their rising costs over the next few years,” S&P said.

Meta’s rivals, however, are monetizing their data centers by leasing compute capacity, including to frontier model labs and enterprise customers, it added.

In a statement to Fortune, a Meta spokesperson said the company disagrees with S&P’s assessment of its AI monetization capabilities, pointing to “significant product and business opportunities” ahead.

“Meta is already delivering stronger engagement, better recommendations, and greater returns for businesses as a result of AI investments,” the spokesperson continued. “We believe the technology and infrastructure we’re building now will open new revenue streams, create long-term shareholder value, and bring us closer to delivering personal superintelligence to everyone.”

The bearish environment on Wall Street also comes as rising bond yields have sent borrowing costs sharply higher, the political backlash against data centers is expected to help Democrats retake Congress, and the Iran war could re-escalate after midterm elections.

Still, some bullish voices aren’t convinced the AI boom is about to go bust, and even think some retrenchment is a good thing.

According to market veteran Ed Yardeni, the AI investment boom faces financial, physical, and political constraints. If any of these constraints worsen, stocks could dive further, especially considering that AI is now a major driver of corporate earnings and GDP growth, he said in a note this week. 

“However, we view these constraints as healthy for the durability of the AI boom!” he added.

For example, if the supply of capital doesn’t flow as easily, it could lead to more efficient allocation, lowering the risk of overinvestment. Yardeni expects hyperscalers to retain access to financing—thanks to their strong balance sheets—and keep spending.

In his view, it all comes down to the U.S. economy, which he said has performed remarkably well over the past few decades. Even the midterm elections are unlikely to derail it, regardless of which party controls Congress.

“Further AI jitters therefore could set up buying opportunities rather than signal the end of the boom,” he said.

United Airlines CEO Accuses Boeing And Airbus Of Colluding To Avoid Building New Planes

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scott Kirby says Boeing and Airbus have quietly agreed not to develop new aircraft because neither faces enough competition to force its hand. I understand his frustration with the slow pace of aircraft development, but that is a substantial allegation, and the United Airlines CEO’s argument overlooks both an airplane his own airline ordered and the competition concerns he dismisses when discussing airline mergers.

Scott Kirby Says Boeing And Airbus Are Holding Back New Aircraft Development

I have already discussed Scott Kirby’s appearance on the Cheeky Pint podcast with John Collison and Ben Thompson. The full conversation is worth a listen, even if I disagree with some of his conclusions. As always, Kirby offers a remarkably candid look at how he thinks about the airline business.

One exchange that deserves a closer look concerns Boeing and Airbus, the lack of an all-new Boeing 757 replacement, and whether the aircraft manufacturers have become too comfortable with their position in the market.

Kirby Accuses Boeing And Airbus Of Quietly Agreeing Not To Build New Airframes

In the interview, Kirby argues that the Boeing–Airbus duopoly has weakened the incentive to invest in new aircraft. He says:

“I think the fundamental problem is Boeing and Airbus have a global duopoly, and they have quietly, not officially agreed, but they have quietly agreed: ‘Neither one of us will invest in new airframes.’”

He attributes that reluctance partly to investors and says he has spent more than two decades asking for a 757 replacement. His proposed remedy is another aircraft manufacturer to challenge the incumbents.

First, that is quite a serious accusation. Kirby does not present evidence of an agreement during this exchange. Two companies independently deciding that an expensive development program does not offer an attractive return is different from agreeing to avoid competing. I can accept that limited competition may reduce the urgency to invest. But that does not establish collusion.

What About The Airbus A321XLR?

There is also a rather large omission from this argument: Airbus has developed a functional replacement for the Boeing 757.

The A321neo family fills much of that commercial role, with the A321LR and especially the A321XLR extending it to longer routes. These are not identical aircraft with interchangeable capabilities in every operating condition and I concede that an A321XLR does not solve every requirement an airline might have for replacing both 757s and larger 767s.

But it is difficult to argue that Airbus simply refused to answer the 757 replacement question.

In fact, United announced its order for 50 A321XLRs in December 2019 specifically to begin replacing its Boeing 757-200 fleet. Its own announcement described the aircraft as an “ideal one-for-one replacement” for older aircraft on certain intercontinental routes.

Airbus advertises a range of up to 4,700 nautical miles for the XLR, opening longer routes with single-aisle economics (though it seems the actual range is much shorter…)

An aircraft does not have to be designed from scratch to represent innovation. Improving an existing platform’s efficiency and extending the routes it can economically operate can achieve the same goal. Kirby may want something substantially better–he mentioned the superior efficiency of delta wing design (a triangular wing shape) like the SR-71–I would welcome that too. But wanting a more ambitious airplane does not mean show collusion or that Airbus has not already offered a 757 replacement.

images: Jet Zero

We Were Discussing The Boeing 797 Back In 2019

This debate also brought me back to two stories I wrote seven years ago.

In July 2019, I covered United’s push for clarity on Boeing’s proposed new midmarket airplane, commonly called the 797. I wrote:

“Boeing is still studying the business case for the NMA, but has shifted the bulk of its attention to the troubled 737 MAX program.”

That same story identified the A321XLR as Airbus’ answer to United’s replacement needs.

Two months later, I covered Delta’s interest in the proposed 797, including its substantial need to replace aging 757s and 767s. Boeing’s MAX crisis was already consuming attention and engineering resources. Meanwhile, Airbus was securing XLR orders.

As I put it then:

“But Boeing’s pain has been Airbus’ gain.”

That sounds like competition to me, even if Boeing failed to offer the product its airline customers wanted.

There is ample room to criticize Boeing’s priorities. But a company struggling with its existing aircraft programs may lack the resources or appetite to launch another enormously expensive one. It may also conclude that the potential market does not justify the investment.

Neither explanation requires a quiet agreement with Airbus.

United Airlines CEO Says Boeing And Airbus Need More Competition. What About Airlines?

Finally, one more thought on competition.

Kirby recognizes that when United has fewer suppliers to choose from, those suppliers may feel less pressure to deliver the products United wants. Another competitor could force them to work harder for his business.

I agree with that principle.

But when I apply similar reasoning to his proposed merger with American Airlines, Kirby wants us to focus on the benefits of a larger network and a better airline.

Aircraft manufacturing and airline operations have different economics. Adding a manufacturer is an immense undertaking, and combining airlines can create useful connections. I do not suggest that the two markets are identical.

Nevertheless, the underlying concern is common: customers lose bargaining power when credible alternatives disappear.

If United benefits from having another manufacturer compete for its aircraft orders, passengers can benefit from having American compete with United for their tickets. A larger route map does not eliminate that competitive pressure’s value.

Kirby is persuasive when explaining why he wants more choices as a customer. He is much less persuasive when explaining why we should accept fewer!

That said, I’d love to see Lockheed get back into the commercial airline business…coming soon, an L1011-800!

CONCLUSION

I would welcome a compelling new aircraft that advances what airlines can offer passengers. Kirby is right to push manufacturers to do better. But the A321XLR demonstrates that development has not stopped, and Boeing’s failure to launch the 797 does not establish an agreement to suppress innovation.

More fundamentally, Kirby understands why a customer should worry about too few competitors. I wish he would give that concern equal weight when the customer is buying a United Airlines ticket!

Binance Lost $6.3 Billion to Its Own Signing Machine

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Thursday evening, 29 October, a Binance hot wallet on Tron sent 612.7 million USDT and 46.4 million TRX to an address the exchange didn't control.

It took less than two minutes. Ethereum followed. So did Bitcoin, Solana, Optimism, and five other chains. By the time Binance paused withdrawals that night, an attacker had moved roughly $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in about 20 minutes, according to TRM Labs.

Nothing in crypto's history comes close. The previous record belonged to Bybit for just over 20 months, and the FBI put that theft at $1.5 billion, a little under a quarter of this one.

"It's the biggest hack in history."

Aravind Srinivasan, head of the systematic OTC business at market maker GSR, quoted by RTÉ

The size of the number isn't what kept me reading, though. This line from Binance did: the exchange said it had "found no evidence of any wallet private key leak." Every one of those transfers was validly signed. Bitcoin, Ethereum and Tron did exactly what they were built to do.

So how do you lose $6.3 billion without losing a key?

How an exchange actually holds your coins

A blockchain accepts any transfer that carries a valid signature for the sending address. It has no way to tell whether that signature came from the rightful owner or from someone who has taken over the process that produces it. Hold on to that point; the whole story turns on it.

Large exchanges keep most customer assets in cold storage (offline, and slow to move on purpose) and a smaller float in hot wallets, which sign automatically so your withdrawal clears in seconds. "Smaller" is relative. Binance held about $144 billion in custody before the attack, so this loss works out to roughly 4% of it.

A hot-wallet signer is, by design, an obedient machine. A request comes in, passes a policy check, and gets signed. The key itself sits in a hardware security module (HSM) or a multi-party computation (MPC) setup and never leaves. That keeps the key safe from theft. Whether each incoming request deserves a signature is a separate problem, and a harder one.

Step one: a DevOps engineer's laptop

Binance's first explanation was that the incident "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop," which let the attacker reach an internal service container able to submit withdrawal transactions.

The story grew the next day. Co-CEO Richard Teng said investigators had found additional intrusion points, that the attacker used a self-destruct mechanism to erase tools and traces, and that Binance is now reviewing endpoints, cloud systems, key management and signing processes across the whole company. It still hasn't said how the laptop was compromised.

"It involved a more complex attack process."

Binance co-CEO Richard Teng, in a post on X reported by Forbes

If that sounds familiar, it should. The Bybit theft also started on a developer's machine: attackers got into Safe{Wallet}'s environment and replaced a JavaScript file so that Bybit's signers approved a transaction they couldn't see clearly. Attackers who target crypto firms have spent years luring crypto developers into fake job interviews whose "coding tests" install malware. Nobody has confirmed that's what happened at Binance. It's the obvious first place to look.

Step two: fifty signatures and one wallet

On Ethereum, the attacker used EIP-7702, a feature that went live with the Pectra upgrade on 7 May 2025. It lets an ordinary wallet (an externally owned account, or EOA) point to smart-contract code and run that code as if it were its own. Wallet teams wanted it for batched transactions and sponsored gas fees. Turning it on needs exactly one thing: a signature from the wallet's own key.

At 20:20:59 UTC, the attacker submitted a single transaction carrying 50 EIP-7702 authorizations, all signed by the hot wallet labeled Binance 14. Each authorization is tied to the account's nonce, a counter that goes up by one with every transaction. A busy exchange wallet's nonce moves constantly, so the attacker prepared 50 consecutive ones; whichever matched at the moment of submission would work. One did.

That authorization pointed Binance 14 at a sweeper contract that took orders only from the attacker. Three minutes and 24 seconds later, the wallet sent out 167,464 ETH and 63 tokens, including 1.615 billion USDT. The delegation stayed in place afterwards, so customer deposits that kept landing at the same address were swept six more times over the next three and a half hours.

Two details matter here. “Fifty sequential signatures” means sustained access to the signing path, well beyond one phished approval. And Teng has said the delegation bypassed Binance's usual approval process; the signer produced something its own policy layer never reviewed.

The technique itself wasn't new. A month after Pectra shipped, Wintermute found that more than 80% of EIP-7702 delegations pointed at copy-pasted sweeper contracts that drain wallets with leaked keys. A December 2025 arXiv paper on EIP-7702 phishing showed that one malicious authorization can hand an attacker persistent control of an account. The Binance attacker ran the same playbook at exchange scale, with Binance's own signer doing the signing.

Security researcher Taylor Monahan made the key point back in 2025, when those sweeper delegations first spread:

"It's not actually a 7702 issue."

Taylor Monahan, speaking to The Block about wallets drained through EIP-7702 (June 2025)

The feature only does what a valid signature tells it to. The weak point is whoever controls the signing.

Why "no key leaked" is cold comfort

TRM's reading of the chain data points the same way. It saw ordinary signed transfers leave Binance wallets on several chains, across different key types, within minutes, and concluded that the attacker could make Binance's hot-wallet signing infrastructure authorize transfers of its choosing.

That's why Binance's reassurance doesn't reassure me much. If an attacker can submit requests from inside the trusted zone, or quietly edit the checks those requests pass through, the HSM can stay sealed and still sign away the balance. Binance's statement is very likely true. It answers a different question from the one customers are asking. Fireblocks, which builds custody infrastructure, put the practical rule well: treat any signature produced outside your signing platform as an incident.

The race to freeze the money

The response was fast, at least for some assets. Within about an hour, Tether had frozen 975.1 million USDT and Circle 823 million USDC in the main receiving wallet, roughly $1.8 billion between them; Circle said it acted in coordination with law enforcement. Tether's total later rose to more than 1.567 billion USDT. Binance now puts everything frozen across issuers and partners at around $3 billion.

"In coordination with law enforcement, we have frozen more than 800M USDC."

Circle CEO Jeremy Allaire, posting on X, via Decrypt

The attacker clearly knew the clock was running. TRM traced about 325 million USDT sold through UniswapX and other intent-based contracts for roughly 122,300 ETH. That implies about $2,650 per ETH at a time when ether traded between $2,300 and $2,350; paying a premium of more than 10% is what urgency looks like on-chain. A second wallet deposited 60 million USDT into Aave and borrowed about 19,760 ETH against it before Aave froze the affected markets. By Bitquery's count, the attacker's ether pile has since grown to 490,361 ETH, mostly from converting stablecoins.

There's an awkward lesson in that split. The most centralized assets in crypto, dollar stablecoins with an issuer who can blacklist an address, clawed back close to half of the loss within hours. BTC and ETH have nobody to call. Even a freeze isn't the end of it: TRM's Ari Redbord points out that getting frozen funds back requires a separate legal process.

Who is behind it

Nobody has been officially named. As of 31 October, no government has publicly attributed the attack, Binance hasn't named a suspect, and its root-cause findings aren't out yet. Analytics firms have shared early assessments based on on-chain patterns; until law enforcement or Binance's post-mortem confirms anything, I'm leaving the question open.

What the chain data does show is planning. According to TRM, preparation began at least 36 hours before the theft: the attacker's wallets were funded through the Tornado Cash mixer, and transaction signatures were collected in advance. Whoever did this was patient, and had deep access long before the first coin moved.

What Binance has promised

Binance says it will cover every loss with company money. It has reserved more than $6.34 billion, about $5 billion of it already held in the same tokens that were taken, and is buying the rest on the market and over the counter. Its emergency SAFU fund alone is worth roughly $1.17 billion.

Withdrawals are still paused. Binance's 31 October update targets a phased restart within about 34 hours, subject to security checks, and proof-of-reserves results by Monday, 2 November. A technical post-mortem is promised but has no date.

What has to change

Bybit's breach began on a developer's machine at a supplier; Binance's began on a DevOps engineer's laptop. Any laptop that can reach production deserves the protection production gets: a dedicated, locked-down device, credentials tied to a hardware key, access that expires when the task is done, and a firm rule against running code from strangers (that recruiter's "take-home assignment" included).

Binance's signer trusted whatever its internal services handed it. A second approver, running on separate infrastructure that a hijacked container can't touch, should match every large withdrawal to a real customer request before anything is signed. Fireblocks makes a related point: hot-wallet keys should never be usable from a developer machine at all.

EIP-7702 needs its own rules. A treasury wallet that suddenly delegates to contract code is about as loud a warning as custody gets, and it shows up on-chain for anyone watching. Exchanges should refuse to sign 7702 authorizations unless a human expects one, and a single request for dozens of them, one per upcoming nonce, should page someone immediately.

Then there's containment. One intruder emptied wallets on ten chains in about 20 minutes, which tells me the signing paths shared far too much. Give each chain and wallet tier its own signer, cap how much value can leave per hour, and halt outflows to unfamiliar addresses automatically. Binance paused withdrawals at about 22:15 UTC, roughly two hours after the first transfers at about 20:05 UTC, and the hijacked Ethereum wallet kept sweeping new deposits even after that.

If you hold funds on Binance, three things. Expect a wave of "refund" and "compensation" phishing; Binance will never ask for your 2FA codes or recovery phrase. When the proof-of-reserves report lands, use Binance's verification tool to check your balance is included. And once withdrawals reopen, ask yourself whether long-term holdings belong on any exchange at all.

Four years ago I argued in Regulating Chaos that IoT security would only improve once the law caught up with its weakest devices. Crypto custody has reached the same point, and its weakest device turned out to be a laptop. Monday's reserves report will tell us whether Binance's customers are whole; the post-mortem will tell every other exchange which of its own engineers' desks to check first.

President Donald Trump called Norway a “shithouse country” after failing to win the Nobel Peace Prize. Norway’s tourism officials decided to take that as a compliment.

Visit Norway, the Scandinavian country’s tourism agency, responded to Trump’s insult with a cheeky campaign celebrating its spectacular public restrooms, declaring that Norway makes “shithouses great again.”

The campaign features some of the country’s most architecturally impressive toilets, many situated along scenic routes overlooking fjords, mountains and waterfalls. Some have even won international design awards.

Trump delivered the insult last Sunday during a campaign rally in Port Huron, Michigan, while complaining about the Nobel Peace Prize, which was awarded earlier this month to international jurist Navi Pillay.

“Norway has become a shithouse country, actually,” Trump told supporters. He also suggested he no longer wanted Norwegian immigrants coming to the United States because he had not received the prize.

The Nobel Peace Prize is awarded by an independent Norwegian committee, not the country’s government.

Rather than engage in a diplomatic spat, Visit Norway embraced the president’s characterization, promoting restrooms that include the award-winning Stegastein viewpoint toilet overlooking Aurlandsfjord and the triangular Golden Toilet on Senja Island.

The agency repurposed material originally developed for World Toilet Day, which falls on Nov. 19.

The response has been an unexpected tourism success.

According to travel industry publication Skift, Visit Norway’s toilet webpage attracted more than 80,000 views from over 170 countries in five days, with traffic surging to more than 540 times the previous week’s level. Roughly a quarter of visitors were American.

The tourism agency spent less than $10,000 promoting the campaign, with approximately 90% of its traffic arriving organically. A Times Square advertisement was also planned for this weekend.
