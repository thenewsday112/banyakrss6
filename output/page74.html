Enterprise AI is waiting for its PageRank moment

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let’s try a little thought experiment in technology-fiction: what would have happened if Larry Page and Sergey Brin’s PageRank, back in 1998, had failed to attract its first investors? Google didn’t invent the search engine: there were plenty of them before, from AltaVista, Excite, Lycos or others who were counting words on pages and selling their results page to the highest bidder, to catalog or portal concepts such as Yahoo! or AOL, that attempted to capture the users’ attention as much as they could to be able to serve them advertising, the more the better. Imagine if these concepts would have gone the distance, and deprived us of the web that resulted from Google’s easy and efficient way of searching … 

What did Larry Page and Sergey Brin realize? They adapted and extended the citation index (or its consequence, the citation impact), a system that they knew worked for academic literature (remember, these guys were graduate students working on their Ph.D., and thus reading academic papers like there was no tomorrow!), and moved it into a new context: the web. They realized there was an underused signal, relationships among pages, and used it to generate a metric, weighting links according to the importance of the pages providing them, that not only allowed them to index the web, but also to define attention as a social construct: a page is important when other pages link to it with a certain term. 

What’s today’s underused signal in corporate AI? 

As I’ve said several times, corporate AI is completely different from individual AI: companies don’t need just answers to questions, what they need is persistent memory and state, feedback learning loops, embedded cybersecurity, logs that can be tracked and not manipulated, etc. 

So far, companies are building these required scaffolding using twentieth-century tools such as Python, and AI agents are proving to be smart enough as to escape from these containments and do all sort of bad things — not because they are intrinsically evil (they cannot be), but because the goals and the guardrails are badly defined, or due to excessive permissions, prompt injection, or weak isolation. These companies are trying to define these properties by repeatedly assembling application by application, rather than supplied coherently by the platform. 

However, companies are repeatedly making all sorts of decisions about suppliers, customers, inventory, pricing, service, etc. Those decisions are constantly generating evidence of what works and what not, provided that the company is smart enough to capture them. What if we could evaluate AI by whether the company learns and improves, beyond the quality of individual outputs? What if AI optimization becomes more important than having access to bigger models? 

The competition moves from model capabilities to system performance 

A company does not just need a very powerful AI model: it needs an architecture that coordinates capabilities toward its goals. Researchers writing on Berkeley’s AI research blog explicitly describe the shift toward compound AI systems, where system design can offer better returns than further model scaling. 

Other experiments, such as FrugalGPT or DSPy are specific precedents for making model selection subordinate to results: FrugalGPT demonstrated that model cascades can match the strongest individual model with up to 98% lower cost on its evaluated tasks, and DSPy showed that optimized pipelines using smaller models can compete with approaches using much larger proprietary models. 

What makes the next step consequential? The ability to learn across executions. If we manage to make each interaction inform subsequent decisions across the relevant population, we can achieve a substantial advantage. And world models add precisely this possibility: they can anticipate consequences before acting. Research about Dreamer successfully demonstrates this mechanism across diverse control tasks. And once you have that, you can use reinforcement learning or another optimization mechanism uses experience to progressively improve decisions. 

Accumulated organizational learning becomes the true competitive advantage 

While broadly available models available through commercial APIs or open-weight deployments can provide shared capabilities, company-specific experience can produce truly differentiated decisions, the ones that can really make the difference. Imagine, for instance, the renewal decisions you can make when you can learn from observed retention outcomes. 

However, bear in mind that simple accumulation does not automatically guarantee an advantage: you have to process the information properly, but not “with an AI”… with “your AI”. Make sure you put the training in the correct layer, the one you can administer and control. 

PageRank unlocked value in something the web was already producing: links. The next breakthrough in corporate AI could unlock value in something companies already produce every day: experience. If that experience becomes accumulated learning that the company controls and retains as models change, the competitive advantage starts to belong to the business itself. The company that stopped waiting for a smarter model might simply be the first to realize that its most valuable intelligence is the intelligence it learns to build — and keep. 

My proposed buying criterion is based on the following question: can the company replace its model provider while retaining its operational history, learned policies, objectives and demonstrated gains? Can your company export and reuse that learning when changing platform providers? Do you own your corporate intelligence, or do you rent the whole thing? When you stop working with “vanilla AI” and you start working with “your own AI”, you control the learning asset, without requiring ownership of every underlying model. 

And that’s when you can become a truly optimizable company. Basically, optimization, not a bigger model, is what you need. 

How Obama appointee and Trump nemesis Boasberg ended up overseeing Ilhan Omar warrant, other key cases

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Federal Judge James Boasberg, dubbed a "Radical left lunatic" by President Donald Trump, is presiding over several high-profile cases challenging actions made by the Trump administration, raising questions about how judges are assigned to cases.

The scrutiny comes as Boasberg, an Obama-appointed chief judge of the Washington, D.C., district court, oversees a challenge brought by several media outlets seeking to force the Justice Department to unseal the search warrant on the home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn.

"Judge James Boasberg is now involved with the Ilhan Omar case," Steve Ferguson, a self-described business owner with more than 250,000 followers on X, wrote in a social media post. "Is everything rigged?"

In the federal court of the District of Columbia, cases are assigned to judges randomly by an electronic system managed by the clerk's office operating under the direction of the Calendar and Case Management Committee, according to the rules of the court. The committee consists of three to five judges appointed by the chief justice.

There are exceptions to the random-assignment process. For example, criminal grand jury matters are assigned to and overseen by the chief judge, currently Boasberg. This means Boasberg’s assignment to oversee the fight over unsealing the search warrant for Omar was "not a surprise" to Eric Wessan, Iowa’s solicitor general.

"But there are a lot of cases that seem to be going to judges maybe based on those cases having similarities, which is not what the local rules say," Wessan told Fox News Digital.

When a case needs to be reassigned, the Calendar and Case Management Committee will determine how the reassignment is made.

Wessan suggested that concerns over how case assignments are made are valid, considering some of the high-profile cases against the Trump administration which have appeared in Boasberg's docket.

"If one is just following anecdotally, there does seem to be a lot of high-profile cases in front of judges that seem to be expressing unusual amounts of frustration or disagreement with the administration," Wessan said, urging greater transparency in how case assignments are made.

TRUMP’S FIGHT OVER RARELY USED 18THCENTURY DEPORTATION LAW LIVES ON IN LATEST COURT CLASH

The fight over unsealing the release of Omar's search warrant is just one of several cases involving the Trump administration and now on Boasberg's docket. Boasberg is also overseeing California’s lawsuit against the Trump administration over voter roll disputes. 

On Friday, he ordered the administration to provide the state with the names and supporting information for roughly 20,000 people whom the DOJ claims are noncitizens on California’s voter rolls by the end of the day.

Boasberg has issued several consequential rulings against the Trump administration, including a high-profile March 2025 order directing the administration to turn around deportation flights carrying alleged Venezuelan gang members to El Salvador. The Trump administration fought against the order, pointing to the Alien Enemies Act as legal justification for the deportations. 

The dispute over the administration’s use of the Alien Enemies Act eventually reached the Supreme Court, which vacated Boasberg's temporary restraining order blocking deportation flights, ruling that the case was brought in the wrong jurisdiction.

That same month, Boasberg was assigned a lawsuit seeking to preserve Signal messages after top Trump administration officials discussed plans for a Yemen strike in a group chat that accidentally included Atlantic editor-in-chief Jeffrey Goldberg. The judge later ordered the administration to preserve the messages.

The string of cases prompted Reps. Jim Jordan, R-Ohio; Darrell Issa, R-Calif.; and Chip Roy, R-Texas, to send a letter in May 2025 to the court clerk asking for greater clarity on how judicial assignments are made. The lawmakers questioned whether assignments were truly random as the court’s rules state.

"The Committee cannot independently verify the randomness of case assignments because the composition of assignment decks is kept secret," the lawmakers wrote.

"We must understand the nature of the case assignment process in the District Court — which is considering a number of cases challenging the Trump Administration’s policy decisions — to consider and develop appropriate legislation."

Meanwhile, Sen. Eric Schmitt, R-Mo., introduced legislation in July that would require federal district courts to assign cases randomly, without exceptions, naming it the Boasberg Act. The bill is stalled in committee.

"Judge Boasberg is the poster child for a rogue judge and exposed serious loopholes in our judicial system. Randomly assigning cases among judges ensures the American people can have confidence in the impartiality of our courts," Schmitt said in a statement.

Under the rules of the D.C. Court, the chief justice does not have the power to reassign a case. The Calendar and Case Management Committee handles case reassignments. The court must notify the judges and attorneys involved, but it does not have to publicly explain why a case was reassigned. Wessan has called for greater transparency.

"The rules shouldn’t say one thing if it’s not true. But if it is true, they could just say so. I think the issue is with the exceptions. What happens in those exceptional cases and how often do they occur?" Wessan said.

"Given the frequency of the concerning episodes in the district court, erring on the side of transparency is probably prudent to the extent that there are exceptions to the normal random-assignment system."

South African Airways Can’t Pay Staff On Time, But Says It Doesn’t Need Another Bailout

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

South African Airways is delaying salary payments for some employees for the second consecutive month, while insisting it has a viable strategy and will not ask for another government bailout. When an airline has to choose between paying its operating bills and paying its pilots on time, it’s difficult to take those assurances seriously.

South African Airways Delays Salaries As Cash Pressure Mounts

View From The Wing highlights that South African Airways is again confronting questions about whether its recovery is financially sustainable. The immediate problem is payroll. SAA has acknowledged staggering salaries in September and October, prioritizing critical operating expenses while moving some employees to later payment dates.

Employees have purportedly been paid according to the following schedule:

  • Ground staff and cabin crew: October 27
  • Pilots: October 28
  • Management and specialists: October 30
  • Executives: October 31

SAA says “most” employees were scheduled to receive their salaries on the normal payday of October 27, but it appears that 30-40% of employees were paid late.

SAA Says It Will Not Ask For More Money

SAa’s corporate affairs executive Mphilo Dlamini attributed the pressure to rising fuel costs and acknowledged that the airline had insufficient capital after emerging from business rescue. He said financial institutions were interested in supporting SAA and insisted,“We are not going to ask for more money.”

“After the business rescue process, we did not get enough capital injection that is going to keep us afloat, cushion our costs, make sure that we expand the way we wanted to expand. So, there are those issues that I want to admit made us, you know, to be affected in terms of liquidity.

“The board and the management are seized with making sure that we are actually the support that we got from government through the guarantee. We are talking and engaging a number of our financial institutions, and I must say and confirm to you now that they are willing and interested in supporting us because we have a bankable strategy. We are not going to ask for more money.”

That assurance comes after the government approved a R1 billion (about 60 million USD) guarantee in August to support working capital.

A guarantee does not hand case to SAA, but helps the carrier obtain financing by putting the government behind the borrowing. But it still exposes taxpayers if the airline cannot repay.

The Reported Profit Deserves A Closer Look

As noted by One Mile At A Time, SAA’s annual report for the year ended March 31, 2025 shows a R155 million group net profit. The airline company itself reported R30 million. But the group also reported a R317 million loss before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization. Its income statement included R1.169 billion in net disposal and lease-modification gains, helping turn the final result positive.

Subtract that gains line from the reported R173 million pretax profit and the result is a R996 million pretax loss. The report also contains a disclaimer of opinion from the auditor-general, who could not obtain sufficient evidence to express an audit opinion on the financial statements.

Selling valuable assets like Heathrow slots can provide breathing room, but it cannot indefinitely substitute for generating enough cash from the business to cover its bills.

Another Rescue Is Not A Recovery Strategy

The government guarantee comes with conditions, including a February deadline to find buyers and a requirement to identify assets for sale. SAA has also denied a report that its board considered returning to business rescue. Still, the fundamental question remains: what makes this business sustainable once the next financing arrangement is exhausted, especially if fuel prices keep going up?

Whatever the cause, it’s sad that SAA has repeatedly asked employees to accommodate its cash shortages. Staff members have their own payment deadlines…it’s a vicious cycle.

CONCLUSION

South African Airways continues to operate, and delayed salaries don’t necessarily suggest that a shutdown is imminent. But repeated payroll delays, dependence on government-backed borrowing, and troubling financial disclosures leave ample reason for skepticism. I would welcome a genuinely sustainable SAA, but that does not appear to be the current state of affairs.

A Comeback? No, Dane Tudor Is in His "Fourth Prime"

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Somewhere in Colorado, hundreds of miles away, Dane Tudor held an umbrella aloft and peered down at me.

He strolled about and patiently answered my many questions, a gray sky creeping into the edge of the frame behind him. I’d signed in to the video call to talk about his new movie, The Fourth Prime, but first I wanted to figure out where he’d been the past few seasons. The answer? Still skiing.

Now 37, the Rossland, British Columbia, and Palmer, Alaska, raised skier lived through and embodied a rapid evolution in snow sports. Skis got fat, twin tips went big, and techy tricks started showing up everywhere. Tudor did it all. 

People continually refer back to his 2009 Everyday Is a Saturday segment with Poor Boyz Productions, a stunning mishmash of everything that was—and still is—sick in freeskiing. To kids of a certain generation, Tudor was one of those figures: above reproach in the style department yet more than handy on skis when he wasn’t flying through the air.

Pillows and flips. Two of Dane’s specialties.

Koover Schmidt

After winning the iF3 Standout Male Skier of the Year award in 2022, though, Tudor parted ways with his sponsor, Scott, and went, for a time, without brand support. He last appeared in a ski movie for Blank Collective’s Feel Real, which, confoundingly, also earned him the iF3 award. You’d think that would be worth a contract or three. 

But it isn’t uncommon for gifted skiers to fade in and out of the film scene. There are only so many brand dollars to go around, talent keeps skyrocketing, and social media has completely changed the ski movie- and magazine-driven environment Tudor came up in. During our chat, he wondered aloud if not knowing how to crack the algorithm held him back. 

While he’d grown tired of pitching sponsors, the same wasn’t true of skiing. Tudor kept ripping around his home mountain, Red, and, though he didn’t want the “break” from being a professional, he found inspiration in the downtime.

“I was able to really think about my philosophy in skiing and work on the next phase of my riding style,” Tudor said, his umbrella hovering in the background. “Rethinking everything from how I ride to what is challenging and what is pushing the limits or boundaries, and how to balance all those things while working on mastering the craft into the future.”

Last winter wasn’t great, apparently?

Koover Schmidt

The Fourth Prime is part of that evolution. With help from the athlete management crew Pangea Collective, Tudor recently signed two new sponsors, Fischer Skis and the outerwear company Nivis. That unlocked the budget he needed, and last winter Tudor shot with longtime ski filmmaker Jeff Thomas. Fellow skier and up-and-comer Jonathan Rollins coined the film’s name. While building a jump together last winter, Rollins kept saying Tudor was now in his “fourth prime.” It stuck.

“The project means a lot because I’m still in the game and I still get to enjoy the backcountry and ride with friends and practice my style and create content,” Tudor said. “It’s just that creative process that’s so fulfilling.”

Given the duration of Tudor’s career—he blew up at 19—you could probably slice it into any number of “primes.” He’s filmed street, pillow lines, and big mountain, plus covered most of the snow in between. Tudor admitted that he didn’t know what Rollins’ breakdown of the “primes” was. His assessment, though, was in part guided by the ski movie production outfits he worked with, starting with Poor Boyz, bouncing between Teton Gravity Research and Sherpas Cinema, and ending with Blank. Well, not quite.

“Now this is the fourth prime, which is pretty cool,” Tudor said.

The trailer is packed with his usual wizardry: dubs, hefty backcountry jumps, and capped blunt grabs. It might come as a surprise, then, that when I asked Tudor about his highlights from filming The Fourth Prime, he nodded to a jibbier, lower-impact trick: the humble but flexible butter. 

One flavor of the move appears early in The Fourth Prime when he locks into an extended butter 360. Perfecting it is, in a way, emblematic of the direction he wants to go. A fat backflip is mind-blowing, sure. But a well-executed butter—no small feat, mind you—can also get the neurons firing. 

“That’s how I’ve been working on progressing my skiing: how can I do things differently than everybody else and make it look cool while not taking massive risk?” he said.

Playing it smart and getting inverted aren’t mutually exclusive.

Koover Schmidt

For Tudor, it slots into a broader approach: squeezing the most out of his environment. Or, turning less into more. Last winter royally sucked for much of the West. That impacted Tudor, who regularly dealt with rain and the crusty layers it brought while filming The Fourth Prime. In the past, understandably, Tudor always wanted the perfect conditions. But he said he’s gotten better at tailoring his skiing to what the mountains provide.

Choosing those features wisely and not taking chances, along with the usual maintenance hacks—eating well, going to the gym—is Tudor’s strategy for staying healthy. He’s had only one season-ending injury: a hip dislocation in 2013. Tudor thinks that’s a product of his careful approach, though he also cited another reason he stays away from the burliest stunts: nerves.

“I have a certain level of conservatism in my writing. I’m not a total wild guy,” he said. “I do battle with more internal fear than maybe some other guys do … I think that’s part of what’s helped me along the way.”

Fear for Tudor and fear for the average skier look a little different. In the spring, Tudor ventured to Blackcomb’s imposing terrain park alongside Kye Petersen. He wanted to revitalize his freestyle skills, getting them close to what they were in 2012, when Tudor’s time spent training in the park coalesced. That involved going upside down twice. Then, on one air, he slipped up, missing his pop, landing sideways, and twisting his ankle pretty good. He offered the moment as an example. It showed that even when you’re careful, mistakes can happen. 

As the years pass, the size and frequency of those mistakes matter. Outside of skiing, Tudor works for the bike park company Gravity Logic, but he’s also into trading, buying and selling stocks. In trading, there’s a metric called “win rate,” which measures the percentage of someone’s trades that result in a profit. Tudor’s found that the concept applies to skiing.

“You can take many small hits and stay in the game,” he said, drawing the comparison. “But if you go massive and you blow up a couple times big, that could be the end of it.”

As of this fall, it’s fair to say Tudor’s in the green, with the latest video—his first in years—premiering across North America on Pangea’s Freeride Film Tour. He doesn’t take this second round in front of the lens for granted. Whenever he gets the chance to shoot, he said, he gives it everything because he doesn’t know if it’ll happen again—the ski industry remains fickle, after all, perhaps more than ever. 

Still, Tudor spoke excitedly about the momentum around The Fourth Prime, and is hatching plans with filmmakers to make another movie happen this season. He’s even considering, if only in a distant way, getting back into the streets. However the future looks, Tudor’s having fun. That isn’t contingent on the ski career thing.

“I ski the way I do because I enjoy that,” he said, adding that he’ll continue venturing into the mountains regardless. “I grew up as a passionate skier—it’s what I love to do.”

On Thursday night, the smear at the heart of the mysterious transgender mailers that have rocked the final weeks of the midterm elections in Texas completed its journey. What began as baseless social media whispers quickly ascended the ladder of conservative influence, jumped into mailboxes across the Lone Star State and ultimately into the comforting prime-time embrace of Fox News.

At a town hall in Fort Worth, a Texas mother of six named Rebekah Smith told Vice President JD Vance that her 11-year-old daughter had received a letter explaining how to transition without her parents ever knowing, and asked how Republicans would keep the government out of her family. “I don’t know who sent that letter to your child,” the vice president said, “but I think the person who sends a letter to an 11-year-old about gender transition, what I call that in my family is a pervert, and that person ought to go to prison.” Host Jesse Watters, never one to let a grievance go to waste, promised that Fox would investigate “this perverted letter.” Vance asked Smith to talk to his staff because he would “like the Department of Justice to investigate that” as well.

Watters rushed to promote the spectacle. “WHOA! Texas MOM tells JD Vance her DAUGHTER received a letter telling her TO TRANSITION TO A BOY,” he posted on X, followed by Vance’s promise. Missing from that framing was the question that should have governed the segment: Who actually sent the material?

The race between Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton and Democratic challenger James Talarico has become a crucible for dark-money maneuvers. That is how a smear works in the closed ecosystem of the American right. It does not need to survive scrutiny. It just needs to circulate upward. An anonymous account posts it; an influencer adopts it; a donor-funded outlet reports the influencer’s outrage as news; an elected official demands an investigation, and the demand becomes proof the story matters; cable news books the controversy; a vice president promises federal action. Each rung launders the one below it. The correction, when it comes, travels in the opposite direction and arrives nowhere.
